# Making the machine do things when you are not there: cron, at and systemd timers

## Define the problem

A great deal of system administration is work that nobody should have to remember: rotate the logs at night, back up the database every weekday, renew a certificate before it expires, apply security updates on Sunday, run a one-off report at the end of the month, retry a failed transfer in an hour. These jobs have to start at the right time without a person logged in, run with a known environment and the right privileges, report failure in a way someone will notice, and not pile up on top of each other if one run takes too long.

Scheduling raises a cluster of design questions. How is time expressed, by a wall-clock pattern or by an interval since something else? What should happen if the machine was off at the scheduled moment: skip the run, or catch up? In which environment does the job run, since a scheduler's shell is not an administrator's shell? How does the output go somewhere useful? How do you stop two instances overlapping, and how do you let some users schedule jobs while forbidding others? The tools in this topic answer those questions in three different ways, and understanding the differences is what lets an administrator pick the right one.

## History and approaches

The classic answer is cron, a daemon that wakes every minute, reads its table and starts any job whose time pattern matches. Its table format is a single line per job with five time fields followed by the command: the minute, the hour, the date within the month, the month number and the weekday. The design is tiny and has hardly changed in decades, which is both its strength and its weakness. It is trivially understood and available everywhere, but it knows only the clock: a job scheduled for 02:30 while the machine is powered off is simply skipped, and a job that takes longer than its interval overlaps with the next run. The environment is minimal by design, and the output is mailed to the owner unless redirected, a feature that surprises everyone the first time a machine has no mail system [src: man-pages 8a908ae96b1f5f15] [src: debian-handbook e6e85889272653e1].

Two companions fill gaps. The at facility runs a job exactly once at a given time, reading commands from standard input, with a daemon (atd) holding the queue; batch is a variant that waits until the load is low. And for machines that are not always on, anacron records when each periodic job last ran and runs overdue ones after boot, with a delay, which is why laptops use it for daily, weekly and monthly maintenance; the periodic directories such as cron.daily are typically driven this way [src: debian-handbook 0579d255dc5cadf7].

The systemd project introduced timer units as a different model: a timer unit does nothing but activate another unit, usually a service of the same name, when its conditions are met. A timer can be calendar-based, like cron, or monotonic, meaning relative to boot, to activation or to the last run of the job. Because the job is an ordinary service, it inherits everything services have: a defined environment, resource limits, sandboxing, dependency ordering, logging in the journal under its own unit, and a status that shows the last result. Timers can also remember missed runs, add random delay to spread load, and wake a suspended machine [src: archwiki 0297aab5c576cb68] [src: man-pages aad3896ad1270375].

The partial successes and failures are instructive. Cron's simplicity made it the default for fifty years, but its silent failure modes (skipped runs, discarded output, wrong environment) cost many outages. Anacron fixed the missed-run problem for periodic jobs but not for jobs at a specific time. Timers fixed most of these but are more verbose: two files for one job, where cron needs one line. Distributions now ship both, and the same maintenance task may be a cron entry on one family and a timer on another [src: rhel-10-docs 4bd0e3577bf08bc6] [src: compliance-as-code b2516459743deaf4].

## Current state

Cron's table is read from several places. Each user has a personal table edited with crontab -e, listed with -l, removed with -r and, for the superuser, selected with -u user; the files live under /var/spool/cron. The system table /etc/crontab and the files in /etc/cron.d use a sixth field after the time fields that names the user to run as, and a personal table does not. Four periodic directories under /etc, named cron.hourly, cron.daily, cron.weekly and cron.monthly, hold plain executable scripts that a helper such as run-parts runs in sequence, whose file names must avoid dots on some systems or the files are skipped [src: debian-handbook e6e85889272653e1].

The five time fields accept an asterisk for any value, a single number, a comma-separated list, a range with a hyphen, and a step with a slash (for example */15 in the minute field). Day of week is 0 to 7 with both 0 and 7 meaning Sunday, and names may be used for months and weekdays. When both day of month and day of week are restricted, most implementations run the job when either matches, an unintuitive rule. Special strings replace the five fields: @reboot, @hourly, @daily, @weekly, @monthly and @yearly. In the command, an unescaped percent sign is converted to a newline and the text after the first one is passed as standard input, so a date format such as +%F must be written with a backslash before the percent signs [src: man-pages 434d8916e7202e52].

The environment is deliberately sparse. Cron sets SHELL to /bin/sh, LOGNAME and HOME from the owner's account line, and a very short PATH; a table may assign its own SHELL, PATH, HOME and MAILTO at the top, and the file's comments must be on their own lines, not after a command. If MAILTO is set to an address, output is mailed there; if set but empty, no mail is sent; if unset, the owner receives it. A job that works at a prompt and fails under cron is almost always a PATH or environment difference, which is why scripts run by cron should use absolute paths and set what they need [src: man-pages 434d8916e7202e52]. Access is controlled by /etc/cron.allow and /etc/cron.deny: if the allow file exists only listed users may use crontab, otherwise users in the deny file are refused, and compliance baselines ask that both cron and at be restricted to authorised users [src: compliance-as-code 9281d269b87d2b50]. The daemon logs each job start to the syslog or journal, which is the first place to look when a job did not run.

The at command takes a time specification such as "now + 5 minutes", "17:00 tomorrow" or "teatime", reads the commands from standard input or a file given with -f, and queues them; atq lists the queue, atrm removes a job and batch queues one to run when the load permits. The job runs with the environment and working directory of the moment it was submitted. Access is controlled by /etc/at.allow and /etc/at.deny in the same way as cron.

A systemd timer is a unit file with a [Timer] section plus, usually, a matching service unit. The calendar form is OnCalendar=, whose syntax is a weekday list, a date part and a time part: Mon..Fri *-*-* 02:30:00 means 02:30 on weekdays. Components can be wildcards, lists, ranges, and repetitions with a slash; a tilde means counting from the end of the month, so *-02~03 is the third-last day of February; omitted time or date parts mean midnight or every day; a time zone can be given as UTC; and shortcuts such as daily, weekly, monthly and hourly exist. The command systemd-analyze calendar expression normalises an expression and prints the next elapse time, and it accepts several, which makes it the best way to test [src: man-pages f8e1d561b2279cfc]. The monotonic options are OnActiveSec= (relative to the timer's own activation), OnBootSec= (since boot), OnStartupSec= (since the manager started), OnUnitActiveSec= and OnUnitInactiveSec= (since the target unit last started or stopped); several can be combined in one unit, and combining OnBootSec with OnUnitActiveSec makes a repeating interval [src: man-pages 2ab09560d78bd0a8].

Other timer keys complete the picture. Persistent=true records the last trigger on disk and runs the job at once after boot if a calendar trigger was missed. RandomizedDelaySec= adds a random delay up to a bound, to prevent a fleet from hitting one server at the same second. AccuracySec= (default one minute) lets the manager coalesce wake-ups, so a job may start up to that late. WakeSystem=true wakes a suspended machine. Unit= names the unit to activate when it does not match the timer's name. The [Install] section normally has WantedBy=timers.target, and the timer, not the service, is the thing that is enabled: systemctl enable --now backup.timer. The command systemctl list-timers shows each timer, when it last ran and when it runs next, and the activated service's output goes to the journal, readable with journalctl -u backup.service. A service run by a timer should usually be Type=oneshot, and overlapping runs are avoided because a service already running is not started again [src: archwiki 665071acb6f9ff21] [src: archwiki 317ca9985dd40083].

The same machinery is used by the system itself: package managers ship timers for automatic updates, discard of unused blocks, log rotation, integrity checks and certificate renewal, and the compliance guides tell administrators to enable specific timers rather than write their own cron lines [src: rhel-10-docs 7dcdeadd85f6dfce] [src: compliance-as-code 5b322ca9e29ec7e0] [src: rocky-docs 23a35934f9d4dd56].

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

The good is the range. Cron is universal and one line is enough for a simple task; at covers the one-off case without inventing a unit; timers give scheduling the full strength of the service manager, with accurate logs, resource limits, catch-up and a way to see what is scheduled. The three coexist, so the right tool can be chosen per job.

The bad is that each has traps that the others avoid. Cron skips runs when the machine is down, has no overlap protection, mails output silently, and runs in a thin environment. Timers need two files, rely on a service being correctly written, and have an accuracy window that surprises people who expect second-level precision. Calendar syntax differs between the two systems: cron counts days of the week from 0 and applies an either-or rule, timers use names and an and rule. Time zones and daylight saving changes make local-time schedules ambiguous: a job at 02:30 may run twice or not at all on a change night, which is why critical jobs are often scheduled in UTC.

The ugly is the silent failure and the security exposure. A cron job that fails because of a changed PATH may do nothing for months with output going to a mailbox no one reads. A timer whose service was never enabled looks configured but never fires. A scheduled script writable by an ordinary user is a privilege escalation, since it runs as its owner or as root. Jobs scheduled by departed staff outlive them in forgotten per-user tables. Overlapping runs of a backup or a database dump can corrupt output. And a job that starts on every host at the same minute can overwhelm shared storage or a package server.

## Applications beyond this domain

Scheduled work exists wherever there is infrastructure. Container platforms provide a cron-like object that creates a pod on a schedule, with fields for concurrency policy and history, which is the same design with the overlap question made explicit. Cloud providers offer event schedulers that invoke functions with cron-style expressions. Databases and storage systems have internal job schedulers, build systems run nightly pipelines, and monitoring systems use the same time patterns for maintenance windows.

The ideas travel too: idempotent jobs that are safe to run twice, locks to prevent overlap, output captured to a log with a unique identifier, jitter to spread load, catch-up semantics for missed runs, and alerting on absence of success rather than on presence of failure. Even software updates on image-based systems use timers to check for and stage new images [src: rhel-10-docs afb67da4fed024ab].

## Examples

A first exercise writes the same job twice. Take a script that prints a message to a file, schedule it at 02:30 on weekdays as a system cron entry with an explicit user and as a timer and service pair, and verify the timer with systemd-analyze calendar and systemd-analyze verify. This is the lab for the topic.

A second exercise explores catch-up. Schedule a job a few minutes in the future in both systems, stop the machine or the daemon across that time, restart, and see that cron skipped it while a timer with Persistent=true ran it. Then add RandomizedDelaySec and watch the start time move.

A third exercise is the environment trap: write a cron job that calls a command found only in a directory not on cron's default path, watch it fail, find the failure in the log, and fix it with an absolute path and a PATH line at the top of the table. Use a percent sign in a date format and see how cron treats it.

A fourth exercise is one-off scheduling with at: queue a command for a few minutes ahead, list it with atq, remove another with atrm, and compare with systemd-run --on-active, which creates a transient timer without files.

A failing example is a timer that never runs because only the service was enabled; list-timers shows no next elapse. Another is a job that takes longer than its interval and overlaps under cron. A third is daylight saving: schedule at 02:30 on a test machine with its clock set to a change night.

## Related problems and ideas

Neighbouring topics are log rotation, which is the commonest scheduled job; time synchronisation, on which every schedule depends; resource control, which limits what a scheduled service may consume; and monitoring, which should alert when expected runs do not happen. Related ideas include distributed schedulers that elect one runner across many hosts, job queues that decouple the trigger from the work, and workflow engines that chain scheduled steps with dependencies and retries.

Ideas for practice: prefer a timer for anything that needs logs, limits, ordering or catch-up, and cron for trivial one-liners; set MAILTO or redirect output deliberately; use absolute paths; run as the least privileged user; lock against overlap with flock or by relying on the service manager; schedule in UTC where the exact moment matters; add a randomised delay on fleets; test calendar expressions before enabling; and alert on missed success rather than waiting for a failure message.

## Active research

Work in this area is mostly engineering at scale. Fleet schedulers explore jitter and coordination so that thousands of hosts do not synchronise, and cloud schedulers explore exactly-once and at-least-once delivery guarantees for scheduled triggers. In the single-host world, effort goes into making the service manager's timers power-aware, with wake-up coalescing to save energy, and into richer calendar syntax. Dead-man-switch monitoring, where a job pings a service on success and the absence of a ping raises the alert, has become a common pattern, and research on log and metric anomaly detection is applied to scheduled workloads.

## Future direction

Expect cron to remain as a compatibility layer and a convenient one-liner, with new maintenance tasks shipped as timers by the distributions. Scheduling will be increasingly declarative and observable, with last-run, next-run and result visible in one place, and with catch-up and jitter as defaults. In cloud and cluster settings the equivalent objects will replace per-host tables. The habits that matter most do not change with the tool: make jobs safe to repeat, give them a defined environment, record their output, and watch for the absence of success.
