# Agreeing on the time: clocks, network time protocol clients and the time and zone tools

## Define the problem

Almost everything a computer does that involves more than one machine, or more than one moment, depends on clocks being right. Log lines from different hosts only interleave sensibly if their timestamps agree. Certificates are valid between two instants, so a clock a day wrong makes valid certificates look expired or not yet valid. Kerberos tickets are rejected if the clocks differ by more than a few minutes. Database replication, distributed locks, token expiry, scheduled jobs, backups and forensic timelines all assume that a timestamp means roughly the same thing everywhere.

The trouble is that no physical clock is right. The crystal in a computer drifts by tens of parts per million, which is a second or more per day, and drifts differently as temperature changes. The hardware clock that keeps time while the machine is off is no better. A virtual machine can be paused and resumed and wake with a clock hours behind. Meanwhile, human-facing time is not a single thing: people see local time with zones and daylight saving rules, while the machine should keep a uniform scale underneath. The topic therefore has three layers: keeping the system clock accurate by comparing it with better clocks over a network, representing the time zone the people want to see, and controlling both with a few administrative commands.

## History and approaches

The first widely used answer was the Network Time Protocol, designed in the early 1980s, which organises time servers in layers called strata. A stratum-zero device is a reference clock such as a satellite receiver or atomic clock, a stratum-one server is directly attached to one, and every further hop adds one to the number. A client polls several servers, filters the delay and offset measurements, discards sources that disagree with the majority and disciplines its clock gently toward the survivors. The reference implementation, the ntp daemon, did this for decades and became deeply entrenched, and its design reflects an era of servers that ran continuously on stable links.

That design fit less well later. Laptops sleep, virtual machines are paused, and networks come and go, so a daemon that assumes continuous operation takes a long time to converge after each interruption. A newer implementation, chrony, was written for these conditions. It converges quickly, copes with intermittent connectivity and large initial offsets, tracks the drift rate of the local oscillator, and can step the clock when it is far off, which the older daemon was reluctant to do. Most Red Hat-family distributions made it the default client, and many others offer it [src: rhel-10-docs abee4c68710434f0].

A lighter alternative also exists. The simple network time protocol client built into the systemd family, systemd-timesyncd, implements only the client side with no filtering beyond the basics, which is enough for desktops and many cloud instances where a few tens of milliseconds of error is acceptable; its manual page notes that the timedatectl set-ntp command enables and starts it [src: man-pages e99773e52d98c781] [src: archwiki 9a2173b7eb1a227a]. For high-precision needs, the precision time protocol, with hardware timestamping in network cards, achieves sub-microsecond agreement inside a data centre.

The failures that shaped practice are well known. Open servers were abused in amplification attacks, which led to restricted default configurations. Leap seconds, inserted occasionally to keep civil time aligned with the earth's rotation, caused outages when software handled the extra second badly; this led to the practice of smearing the extra second across hours, and the international decision to stop adding them in the future. And the question of what the hardware clock stores, universal time or local time, produced years of confusion on machines that dual-boot with a system that assumes the other.

## Current state

The system has three clocks to keep straight. The system clock is software, counted by the kernel in universal time since the epoch and corrected by the time daemon through slewing (running a little fast or slow) or stepping (jumping). The hardware clock, also called the real-time clock or RTC, runs on a battery while the machine is off and is read at boot; it should be kept in universal time. And the time zone is a presentation setting: a file under /usr/share/zoneinfo selected by a symbolic link at /etc/localtime. Everything displayed to people is converted from the universal count using that link.

The command timedatectl is the front end for all of this. Without arguments it prints status: local time, universal time, RTC time, the time zone, whether the system clock is synchronised, whether a network time service is active, and whether the RTC is in local time. Its commands include status, show (the same data in machine-readable form, optionally restricted with --property=), set-time (set the system clock and the RTC, with a timestamp such as "2026-11-14 18:17:16"), set-timezone (change the /etc/localtime link, taking a name from list-timezones such as Europe/Berlin), list-timezones, set-local-rtc (keep the hardware clock in local time, which the page discourages because it breaks across daylight saving changes), and set-ntp (enable or disable network synchronisation, by starting the first available synchronisation service and stopping the known others) [src: man-pages 535540cdf94478b4] [src: man-pages c6aa09a129775b7f]. Setting the time by hand while a daemon is active is refused, because the two would fight, and the usual advice is to leave the clock to the daemon and fix the configuration instead. The companion commands hwclock (read or write the RTC, with --systohc to copy the system time to the hardware and --hctosys the other way) and date remain useful for scripts and recovery.

The chrony client is a daemon, chronyd, configured in /etc/chrony.conf (on Debian-family systems /etc/chrony/chrony.conf, with extra files under a conf.d directory), and controlled by the command chronyc. The directives that matter most are these. The server directive names a single time source and pool names a DNS name that resolves to several servers; both usually take the iburst option, which sends a quick burst of requests at start-up so the first synchronisation happens in seconds. The makestep directive takes a threshold in seconds and a number of updates, so makestep 1.0 3 allows the clock to be stepped when the offset exceeds one second during the first three updates and slewed afterwards, which is the standard compromise between fast start-up and safety. The driftfile directive names the file where the measured frequency error is saved, normally /var/lib/chrony/drift, so a restart does not start from zero. The rtcsync directive keeps the hardware clock in step with the system clock. The allow directive turns the machine into a server for a subnet, with deny and cmdallow for refinements, and local stratum 10 lets a server go on serving its own clock when it has lost its upstream sources. The hwtimestamp directive enables hardware timestamping, and trust marks a source as always correct [src: rocky-docs 43aa572d035bc704]. Further directives include minsources, leapsectz, log tracking and logdir for diagnostics, and keyfile and authentication options for authenticated sources. Newer versions also support network time security, a way of authenticating servers with certificates, through the nts option on a server line.

Checking the result is done with chronyc. The tracking command reports the reference ID (the server currently followed), the stratum of this host (the source's stratum plus one), the reference time, the current offset between the system clock and network time, the last and RMS offsets, the frequency error in parts per million, the residual frequency, the skew, the root delay and dispersion, the update interval and the leap status. The sources command lists each source with columns for mode, state, stratum, poll, reach, last receive and the last sample. The mode column shows ^ for a server, = for a peer and # for a local reference clock; the state column shows * for the best source, + for combined, - for not combined, ? for unusable, x for a probable error and ~ for too variable; the poll column is a base-2 logarithm of the polling interval in seconds; and the reach column is an octal register of the last eight attempts, so 377 means all eight succeeded. The sourcestats command shows the drift and offset estimation per source, -v adds explanatory captions, and makestep from chronyc steps the clock immediately [src: rhel-10-docs abee4c68710434f0] [src: rocky-docs 71a922c851aa11d5].

The usual diagnostics follow from these fields. If the sources list shows only ? entries or a reach of 0, the network path or firewall to port 123 is blocked. If chrony reports that it cannot synchronise because there is no majority, the sources disagree and the administrator needs more independent sources or a trusted one. If the offset is large and not shrinking, check the step policy and whether a virtualisation guest tool is also adjusting the clock. When chronyc cannot connect, check that the daemon is running and that the command socket is accessible; remote monitoring is restricted by default to the local host, and opening it is a deliberate step with cmdallow and bindcmdaddress [src: rhel-10-docs 123367e4f6fe704b].

Cloud and image-based systems configure time at first boot: image-mode builds can embed the chrony configuration in a provisioning file, with the same directives, so that an instance is synchronised without manual steps [src: rhel-10-docs 672bef82b5f48906]. Many clouds also offer a local time source reachable at a link-local address, which is both faster and not dependent on the public internet.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

The good is that the problem is largely solved for ordinary servers. A default chrony configuration synchronises within seconds of boot to within milliseconds of true time, tracks drift across restarts and survives network interruptions. The tools are small and the diagnostics are explicit, with fields that map directly to causes. The separation of the universal system clock from the presentation time zone means that daylight saving changes never affect stored timestamps.

The bad is that the interactions are easy to get wrong. Two services trying to discipline the clock, such as chrony and timesyncd, or chrony and a hypervisor integration, produce oscillation. Manual changes with date or set-time are overridden or refused. A time zone change does not affect running processes that cached the old zone. The RTC in local time is a persistent source of one-hour errors. Virtual machines need explicit care because their clocks pause when the guest is suspended, and the correct fix may be in the hypervisor, not the guest. Default configurations may point at public pools that firewalls block, leaving the machine silently unsynchronised.

The ugly is the set of failures that are invisible until they cause damage. A machine that never synchronised looks fine until a certificate or ticket check fails. A large step at the wrong moment can make a database or scheduled job run twice, skip a run or see time go backwards; stepping is therefore restricted after start-up. Unauthenticated time sources can be spoofed on the path, moving a clock to invalidate certificates or replay tokens. A server open to the world may be used as part of an amplification attack. And leap-second handling remains a hazard for software that does not expect a minute of 61 seconds.

## Applications beyond this domain

Time synchronisation is a building block of distributed systems. Databases that offer global consistency rely on tightly bounded clock error, and some publish the uncertainty interval so that applications can wait it out. Financial regulation requires traceable clock accuracy for trade timestamps. Telecommunications and power grids depend on phase alignment far beyond what network time protocols offer. Security systems use time in protocols such as one-time passwords, signed tokens and certificate validation, where an incorrect clock is both a reliability bug and a vulnerability.

The vocabulary of strata, offset, skew and dispersion carries over into other measurement problems: any system that estimates a hidden quantity from noisy, delayed observations from several sources, filters outliers, and decides which sources to trust faces the same structure. Hardware offload in network cards, and sub-microsecond protocols for industrial control, apply the same ideas with better timestamps. Even cluster schedulers and container runtimes depend on sane clocks for lease expiry and leader election.

## Examples

A first exercise reads the state of a machine. Run timedatectl and identify the three clocks and the zone, then run chronyc tracking and chronyc sources -v and explain the best source, the stratum of the host, the offset and the reach register. Write down what each field would look like if the network were blocked.

A second exercise edits the configuration: add a pool line with iburst, a makestep policy, a drift file, rtcsync and an allow line for a subnet, validate with chronyd -p -f against the file, restart, and watch the sources list converge. The lab for this topic does the file work and the time zone link.

A third exercise sets the zone: list zones, change the zone with set-timezone, confirm the symbolic link and show the same instant in two zones with the TZ variable. Then show that the stored timestamps did not change.

A fourth exercise provokes a step. On a disposable machine set the clock a few minutes wrong with the daemon stopped, start it, and observe whether it steps or slews according to makestep, and what the log shows.

A failing example is a firewall that drops outbound port 123: sources show reach 0 and the unreachable state. Another is two synchronisation services enabled at once. A third is a virtual machine that resumes from suspend with a large offset.

## Related problems and ideas

Neighbouring topics include logging, because ordering of events across hosts depends on clocks; scheduled jobs, which run by wall-clock time and are affected by zone and daylight saving; certificate and ticket lifetimes; and monotonic clocks, which applications should use to measure durations because they never jump. The precision time protocol, hardware timestamping and GPS-disciplined oscillators address needs beyond the network time protocol. Authenticated time through network time security addresses spoofing.

Ideas for practice: keep the RTC in UTC; run exactly one time service; prefer several independent sources, at least three or four; use iburst and a modest step policy; point fleets at internal servers and give those servers diverse upstreams; monitor offset and reach rather than assuming; store and exchange timestamps in universal time and convert only for display; and use monotonic time for durations in code.

## Active research

Research and standards work here includes authenticated time, with network time security now standardised and being deployed, and with studies of how many public servers and clients support it. Protocol work on fixing the weaknesses of the older design, on better filtering of asymmetric delays and on resilience against malicious sources continues. Hardware timestamping and boundary clocks push accuracy into the nanosecond range for data centres, with open implementations of the precision time protocol. Distributed databases continue to explore bounded-uncertainty clocks, and measurement studies examine how virtualised and containerised hosts keep time. The future of leap seconds has been decided in principle, with insertions due to stop by about 2035 (to verify), and software is preparing for the change.

## Future direction

Expect authenticated, certificate-based time to become the default for client configuration, with distributions shipping secure sources out of the box and warning about unauthenticated ones. Cloud platforms will continue to provide local, high-quality sources, and virtual machines will rely on host-provided clocks for correctness across suspend. Leap seconds will disappear from civil time, simplifying software. For the administrator the practice stays the same: let one daemon discipline the clock, keep the machine's universal time right, choose the zone only for presentation, and read the tracking and sources output when something looks wrong.
