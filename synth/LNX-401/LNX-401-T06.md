# Reviewing an incident so the estate improves

## Define the problem

After an outage, teams are tempted to restore service and move on. Without a record of what happened, why it happened and what will change, the same failure returns. The problem is to write a blameless review that is accurate on timeline, honest about contributing causes, and ends in specific, owned, checkable actions. This slice covers writing the review from evidence and checking its internal consistency.

## History and approaches

Aviation and healthcare developed formal incident analysis long before computing. Web-scale operators adopted blameless reviews, where the question is how the system allowed the error rather than who made it. Methods include timeline reconstruction, five-whys questioning and contributing-factor analysis, which argues that complex failures rarely have a single root cause. Templates standardised the structure: summary, impact, timeline, causes, what went well, what went badly, and actions.

## Current state

### The parts

A review has a header (title, dates, severity, authors), a summary, impact in measurable terms (duration, users, requests), a timeline with times in one zone, causes and contributing factors, detection and response assessment, and an action list with owners, due dates and a type (prevent, detect or mitigate).

### How the mechanism applies

The author gathers logs, alerts, chat and commit history, builds the timeline from timestamps, computes time to detect and time to recover, and checks that every action has an owner and a verifiable outcome. Consistency rules can be checked mechanically: timeline entries in order, durations that add up, every cause linked to at least one action, and no action without an owner.

### Generalised pattern

Reconstruct from evidence, separate cause from trigger, state impact in numbers, and convert each lesson into an owned action.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

Good reviews build trust and reduce repeats. Poor ones assign blame, list vague actions ("be more careful"), or never get followed up. Hindsight bias makes the failing decision look obvious. The ugly part is the long list of actions that nobody completes, so tracking completion is part of the practice.

## Applications beyond this domain

Security incident reports, safety investigations, project retrospectives and customer support escalations use the same structure.

## Examples

A worked example: a certificate on the directory server expired at night; monitoring had checked only the web certificate. The review shows detection by users after forty minutes, recovery in ninety, and actions: check all certificate sources, alert at thirty days, and add a renewal drill. A failing review: it names a person as root cause and lists two actions without owners; the checker rejects it.

## Related problems and ideas

Related practices are error budgets, risk registers, near-miss reporting and formal learning reviews.

## Active research

Studies look at what makes reviews lead to changes and at tools that assemble timelines automatically (to verify).

## Future direction

Expect tooling that links alerts, commits and chats into a draft timeline and tracking of action completion across teams.
