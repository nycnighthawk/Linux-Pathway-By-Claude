# Surviving the loss of a disk: redundant arrays with the md driver and mdadm

## Define the problem

Disks fail. They fail on a schedule that statistics describe well and that no individual drive respects: after a few years of service the chance that one of a dozen drives dies in a given year is high, and a single failure should not mean a night of restoring from backup. Two separate goals pull in different directions. **Availability and integrity**: keep serving data when a disk dies, ideally without anyone noticing until the monitoring system says so. **Performance and capacity**: combine several disks so that reads or writes spread across spindles and one file system can be larger than any one drive.

RAID (redundant array of independent disks) answers both by combining devices under defined layouts: striping for speed and size, mirroring for copies, parity for efficient redundancy. The administrator's questions are practical. Which level fits the workload and the number of disks? How is an array created, described to the system so that it assembles at boot, watched for failure, repaired after one, and extended when capacity runs out? How long does a rebuild take and what happens if a second disk fails during it? And what does RAID not protect against? It does nothing about deletion, corruption written faithfully to all copies, a lightning strike or a mistaken `rm`; the handbook of one distribution says plainly that RAID is not backup. [src: debian-handbook 835eed659851bc7c]

## History and approaches

The term came from a 1988 paper by Patterson, Gibson and Katz that proposed building reliable storage from cheap disks instead of one expensive one, and numbered the schemes. The numbering stuck, though the ordering of usefulness changed. In practice today's useful levels are few: RAID 0 (striping, no redundancy), RAID 1 (mirroring), RAID 5 (striping with one rotating parity block per stripe), RAID 6 (two parity blocks), and RAID 10 (striped mirrors). RAID 4 (a dedicated parity disk) survives in specialised designs. Linear mode appends devices end to end without striping. [src: man-pages 6dd328e7f2234d70]

Implementations came in three families. **Hardware RAID** puts the logic in a controller with its own processor and usually a battery-backed write cache; the operating system sees one disk. **Firmware ("fake") RAID** is configured in the BIOS but computed by the host CPU and driver; Intel's matrix and rapid storage formats and the industry DDF format are examples, and Linux reads them through the same md code using external metadata. **Software RAID** is implemented entirely in the kernel. Linux's md driver has been in the kernel since the 1990s, and `mdadm` replaced the older `raidtools` with a single command and a configuration file. On current Red Hat Enterprise Linux it is the preferred software RAID implementation, and its documentation describes the subsystems and the external metadata formats it supports. [src: rhel-10-docs 23eae1909cf1b584]

Several problems shaped the design. The **write hole** of parity RAID: if power fails after the data block of a stripe is written but before the parity block is, the stripe is silently inconsistent, and after a disk failure that stripe reconstructs wrongly. The responses were the write-intent bitmap (which limits resync after a crash to dirty regions), a write journal on a fast device, and the partial parity log that newer kernels offer. The **rebuild problem**: as drives grew to many terabytes, rebuild time grew to many hours, and the probability of meeting an unreadable sector on a surviving disk during that time stopped being negligible; double-parity RAID 6 and mirrors with three copies are the usual replies. The **metadata problem**: where on the disk the array description lives affects whether a boot loader can read a mirrored member as a plain disk, and why several superblock versions exist. And the **naming problem**: arrays assembled with a name that does not match the host once appeared as `/dev/md127`, because the array's stored host name differed from the machine's. [src: man-pages f798e255aac89d49]

The partial success is that md is mature and flexible (online level changes, reshape, growth), while its integrity guarantees stop at agreement between the member disks. It has no checksums of data, so it can tell that two mirror halves differ but not which is right. That gap is why file systems with end-to-end checksums such as Btrfs and ZFS pursue integrity differently, a point the course returns to elsewhere. [src: rocky-docs dbf21bb24a9d312e]

## Current state

### Levels at a glance

| Level | Min. devices | Usable capacity | Survives | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| linear | 1 | sum | nothing | concatenation only |
| RAID 0 | 2 | sum | nothing | speed and size; one failure loses everything |
| RAID 1 | 2 | one device | all but one | reads can use any copy; common for boot and small systems |
| RAID 4 | 3 | N-1 | one | dedicated parity disk, a write bottleneck |
| RAID 5 | 3 | N-1 | one | rotating parity; slow small writes; long rebuilds on large disks |
| RAID 6 | 4 | N-2 | two | double parity; recommended over RAID 5 for large disks |
| RAID 10 | 4 (md allows 2 or 3 with layouts) | about half | one per mirror pair, at least | mirrors, then striping; good for databases |

For RAID 10 the md layout options are `n2` (near, the default: copies adjacent), `f2` (far: copies in a second half, faster sequential reads) and `o2` (offset). [src: man-pages 6dd328e7f2234d70]

### Components

The **md driver** creates `/dev/mdN` block devices (with friendly names under `/dev/md/`). Members carry a **superblock** that describes the array so that it can assemble itself from the members alone. Versions: 0.90 (at the end of the device, limited to 28 members, understood by old boot loaders and by in-kernel autodetect), 1.0 (at the end), 1.1 (at the start) and 1.2 (4 KiB from the start; the default for new arrays). The **chunk size** is the stripe unit for levels with striping (`--chunk`, in KiB; the default is 512 KiB in current mdadm). A **write-intent bitmap** (`--bitmap=internal`) records which regions may be out of sync, so that after an unclean shutdown or after a briefly removed disk is re-added, only those regions are resynchronised. A **write journal** (`--write-journal`) or a partial parity log (`--consistency-policy=ppl` on RAID 5) closes the write hole. **Spare** devices wait idle and are pulled in automatically on failure. External metadata containers (IMSM, DDF) hold several volumes on one set of disks.

### Creating and describing an array

```bash
mdadm --create /dev/md0 --raid-devices=2 --level=mirror --bitmap=internal /dev/sdb1 /dev/sdc1
mdadm --create /dev/md1 --level=5 --raid-devices=4 --spare-devices=1 --chunk=256 /dev/sd[b-f]1
mdadm --create /dev/md2 --level=10 --layout=f2 --raid-devices=4 /dev/sd[g-j]1
cat /proc/mdstat
mdadm --detail /dev/md0
```

After `--create` the array starts a background resync; it is usable immediately, but redundancy does not exist until the sync finishes. `--assume-clean` skips the initial sync and is only for arrays that will be completely rewritten (or testing). Watch progress in `/proc/mdstat`, where an array line looks like `md0 : active raid1 sdc1[1] sdb1[0]` followed by a size line ending in a status such as `[2/2] [UU]` (each `U` is a working member, an underscore is a missing one), and a recovery or resync line with percentage, speed and finish time while work is in progress. The same state is in `/sys/block/md0/md/`: `array_state`, `degraded`, `sync_action`, `sync_completed`, `raid_disks`, `level`, `chunk_size`, `mismatch_cnt`. [src: man-pages 1f768c38d4b021e8]

### Persisting and assembling

Arrays are assembled by `mdadm --assemble --scan`, which uses `/etc/mdadm.conf` (RHEL family) or `/etc/mdadm/mdadm.conf` (Debian family) and the superblocks. The file is optional but strongly recommended. A minimal one:

```
DEVICE partitions
MAILADDR root
ARRAY /dev/md0 metadata=1.2 name=host:0 UUID=3c1f9d06:0b6a7e2d:8a24f6b5:11c9d7aa
```

Generate the ARRAY lines with `mdadm --detail --scan >> /etc/mdadm.conf` (or `--examine --scan`), review them, and then rebuild the initramfs so that the root array assembles at boot (`dracut -f` on RHEL family, `update-initramfs -u` on Debian family). `MAILADDR` and `PROGRAM` define what the monitor does on events. Related: `mdadm --incremental` is what udev calls as member devices appear, so that assembly is event-driven, and `mdadm --assemble --force` is the repair tool for an array whose members have fallen out of step. [src: man-pages 855607e604b9bcdd] [src: archwiki 30ec1f552c43ee76]

### Monitoring

`mdmonitor.service` (or `mdadm --monitor --scan --daemonise --delay=1800`) watches arrays and reports events such as `Fail`, `DegradedArray`, `SpareActive`, `RebuildFinished` and `NewArray` by mail or program. Without a working mail path, a degraded array can run silently for months until the second disk fails, which is the most common story behind RAID data loss. Test the path with `mdadm --monitor --scan --oneshot --test`. A scheduled **scrub** reads every block and compares: `echo check > /sys/block/md0/md/sync_action`, then read `mismatch_cnt`. Debian ships `checkarray` with a monthly cron entry; RHEL ships `raid-check` with `/etc/sysconfig/raid-check` and a systemd timer. Setting `repair` instead of `check` rewrites mismatches, but with plain mirrors md cannot know which side is correct. Security baselines sometimes list the monitor service as required or, where RAID is not used, as unnecessary; check your profile. [src: compliance-as-code 35ff606317e14bb6]

### When a disk fails

```bash
mdadm --detail /dev/md0                      # State : clean, degraded ; shows which slot is missing
mdadm /dev/md0 --fail /dev/sdb1              # mark failed if the kernel has not
mdadm /dev/md0 --remove /dev/sdb1            # detach it
# replace the hardware, partition the new disk like the old one
sfdisk -d /dev/sdc | sfdisk /dev/sdb
mdadm /dev/md0 --add /dev/sdb1               # rebuild starts automatically
watch cat /proc/mdstat
```

Checks before and after: confirm which physical disk is which (`lsblk -o NAME,SERIAL`), copy the boot partition and boot loader to the new disk on a mirrored boot disk, and verify that `smartctl -a` of the surviving disks does not show impending trouble. A **proactive replacement** of a healthy but ageing member uses `--replace` with `--with`, which builds the new copy while the old member stays in service: `mdadm /dev/md0 --replace /dev/sdb1 --with /dev/sde1`. After a crash or a disk's short disappearance, `--re-add` with a bitmap resynchronises only the changed regions. Speed limits are in `/proc/sys/dev/raid/speed_limit_min` and `speed_limit_max` (KiB/s per device) and per array in `sync_speed_min` and `sync_speed_max`. [src: man-pages 2b2e72967abf8c34]

### Growing and reshaping

```bash
mdadm --grow /dev/md1 --raid-devices=5           # add a member to a RAID 5, then --add first
mdadm --grow /dev/md0 --size=max                 # after replacing members with larger disks
mdadm --grow /dev/md0 --level=5 --raid-devices=3 # convert RAID 1 to RAID 5 (needs a backup file or room)
```

After the array grows, grow the layers above it: `pvresize` if it is an LVM physical volume, `resize2fs` or `xfs_growfs` for a file system. A reshape is slow, interruptible only with care, and requires a **backup file** (`--backup-file=/root/md1.bak`, on another device) for some changes. Never reshape without a verified backup.

### Stopping and removing

`umount`, `mdadm --stop /dev/md0`, then `mdadm --zero-superblock /dev/sdb1 /dev/sdc1` to erase the superblocks (otherwise the members reassemble as soon as they are seen), and remove the ARRAY line and rebuild the initramfs. `blkdeactivate` can unwind stacked layers (file system, LVM, md) in order. [src: man-pages 3157526848098722]

### Layering

The common stack is partitions or whole disks, then md, then LVM or a file system directly. LVM can also do RAID itself with the same kernel code (`lvcreate --type raid1`), which removes one layer of tooling but gives up mdadm's monitoring conventions. A root-on-RAID1 install needs the boot loader to be installed on every disk (`grub-install /dev/sda; grub-install /dev/sdb`), or on a mirrored ESP in a metadata 1.0 array. [src: archwiki bcd0a7dde6769d83]

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

**The good.** md is in the kernel, open and well documented. It turns commodity disks into volumes that survive a failure, offers a spectrum of levels, grows and reshapes online, and is observable through plain text interfaces (`/proc/mdstat`, sysfs). A mirror is a very simple and robust thing: either member can be read as a normal disk, and a mirror can be recovered with ordinary tools. Event-driven assembly and a config file make it dependable at boot. The learning cost is low and the skills carry over to every similar system.

**The bad.** RAID levels with parity suffer from slow small writes (read-modify-write) and long rebuilds, with a window of reduced redundancy that grows with disk size; RAID 5 on large disks has become hard to defend. md does not checksum data, so silent corruption is propagated or, with a mirror, only noticed as a count in `mismatch_cnt`. Monitoring is separate from the array: if mail does not work, the array tells no one. An array is not a backup, as every vendor guide repeats.

**The ugly.** Operations on arrays have sharp edges. `mdadm --create` on the wrong devices destroys existing data instantly, and `--create` re-run over old members with different parameters scrambles the layout. Naming mistakes, such as arrays that renamed themselves to `md127` after a hardware change, break `fstab` entries written with device names (use UUIDs). A second failure during rebuild, a power loss without a bitmap (a multi-hour resync), disks of the same batch failing together, and controllers that lie about write-cache flushes are the real-world causes of lost arrays. Drives with different sector sizes, or SMR drives in a parity array, create unpleasant performance cliffs. Finally, "fake RAID" on a motherboard looks like hardware RAID but depends on host software, so moving a set to another machine requires the same support.

## Applications beyond this domain

The same ideas of striping, mirroring and parity shape many systems. Distributed storage replaces disks with nodes: Ceph and similar systems keep replicas or erasure-coded fragments across hosts, which is parity RAID generalised to a network, with the same trade-off between capacity overhead and rebuild traffic. Cloud block volumes advertise replication inside their own service, so RAID inside a guest usually adds little except striping for throughput across several volumes. Databases reproduce the pattern with replicas and write-ahead logs. File systems with built-in redundancy (Btrfs profiles, ZFS vdevs) apply RAID-like logic with checksums and so can repair silent corruption; the RAID-versus-pooled-file-system argument is one of the standing debates in storage. [src: rocky-docs d74d19fb8f505c84]

High availability clusters link the topic to the next layer of redundancy: shared storage and replicated block devices keep services running through a node loss, while RAID inside each node handles a disk loss. The Rocky documentation on Pacemaker and the high-availability part of the course show where RAID ends and cluster storage begins. [src: rocky-docs 502048b210f24670]

## Examples

**Mirror two partitions and put a file system on them.**

```bash
mdadm --create /dev/md0 --metadata=1.2 --level=1 --raid-devices=2 /dev/vdb1 /dev/vdc1
mkfs.ext4 -L mirror /dev/md0
mdadm --detail --scan | tee -a /etc/mdadm.conf
dracut -f
```

**Read the status of every array in one line each.**

```bash
awk '/^md/ {print $1, $3, $4}' /proc/mdstat
for m in /sys/block/md*/md; do printf "%s %s %s\n" "$m" "$(cat $m/array_state)" "$(cat $m/degraded)"; done
```

**Replace a failed member of a RAID 5 and watch it rebuild.**

```bash
mdadm /dev/md1 --fail /dev/sdd1 --remove /dev/sdd1
sfdisk -d /dev/sdb | sfdisk /dev/sdd
mdadm /dev/md1 --add /dev/sdd1
watch -n 5 cat /proc/mdstat
```

**Run a monthly consistency check by hand and read the result.**

```bash
echo check > /sys/block/md1/md/sync_action
cat /sys/block/md1/md/mismatch_cnt
```

**Test the alert path before you need it.** `mdadm --monitor --scan --oneshot --test` prints one test message per array; if nothing reaches a mailbox, fix mail first.

## Related problems and ideas

RAID interacts with nearly every other storage topic. With **LVM**, md arrays make good physical volumes; with **partitioning**, the type GUID for Linux RAID marks members but md does not require it; with **swap**, a failed disk can crash processes if swap sat on it; with **LUKS**, encryption goes either on top of the array (one key) or on each member (many keys, more overhead). **SMART monitoring** (`smartctl`, `smartd`) warns of failing drives before they die and complements the array's own events. **Hot spares and spare pools** reduce the window of vulnerability; **scrubbing schedules** reduce latent errors. For capacity planning, remember that usable space is not the sum of disks, that mixed-size members waste space under the smallest one, and that a rebuild consumes I/O the production workload needs.

There is also the neighbouring question of **multipath**: the md multipath level is deprecated in favour of the device-mapper multipath tools, because a path to a device is not a copy of it. The final topic in this course covers that device-mapper approach. [src: man-pages 6dd328e7f2234d70]

## Active research

Work in the md subsystem includes better handling of the write hole (the partial parity log, write journals on persistent memory or fast flash), faster and lower-impact reshape and rebuild, improvements for very large arrays and many-core scaling of parity computation (multi-threaded RAID 5 and 6 work), better support for zoned devices, and integration of integrity layers (`dm-integrity` under md for detect-and-repair of silent corruption). In the wider field, erasure coding with locally repairable codes, declustered parity (where rebuild work is spread across all disks, not concentrated on a single replacement) and checksummed redundancy in file systems are active areas, along with studies of disk failure correlation and the effect of rebuild times on risk.

## Future direction

Three trends stand out. Capacity growth pushes toward double parity or triple mirrors and declustered layouts, because single-parity rebuilds on very large disks are too risky. Solid-state drives change the economics: write amplification, wear levelling and the speed of rebuild all differ, so some guidance (stripe sizes, spare policy, scrub frequency) is being rewritten. And redundancy moves up the stack: object stores, replicated databases and cloud volumes provide it at the service level, leaving local md arrays most relevant for boot disks, small servers, labs and edge systems. For the administrator the habits stay the same. Test the alert path; keep a current copy of the array description (`mdadm --detail --scan`) off the host; know the replacement procedure before you need it; and keep backups, because the array protects the disks and not the data. [src: rhel-10-docs a032784ee7b4bfbf]
