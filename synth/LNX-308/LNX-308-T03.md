# Connecting guests to networks: NAT, bridges, macvtap and VLANs

## Define the problem

A guest's network card is a software object. It must be connected to something: other guests on the same host, the host itself, or the physical network beyond. Which of these is right depends on what the guest is for. A throw-away test guest only needs outbound access and does not care about its address. A web server needs a stable address that clients can reach. A set of guests that form a three-tier application need a private segment that the outside cannot see. A hosting provider needs guests of different customers kept apart on the same wire and on the same host. Each requirement implies a different place to attach the virtual cable and different rules for who can see whose frames.

The mechanics stack up in layers that are easy to confuse. The guest has a virtual NIC (an emulated model such as e1000 or the paravirtual virtio-net). The host end of that NIC is a `tap` device (a layer-2 interface backed by a file descriptor QEMU holds) or a macvtap device, and the host decides what the tap is plugged into: a Linux bridge, an Open vSwitch bridge, the physical NIC through macvtap, or nothing but a private point-to-point channel. Then there is addressing (DHCP, DNS, routing and NAT), filtering (what the guest is allowed to say, so that a compromised guest cannot spoof another's MAC or IP), and segmentation (VLANs, VXLAN, or separate bridges). Failure means guests that can talk to the Internet but not to the host (a macvtap property), guests that vanish from the network after a firewall reload, DHCP servers answering on the wrong segment, or a bridge that floods broadcast storms because of a forwarding loop.

This slice covers the network models libvirt offers, the Linux bridge and VLAN-aware bridge, macvtap, the role of Open vSwitch, the libvirt network XML and the interface XML that refers to it, and the diagnostic commands for finding where a frame stops. Performance tuning of the datapath (multiqueue, vhost) belongs to T04, and overlay networks and software-defined networking for clusters to T08 and to the networking courses.

## History and approaches

Early QEMU offered **user-mode networking** (SLIRP): a TCP/IP stack inside the QEMU process that translated guest traffic into ordinary socket calls from an unprivileged user. It needed no root, no host configuration and no bridge, which is why it remains QEMU's default when nothing else is requested. Its limits taught the field why other models were needed: it is slow, the guest is unreachable from outside unless ports are explicitly forwarded, ICMP and many protocols do not work, and the guest cannot be seen by other hosts at all. The second approach was the **tap device plus Linux bridge**: the kernel's software switch (the bridge module, introduced in the 2.2 and 2.4 kernels) joins the host's physical NIC and all guests' taps into one layer-2 domain, so that a guest appears on the LAN like a real machine. Administrators wrote shell scripts for `brctl addbr`, `brctl addif` and `ifup`, and the scripts broke when the physical NIC was a wireless card (which cannot join a bridge because the access point accepts only one MAC) or when NetworkManager reconfigured the interface. Xen's early network scripts and the libvirt `default` network (introduced to give desktop users working networking without root-owned bridges) came from this experience.

libvirt's **virtual networks** formalised the NAT variant: an isolated bridge `virbr0`, a dnsmasq instance serving DHCP and DNS on it, and iptables or nftables rules for masquerading. This is an example of a partial success. It turned guest networking into something that works out of the box, but guests are invisible from outside, which was fine for laptops and wrong for servers; and the firewall rules libvirt writes interacted badly with other firewall managers for years, notably with `firewalld` and Docker, until libvirt gained a firewalld backend and nftables support (the libvirt documentation lists the firewall backend options and `<forward mode='nat'>` with `ipv6='yes'`, available since 6.5.0, as an explicit opt-in because IPv6 had historically been routed rather than translated [src: libvirt-docs 92d455780071b08d]).

Pressure for better performance and isolation produced two further families. **macvtap** (kernel 2.6.34, 2010) replaced bridge plus tap with one device: a macvlan sub-interface stacked on the physical NIC, with its own MAC address, opened as a character device by QEMU. It skips the bridge code path and needs no host-side bridge configuration, and for exactly that reason it became popular with cloud-style deployments that wanted direct attachment; KubeVirt's macvtap binding plugin claims the same advantage: guests sit on the node's own layer-2 network without a bridge in between [src: kubevirt-user-guide 4d4cef5bff0ab01f]. Its weakness is that guests cannot talk to the host through the same NIC in the common modes, because the frame leaves the NIC and the switch will not hairpin it back. **Open vSwitch** (2009) took the opposite path: a programmable multi-layer virtual switch with OpenFlow, tunnelling (VXLAN, GRE, Geneve), flow tables and rich monitoring, widely used in clouds and managed by OVN. It was solving the problem that the Linux bridge was a plain learning switch with limited VLAN support. That limitation was removed from the other side: the Linux bridge gained **VLAN filtering** (kernel 3.8, 2013), and with it a single bridge can behave like a managed switch with per-port VLAN membership.

SR-IOV, which divides a physical NIC into hardware virtual functions that a guest owns directly, appears as a third approach for highest performance and is reached through libvirt's `hostdev` forward mode; it trades live migration, filtering and flexibility for speed.

The lessons that survived: keep the common case (NAT) trivial; offer a bridge for guests that must be seen; do not rely on the physical NIC to behave like a bridge port (wireless cannot); separate the datapath from policy so that a guest cannot forge addresses; and do VLAN separation in the kernel bridge or the switch rather than by creating a bridge per VLAN.

## Current state

**libvirt network objects.** A *network* is a libvirt object stored as XML under `/etc/libvirt/qemu/networks/`, manipulated with `virsh net-define`, `net-start`, `net-autostart`, `net-list --all`, `net-dumpxml`, `net-edit`, `net-destroy` and `net-undefine`. Its `forward` element determines the mode.

- No `forward` element: an *isolated* network. Guests on it reach each other and the host, nothing else. A DHCP range is optional.
- `forward mode='nat'`: guests are translated to the host's address on the way out. `<nat><port start='1024' end='65535'/></nat>` bounds the source port range; `dev=` pins an outgoing interface.
- `forward mode='route'`: forwarding without translation, which needs routes on the upstream network pointing back to the host. This suits an environment where the guests' subnet is routed.
- `forward mode='open'`: like route but libvirt adds no firewall rules, leaving policy to the administrator.
- `forward mode='bridge'`: a network defined by naming an existing bridge (`<bridge name='br0'/>`), so that guests can refer to it by network name yet libvirt does not manage the bridge.
- `forward mode='private' | 'vepa' | 'passthrough' | 'bridge'` with an `<interface dev=.../>` pool: macvtap modes, where libvirt picks the least-used physical interface from the pool, or fails when a mode cannot share a device [src: libvirt-docs 53dfaa290ffed70c].
- `forward mode='hostdev'`: an SR-IOV VF pool.

A NAT network's `<ip address='192.168.122.1' netmask='255.255.255.0'><dhcp><range start='192.168.122.2' end='192.168.122.254'/><host mac='52:54:00:...' name='db1' ip='192.168.122.10'/></dhcp></ip>` provides the gateway and DHCP, including fixed-address reservations. `virsh net-dhcp-leases default` lists leases. A `<portgroup>` section defines named bundles of settings (such as a VLAN tag or bandwidth limits) that interfaces refer to.

**The interface element.** In a domain, `<interface type='network'><source network='default'/><model type='virtio'/></interface>` joins a libvirt network, creating a tap on the bridge that backs it. `type='bridge'` with `<source bridge='br0'/>` attaches directly to a host bridge, and with `<virtualport type='openvswitch'/>` to an OVS bridge. `type='direct'` with `<source dev='eth0' mode='bridge'/>` requests macvtap. `type='ethernet'` leaves everything to a script. `type='user'` is the SLIRP mode and is used for unprivileged session guests. Each interface can have `<mac address=.../>` (the locally administered prefix 52:54:00 is libvirt's convention), `<vlan><tag id='100'/></vlan>` for access-port semantics on an OVS or VLAN-aware bridge, `<bandwidth>` with `inbound` and `outbound` token-bucket limits, `<driver name='vhost' queues='4'/>`, and `<filterref filter='clean-traffic'/>`, which attaches a rule set from libvirt's network filter subsystem (`nwfilter`) that blocks MAC, IP and ARP spoofing. The domain XML reference describes these in its devices chapters [src: libvirt-docs 9a62dc37e5bf8222].

**Linux bridges.** On a host, a bridge `br0` holds the physical NIC as a port and receives guest taps. A persistent definition is made with NetworkManager (`nmcli connection add type bridge ifname br0 ...`, then enslaving the NIC with `type bridge-slave`), with systemd-networkd (`.netdev` of Kind=bridge plus `.network` units), or with Netplan on Ubuntu. The host's IP address moves from the NIC to the bridge. `bridge link`, `bridge fdb show`, `ip -d link show br0` and `bridge vlan show` expose ports, learned MAC addresses and VLAN tables. Spanning tree is off by default in many setups (`bridge stp_state`), and the `bridge-nf-call-iptables` sysctl, relevant to the `br_netfilter` module, decides whether bridged frames traverse iptables. Because a bridge floods unknown unicast and broadcast, a loop made by plugging two ports into the same switch without STP produces a storm.

**VLANs.** There are three ways to tag. The older: create a VLAN sub-interface on the physical NIC (`ip link add link eth0 name eth0.100 type vlan id 100`) and a bridge per VLAN (`br100`) holding it; the `ip-link` manual documents the `vlan` type and its options [src: man-pages f1297b4373ee9242]. This works but means a bridge and a sub-interface for every network. The modern way: a single bridge with `vlan_filtering 1`, the NIC as a trunk port with allowed VLANs, and each guest tap as an access port with a `pvid` and `untagged` flag. Red Hat's documentation describes enabling VLAN filtering on a bridge so that many VM networks share one uplink and the host needs no per-VM tap configuration, because libvirt adds the taps dynamically when guests start [src: rhel-10-docs 51be69dadc42d12d]. Guest-side tagging is the third: the guest runs its own 802.1Q sub-interfaces and the host passes frames unchanged, which needs the bridge to allow all VLANs on that port ('trunk to guest'). In libvirt, a bridge-mode network or portgroup with `<vlan trunk='yes'><tag id='10'/><tag id='20'/></vlan>` expresses it for OVS, and for native Linux bridges recent libvirt versions can set VLAN membership as well (to verify the exact version in which bridge VLAN support arrived).

**macvtap.** Four modes: `bridge` (guests on the same parent NIC can talk to each other directly, and the traffic is switched internally), `vepa` (all frames go out to the external switch, which must reflect them back, a feature called reflective relay or hairpin mode), `private` (no guest-to-guest traffic even through the switch), and `passthrough` (the whole NIC is dedicated to one guest). In all of them the host cannot exchange traffic with its own guests through the parent NIC. The standard workaround is a macvlan sub-interface for the host on the same parent. The Docker documentation on macvlan and ipvlan drivers describes the same family from the container side, and notes that ipvlan in L2 mode shares the parent's MAC address among sub-interfaces whereas macvlan assigns each its own, which matters for switches that limit MAC counts per port [src: docker-docs 2505dcc6047828fe] [src: docker-docs 3045ea6b6f4616e6].

**Open vSwitch.** `ovs-vsctl add-br ovsbr0`, `add-port ovsbr0 eth0`, `set port vnet0 tag=100`, `show`, `ovs-ofctl dump-flows` and `ovs-appctl fdb/show` administer it; the ovs-vsctl manual page describes the commands for bridges, ports, interfaces and database records [src: man-pages 72b4cc11f1958b79]. A libvirt guest attaches with `<virtualport type='openvswitch'/>`. The point of OVS in a guest-networking context is features the bridge lacks: VXLAN/Geneve tunnels between hosts, port mirroring, QoS, sFlow, per-flow ACLs and central control through OVN.

**Diagnostic order.** When a guest "has no network", walk the path: in the guest, `ip addr`, `ip route`, `ping` the gateway; on the host, `virsh domiflist NAME` gives the tap name and MAC; `ip link show vnetN` shows state; `bridge link` shows whether it is a port and in `forwarding` state; `virsh net-dhcp-leases` or `journalctl -u dnsmasq`, if DHCP is the issue; `tcpdump -ni vnetN` and `-ni br0` to see where frames stop; `nft list ruleset` or `iptables-save` to see libvirt's and others' rules; `sysctl net.ipv4.ip_forward` for NAT and routed networks.

**Generalised pattern.** Virtual networking replicates physical networking at every layer: NICs, cables (tap, veth), switches (bridge, OVS), VLANs, routers (forwarding and NAT), DHCP and firewalls. A reliable way to reason about it is to draw the same diagram you would draw with physical equipment and check each hop.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

**The good.** The default NAT network gives working connectivity within seconds, with DHCP, DNS and an isolated address space, and nothing outside has to be configured. libvirt's network definitions are portable text and reproducible. Linux bridges are in the kernel, fast enough for most use, and with VLAN filtering they cover the typical needs of an enterprise rack. macvtap is simple, avoids a configuration step on hosts, and its performance is good. Open vSwitch brings tunnelling and observability. Bandwidth limiting and network filters protect shared hosts.

**The bad.** There are more models than a beginner can reason about, and the same result can be achieved in four ways with subtle differences. NAT hides guests, so inbound services need port forwards or a routed network; libvirt has no built-in port forward declaration for NAT networks and administrators write hooks (`/etc/libvirt/hooks/qemu`) that call iptables or nft. Wireless NICs cannot be bridged in the usual manner. Network configuration on the host (NetworkManager, systemd-networkd, Netplan, ifupdown) varies by distribution, so the same bridge is created with different commands in each. Layered firewalls (libvirt, firewalld, Docker, Kubernetes CNI plugins) compete for the same chains. Troubleshooting needs tools on both sides of the boundary.

**The ugly.** The notorious one is the macvtap host-isolation gotcha: the guest works for every machine except the hypervisor itself, because the kernel sends frames from the macvtap straight out and does not loop them back to the parent NIC's own stack. Another is Docker setting the iptables FORWARD policy to DROP and thereby breaking guest NAT after Docker installs on the same host; the libvirt and Docker firewall rules need to be reconciled in a dedicated chain or with nftables. Another is MAC address handling: cloned guests with duplicate MACs on one bridge cause intermittent loss that looks like a switch problem; hosts with port security on the upstream switch drop frames from any MAC the switch does not expect, which also affects macvtap's extra MAC addresses and bridged guests. Another is the bridge MAC address change: when a NIC is enslaved, the bridge takes the lowest port MAC, and if a guest tap has a numerically lower address the bridge's identity changes and the host loses connectivity for a moment. And spanning-tree interactions (a bridge delay of 15 seconds before forwarding on a new port) show up as DHCP timeouts at boot. Open vSwitch is configured in a database, so its state can survive reboots even when the host's network configuration no longer mentions it, which produces ghost ports.

Security-wise, the default libvirt bridge lets any guest on it talk to every other guest and the host, and nothing stops ARP spoofing unless an `nwfilter` is attached. Mitigation: use per-tenant bridges or VLANs, enable `clean-traffic` or equivalent, avoid placing the management interface on guest-visible networks, and keep host services bound to specific addresses.

## Applications beyond this domain

The same constructs are the plumbing of containers: Docker's bridge driver is a Linux bridge with veth pairs and NAT; its macvlan and ipvlan drivers are the macvtap family; Kubernetes CNI plugins such as bridge, macvlan and ipvlan are thin wrappers on the same kernel features; the Docker documentation notes the L3 and 802.1Q trunk examples for ipvlan [src: docker-docs 03ffbbb4402eae49]. KubeVirt maps VM interfaces to pod networks with bridge, masquerade and macvtap bindings, and its release notes record continuing changes to SR-IOV and binding plugins [src: kubevirt-user-guide 2981c186b409400a]. Cloud virtual private networks, with VXLAN overlays and virtual routers, are the same pieces with a control plane on top; OVN is the open-source version, and its architecture manual describes logical switches and routers realised as OpenFlow rules by the controller on each host [src: man-pages 0983663752c0dce8]. The Rocky networking labs show the basic device, address and interface creation exercises that this builds on [src: rocky-docs fe08832f3fbd7138]. Network testing labs on a laptop with namespaces and veth pairs use the same abstractions without any guest.

## Examples

**An isolated segment for a three-tier test.** Define a network with no `forward` element and a private subnet: `virsh net-define db-net.xml; virsh net-autostart db-net; virsh net-start db-net`. Attach app and db guests to it, and give only the app server a second interface on `default`. The database cannot be reached from or reach the Internet, yet the app server can talk to both.

**Bridged server on a VLAN-aware bridge.** On the host, create `br0` with `vlan_filtering` enabled, enslave `eno1` as a trunk allowing VLANs 10 and 20. In the domain XML use `<interface type='bridge'><source bridge='br0'/><vlan><tag id='20'/></vlan><model type='virtio'/></interface>`, if the libvirt version supports it, or `bridge vlan add vid 20 pvid untagged dev vnet3` by hook otherwise. The guest sees untagged frames, and the switch sees tag 20 on the trunk.

**Failing: macvtap guest cannot reach the host.** A backup agent on the host cannot connect to a guest attached with `type='direct'`. Every other machine on the LAN can. Cause: macvtap traffic does not return through the parent NIC. Fix: either add a macvlan interface on the host (`ip link add mv0 link eno1 type macvlan mode bridge`) and use it for host-guest traffic, or switch the guest to a real bridge.

**Failing: guests lose NAT after installing Docker.** Outbound traffic from guests stops after Docker's installation. `iptables -S FORWARD` shows policy DROP and no accept rule for `virbr0`. Fix: restore libvirt's rules by restarting its network, or use nftables and ensure each manager keeps its rules in a table it owns. The incident shows why a diagnosis of "NAT broken" should start with the rule dump, not the guest.

## Related problems and ideas

Neighbouring problems are address management (IPAM and DHCP at scale), service discovery, load balancing across guests, and traffic observability. Alternatives to the stack described: a **routed** design where each guest or host-pair is a /32 announced by BGP (popular in large data centres, as it avoids layer-2 domains altogether); Proxmox's software-defined networking zones (simple, VLAN, VXLAN, EVPN) which automate the above; **ipvlan** instead of macvlan for switches that limit MAC addresses; **vhost-user** with DPDK or VPP for userspace datapaths in network-function virtualisation; and **eBPF**-based datapaths such as Cilium that bypass the bridge. Not yet mainstream: **virtio-net hardware offload** by DPUs and SmartNICs, where the virtio ring is served directly by hardware (vDPA), and `passt`/`pasta`, a modern replacement for SLIRP that provides unprivileged user-mode networking with good performance, which libvirt and QEMU now support as the `passt` backend.

## Active research

Work centres on the datapath's cost and its isolation. Researchers and vendors study offloading the virtual switch to SmartNICs and DPUs, on-NIC connection tracking, and the consequences for live migration (the NIC state has to move). In the kernel community, the bridge's FDB and VLAN code, multicast snooping and the replacement of ageing netfilter bridge hooks are active. In libvirt the firewall backend is converging on nftables with firewalld cooperation, and `passt` integration is maturing. Security research examines side channels and spoofing in shared virtual switches. Open questions include how to describe per-guest network policy declaratively in a form that is portable between libvirt, Kubernetes and cloud APIs, and how to migrate guests with SR-IOV or vDPA devices without a visible outage.

## Future direction

Expect nftables to be the sole firewall backend in new libvirt releases, the Linux bridge with VLAN filtering to remain the default building block, and `passt` to replace SLIRP for session guests. Larger fleets move to routed or EVPN-based fabrics where the hypervisor is a layer-3 node, with cluster managers such as Proxmox exposing that as zone types. Watch DPU offload, the growth of vDPA as the vendor-neutral way to give guests hardware virtio without losing migration, and the slow retirement of per-distribution host network scripts in favour of NetworkManager or networkd. For the practitioner, the durable skill is the habit of drawing the path from guest NIC to destination and testing each hop.
