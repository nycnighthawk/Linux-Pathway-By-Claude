# Disaster-recovery drills: objectives, plans, rehearsals and the evidence that recovery works

## Define the problem

High availability copes with the loss of a component: a node, a disk, a switch. **Disaster recovery** (DR) copes with the loss of a whole site, a whole data set, or the trust in a system: a fire, a flood, a ransomware attack, a mistaken command that wipes the production database, a cloud region outage. The two are related but different. HA hides failure by redundancy that is always on. DR accepts an outage and restores service from a copy that was prepared in advance, within a stated time and with a stated amount of lost data.

The uncomfortable fact about DR is that a plan nobody has executed is a hypothesis. Backups that were never restored, runbooks that assume a person who left the company, failover scripts that point at hosts that were renamed, and credentials stored only inside the system they are needed to recover are normal findings the first time an organisation runs a drill. A **DR drill** (also called a game day, a failover test or a recovery exercise) is a scheduled, controlled rehearsal of the plan that turns a hypothesis into evidence, finds the gaps while they are cheap, and trains the people.

This topic teaches how to set recovery objectives, how to design the architecture that meets them, how to run drills of increasing realism, and how to record the result. It builds on the previous topics (cluster failover, replicated data, database standbys) and treats them as parts of a recovery design, not as a recovery plan on their own.

## History and approaches

Formal contingency planning grew in the mainframe era, when a data centre loss could end a company, and produced vocabulary still in use: hot, warm and cold sites; recovery time and recovery point objectives; business impact analysis. The control catalogue of the US government makes the practice explicit. Its contingency-plan control requires a plan that identifies essential functions, sets recovery objectives, restoration priorities and metrics, names roles and contact details, covers continuing essential functions during a disruption, and covers restoration to full operation without loss of the planned controls; it must be distributed to key people, reviewed at a set frequency and coordinated with incident handling [src: nist-sp800-53 27013480f70f00fc]. The testing control then asks for the plan to be exercised, with enhancements for coordination with related plans, for automated mechanisms that give more complete coverage and more realistic scenarios, and for full recovery and reconstitution to a known state as part of the test [src: nist-sp800-53 d21eee504a3d162c] and [src: nist-sp800-53 a44bf68582278417].

Two objectives define a design. The **recovery time objective (RTO)** is the longest tolerable time from the disaster to a working service. The **recovery point objective (RPO)** is the longest tolerable window of lost data, measured backwards from the disaster. An RPO of zero needs synchronous replication to a second site; an RPO of fifteen minutes allows asynchronous replication or frequent log shipping; an RPO of a day is met by nightly backups. These two numbers, set by the business and not by the engineers, determine the cost: halving them roughly multiplies the price. A third number is often added, the **maximum tolerable downtime**, beyond which the loss becomes unacceptable, and the **recovery capacity** (how much of the normal load the recovered site must carry).

Strategies for the standby site are classified by readiness. A **backup and restore** design keeps only backups off-site and rebuilds from them: cheapest, RTO in hours or days. A **pilot light** keeps a minimal live core (data replication and a few servers) and scales out on demand. A **warm standby** runs a reduced copy of the full stack. A **hot site** or **active/active** design runs full capacity in two places and shifts traffic. Cloud providers use the same ladder in their guidance. The important lesson is that every step up the ladder costs more and adds complexity; the objectives pick the rung.

Tooling covers each rung. For backup and restore on Linux, Red Hat ships **Relax-and-Recover (ReaR)**, which produces a system backup and a bootable rescue image that replicates the storage layout and can restore on different hardware; its documentation presents it both as a disaster-recovery solution and as a migration tool, and warns that restoring to a disk erases whatever it holds [src: rhel-10-docs 7359cbcd0c486e5c] and [src: rhel-10-docs 5f1b7326bd94962f]. For application data, database replication and archived logs provide the data side (see the previous topic). For multi-site clusters, Pacemaker offers a ticket mechanism: a **cluster ticket manager** such as Booth runs on each site and on arbitrator machines, uses the Raft consensus algorithm to agree which site holds a ticket, and so decides where resources may run; an arbitrator plays the role of a quorum-only node when the number of sites is even [src: clusterlabs-docs ec787e8ea1597995]. Red Hat's configuration chapter gives an example with two clusters, each with its own floating address, and an arbitrator at a third location, with commands to show Booth's configuration and state [src: rhel-10-docs 35737e9e95462ce9]. The same material warns that Booth ticket state can persist in the cluster configuration after a ticket is removed, which can freeze or fence nodes unless it is cleaned up with `pcs booth ticket cleanup` [src: rhel-10-docs 271fed9d737f9bc7].

## Current state

**Designing from objectives.** Write down, per service: RTO, RPO, dependencies (identity, DNS, certificates, secrets, networks, license servers), data location, and the owner. Draw the dependency order of recovery, because the directory service and DNS come back before the applications that need them. Then choose the strategy that meets the numbers at acceptable cost. Backups must reach a place that does not share the failure: another site, another account, ideally immutable or offline so that ransomware cannot encrypt them. The transfer of backup data to an alternate site has to be fast enough to match the objectives; the control states the transfer period and rate as a function of recovery time and recovery point [src: nist-sp800-53 b502f9be0ef7c815].

**Recovery clusters in Pacemaker.** The Red Hat chapter on disaster-recovery clusters sets out the rules: the DR site must itself be a cluster, so the same tools and procedures apply; the primary and DR clusters are created by independent setup commands; resources and data must be configured so that synchronisation and failover are possible; node names must differ between the sites; and the `hacluster` user must be authenticated for every node of both clusters from the node where the commands run [src: rhel-10-docs bb42d35ade9a1d30]. The configuration only lets one view the status of both clusters, with a note that it neither sets up resources nor replicates data, so those remain manual work [src: rhel-10-docs 06095eabac440ed3]. The consequence is clear: tools orchestrate; the data path is the engineer's responsibility.

**Isolating data loss.** Some disasters are logical, not physical: corruption or deletion spreads through replication. The Red Hat guidance for directory servers is an example of the principle: on data loss, cut the damaged servers out of the replication graph at once so the harm cannot spread, and rebuild them as fresh copies cloned from servers that are still sound [src: rhel-10-docs 6499489017f6bf46]. The same rule applies to databases and file stores: stop replication from carrying damage, then restore from a clean source.

**Kinds of drill.** Drills should grow in realism over time. A **tabletop exercise** walks through a scenario on paper: who is called, what is decided, what is the order of steps; it finds gaps in roles and contact lists at almost no cost. A **component test** checks one capability: restore last night's backup to a scratch host and compare checksums; fail over a database standby; fail over a cluster by putting a node in standby, as the ClusterLabs tutorial does by stopping the cluster services on the node holding the address and watching the resource move [src: clusterlabs-docs 0244827199177cc1]. A **restore sampling test** takes a sample of backup data and restores selected functions to confirm they work, which is the stated purpose of the backup control's sampling enhancement [src: nist-sp800-53 11b67359634f87c1]. A **full site failover** moves real production traffic to the recovery site, runs there for a defined period, and returns (failback). A **chaos exercise** injects failures unannounced, within safety limits, to test detection and response as well as the plan.

**Running a drill.** The structure is stable: (1) *Objective and scope*: which services, which scenario, which success criteria in terms of RTO and RPO. (2) *Preparation*: approvals, communication to stakeholders, backout plan, observers, a freeze on unrelated changes, and a clock. (3) *Execution*: follow the runbook exactly, recording each step and its time, and note every deviation or improvisation. (4) *Validation*: not just "the service starts" but a checklist of functional tests, data checks (row counts, latest transaction time, checksums), integration tests with dependants, and monitoring. (5) *Failback*: return to normal and verify that the primary is again authoritative; failback is often harder than failover because the roles have swapped and data has diverged. (6) *Review*: measured RTO and RPO against targets, findings with owners and dates, and updates to runbooks and automation. A drill that is not reviewed is wasted.

**Evidence and metrics.** Keep records: date, scenario, participants, timeline, measured recovery time, measured data loss, defects found, and fixes verified in the next drill. Track the **restore success rate** and **time to restore** per backup set, the age of the oldest verified backup, and the share of services with a tested plan. Auditors and customers ask for this evidence, and so does the next engineer.

**Automation.** Everything that can be scripted should be: building the recovery environment from code (the provisioning course), restoring from backup, switching DNS or addresses, running validation tests. Automated testing, which the control explicitly recommends because it covers more issues with more realistic environments and stress [src: nist-sp800-53 d21eee504a3d162c], allows frequent small drills such as a nightly restore test of a random backup to a disposable environment. Infrastructure as code makes the recovery site reproducible; configuration management makes servers consistent; immutable images make rebuilds quick.

**People and communication.** Define roles (incident commander, communications lead, technical leads), a decision authority for declaring a disaster and for failback, contact trees that work when email is down, and a status page or channel on separate infrastructure. Credentials and runbooks must be available when the primary site is not: an offline copy, a vault that is itself replicated, and break-glass accounts that are tested.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

**The good.** Regular drills convert unknown risk into known numbers, shorten recovery, expose hidden dependencies, build confidence and are required by most compliance regimes. Small, frequent, automated tests cost little and catch most defects.

**The bad.** Drills take time and carry real risk when they touch production; they compete with project work; business owners often resist because of the downtime. Realistic drills need a prepared environment, which costs money. Success in a drill does not prove success in a real event, which may involve missing staff, damaged communications or simultaneous failures.

**The ugly.** The untested backup: a restore fails because encryption keys were stored only on the failed system, because a tape or object store was never verified, or because the backup job had been silently failing for months. The circular dependency: the recovery runbook lives on the wiki that lives on the site that burned. The plan that works only with the one person who knows the passwords. The failback that overwrites newer data. The "paper plan" that satisfies an audit and nothing else.

**Decision rules.** Set RTO and RPO per service with the business, then choose the cheapest strategy that meets them. Test restores more often than you test failover. Keep at least one backup copy that an attacker with administrator rights cannot delete. Run a drill at least yearly for each critical service, and more often for automated parts, and treat each drill as a project with a report.

**Assumptions.** Terms and control statements follow NIST SP 800-53 as stored in the project corpus; command names and chapter structure follow the cited Red Hat and ClusterLabs material for current releases; the ladder of DR strategies and the drill taxonomy summarise common industry practice and public cloud guidance and are marked where uncertain (to verify) [web: nist.gov] [web: aws.amazon.com].

## Applications beyond this domain

DR thinking applies to any critical system: payroll, hospital records, industrial control, SaaS products. Chaos engineering extends drills into continuous practice. Compliance regimes (financial regulations, healthcare privacy rules, ISO 22301 on business continuity) require tested plans. Software teams rehearse the same ideas as rollback and failover drills, and security teams as incident-response exercises, with the ransomware scenario merging the two.

## Examples

**Example 1: a restore test.** A weekly job restores the latest database base backup and archived logs to a disposable host, runs a set of queries, compares the latest transaction time with the production value, and records the elapsed time against the RTO.

**Example 2: a ticket-based multi-site failover.** Two clusters, each with an Apache resource group and a floating address, and an arbitrator at a third site; the Booth ticket moves to the DR site when the primary site is lost, and the ticket constraint starts the resource group there [src: rhel-10-docs 35737e9e95462ce9].

**Example 3: a bare-metal restore with ReaR.** Create the rescue image and backup, boot the image on spare hardware, let it recreate the disk layout and restore files, then check services and compare package and configuration drift (see the lifecycle course) [src: rhel-10-docs 5f1b7326bd94962f].

**Example 4: a tabletop.** Scenario: ransomware encrypted the file servers and the backup server's administrator account was used. Questions: who declares the incident, which backups are immutable, where are the clean images, how are users told, how long to rebuild the identity service?

**Example 5: a drill report.** Target RTO 4 hours, measured 7 hours 20 minutes; target RPO 15 minutes, measured 40 minutes because log shipping paused during a certificate expiry; findings: expired replication certificate, missing DNS record at the DR site, runbook step out of order; actions with owners and a retest date.

**Worked failure: the key in the vault that burned.** A company encrypted all backups and stored the key in a secrets manager that ran only in the primary data centre. A power and cooling failure took the data centre offline for days. The backups were intact at the off-site location but could not be decrypted until the vendor of the secrets manager restored an old copy of its own storage. Recovery took nine days against an RTO of one. A drill whose scenario forbade any use of the primary site while restoring from the off-site copy would have found it.

## Future direction

Expect recovery to become more code-driven: environments rebuilt from pipelines and data restored from immutable stores on a schedule, with drills as continuous tests rather than annual events. Expect regulators to ask for evidence of tested recovery, not of plans. The essential habit stays the same: measure recovery, do not assume it.
