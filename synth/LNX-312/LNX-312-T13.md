# Routing to the host: BGP between cluster nodes and top-of-rack switches

## Define the problem

A cluster network built on overlays hides itself from the physical network. Pod addresses exist only inside tunnels, load-balancer addresses are announced by ARP on one segment, and the switches see nothing but UDP packets between node addresses. That is convenient for the platform team and awkward for everyone else. The network team cannot see which workload sent a flow, cannot filter by Pod range, cannot send traffic for a service address to the nodes that can actually serve it, and has to treat the cluster as an opaque blob. Meanwhile large layer-2 domains, the usual way of making addresses mobile between hosts, carry their own risks: broadcast storms, spanning-tree convergence, limited scale and a failure domain that grows with every rack.

Modern data-centre fabrics solve this by routing everywhere. Each rack has a top-of-rack switch, and the link between a server and its switch is a routed link, not a switched segment. The server runs a routing daemon, tells the switch which addresses live on it, and learns the routes to everything else. Pod ranges, service addresses and node addresses all become ordinary prefixes in the fabric's routing table. The protocol that has settled in this role is BGP, used in its external form between devices in different autonomous systems.

This slice is an introduction at the lowest depth in the course, with a hardware-oriented lab. It explains the design of a routed fabric, shows how the Linux side of the session is configured with FRR or with a CNI's own BGP speaker, lists what the network team will ask for, and gives a method for testing on a switch, a real one if available or a Linux box that plays the part otherwise. It builds directly on the MetalLB slice, which showed the cluster side of the session in a lab setting.

## History and approaches

The traditional enterprise network put servers on VLANs that stretched across racks, with spanning tree preventing loops and a pair of aggregation switches routing between VLANs. As clusters grew, the model's limits showed: the size of the broadcast domain, the dependence on the aggregation pair, and the difficulty of moving an address between racks without stretching a VLAN even further. Large web companies moved first, to a Clos topology of leaf and spine switches with routing on every link and equal-cost multipath across spines. An informational RFC from 2016 documented one such design in which BGP is the only routing protocol, with each rack assigned its own autonomous system number and the spines sharing another, and gave the reasoning: BGP scales well, its policy tools are rich, its behaviour under failure is predictable and its operations tooling is mature. Interior gateway protocols such as OSPF and IS-IS compete for the role and remain common inside smaller networks and for the underlay in some designs.

On the server side, the first practical way to speak BGP was to run a general routing daemon such as Quagga, then BIRD or FRR, on the host. Network plugins and load-balancer add-ons then built in their own speakers. Calico used BIRD, kube-router embedded GoBGP, MetalLB used a native speaker and then FRR, and Cilium's early BGP support used the MetalLB library before a dedicated BGP control plane was written with its own custom resources. The Cilium documentation still describes the earlier approach of running BIRD beside the agent but marks it as deprecated and pointing at the newer control plane [src: cilium-docs 5c728e0b12edb4ff].

Two technical ideas made the design more practical. The first is BGP unnumbered. Configuring a unique IP address on both ends of every server link and entering the neighbour address in configuration is tedious and error-prone at the scale of thousands of servers. With the extended next-hop capability, a BGP session can run over the IPv6 link-local address of the interface and still carry IPv4 prefixes, so a server needs no per-link address and the switch discovers the neighbour from router advertisements. The second is the pairing of BGP with fast failure detection (BFD) and graceful restart, so that sessions drop in under a second on a real failure and do not drop at all when only a routing daemon is restarted.

Failures and partial successes accompany every migration. Some early designs used one shared AS for all racks and needed workarounds such as allowing a path to carry the local AS or relaxing the path comparison in multipath. Teams that enabled BGP without prefix filters learned that a node announcing the wrong prefix could attract traffic fabric-wide. Teams that expected the switch to know what a Pod was found that a routing session describes prefixes, not workloads, so everything above that, from identity to policy, still lives in the cluster.

## Current state

**Fabric design.** A typical design has leaf switches in each rack and spine switches above them, with every leaf connected to every spine. Servers connect to one or two leaves. Each leaf runs eBGP to the spines and to its servers. The common assignments give each rack its own private AS number from the 16-bit private range or the 32-bit private range, and the servers in a rack share a rack AS different from the leaf's, or each server gets its own, depending on the operator's preference. The spines announce nothing except what they learn, and the leaves accept from servers only the prefixes that servers are allowed to announce, namely the node's own loopback, its Pod range and the service ranges assigned to the cluster.

**Server side.** The server announces the addresses it hosts. With a CNI in native routing mode the node announces its Pod CIDR, so the fabric can route to Pods directly. With a load-balancer feature the node announces service addresses with the /32 form so that the fabric spreads traffic across nodes that have endpoints. The Cilium BGP control plane describes this with a small set of resources: a cluster configuration with node selectors and the list of peers, a peer configuration with timers, graceful restart and address families, an advertisement that says which prefixes to announce (Pod CIDRs, Service addresses of given types, or selected objects), and an override resource for node-specific settings. Auto-discovery of the local address and, in some cases, the peer address reduces per-node configuration [src: cilium-docs 8c7720b6c73cc4c4]. For hosts that are not Kubernetes nodes, or for clusters whose plugin has no speaker, FRR on the host does the same job with a few dozen lines of configuration.

**Timers and failure behaviour.** The documented failure scenarios show the points that matter in operation: what the fabric sees when an agent is down, restarting or being upgraded. With graceful restart enabled on both sides, the switch keeps forwarding to the node's routes for a configured time while the agent restarts, so rolling upgrades do not cause packet loss; without it, routes are withdrawn at the start of the restart and traffic is lost until the session is re-established. The same document explains the risk in the other direction: if the agent is down for longer than the restart time, the switch withdraws the routes, and Pods on the node become unreachable even though they are running [src: cilium-docs 83ff33a18738dc97]. BFD shortens detection of link and node failure to the order of a second; its timers should be agreed with the network team because aggressive values on a busy CPU cause false positives.

**ECMP and hashing.** The switch spreads flows over the equal-cost paths by hashing header fields. A fabric that hashes on the full five-tuple distributes load evenly; one that hashes on addresses only maps each pair of hosts to a single path. Equal-cost multipath requires the switch to accept several next hops for the same prefix, which is a configuration setting, and in BGP it requires the paths to be equal in the comparison unless the multipath relax option is used for routes with different AS paths of equal length. An ECMP section of the earlier BIRD documentation explains the same idea from the server side, for hosts that have two upstream switches [src: cilium-docs 10b51eb26003b831].

**Policy and security.** The switch should treat the servers as untrusted. Standard protections are an inbound prefix list per neighbour that accepts only the server's allowed prefixes, a maximum-prefix limit that shuts the session if a server announces too many routes, MD5 or TCP-AO authentication of the session where supported, filtering of AS-path length, and communities that tag routes for further policy. The Generalised TTL Security Mechanism limits the session to directly connected neighbours. Servers should accept only a default route or the routes the workload needs, not the entire fabric table, since the host rarely needs every prefix and the kernel's memory and the daemon's CPU are better spent elsewhere.

**Tooling on the switch.** The switch operating systems in common use include Linux-based network operating systems with FRR (so the configuration looks like the server's), traditional vendor systems with their own syntax, and open ones such as SONiC. The lab on a Linux box mimicking a switch uses the same daemon on both ends, which is useful for learning the protocol even when the production switch is a vendor device. The commands are the same in spirit everywhere: show the neighbour summary, the received and advertised routes per neighbour, the forwarding table with all next hops, and the BFD sessions.

**Operating the connection.** Useful alerts are the number of established sessions, the number of routes per neighbour against the maximum, flapping rates, BFD session state and the difference between what the cluster believes it announces and what the switch has received. A runbook covers the failure cases: a session that will not establish (authentication, timers, AS number, reachability), a session that establishes but carries no routes (filters or address families), and routes that exist but do not carry traffic (policy, MTU, ECMP).

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

**The good.** Routing to the host gives the network team and the platform team a single language, prefixes. Pod and service addresses become visible and filterable in the fabric, load balancing is real ECMP with no layer-2 coupling, failover is fast and overlays disappear along with their MTU arithmetic. The same design serves virtual machines, bare-metal workloads and Pods. Operations tooling for BGP is mature, and so are the skills.

**The bad.** It needs a cooperating network team, an addressing plan that allocates prefixes to nodes and services, and switches that support the needed features. Vendors differ in the support for unnumbered sessions, in multipath options and in the number of ECMP paths. Debugging is split between two teams and two sets of tools. The cluster's identity-based policy does not carry across, so the fabric sees prefixes while the cluster sees identities, and security policy has to be consistent at both levels.

**The ugly.** The worst incidents are route leaks and table overflows. A server announcing a prefix it does not own attracts traffic that it cannot serve. A cluster that announces a /32 for every Pod can exceed the switch's forwarding table, which is much smaller than the routing table of a general-purpose router, and the failure is a silent drop of routes beyond the limit. A restart of all agents at once without graceful restart empties the fabric of the cluster's routes. And a mistake in an inbound filter on the switch, applied to all neighbours at once, can disconnect a whole rack of nodes from their own Pods.

## Applications beyond this domain

Routing to the host is used for far more than Kubernetes. Anycast services such as public DNS resolvers and content-delivery nodes announce the same service address from many sites and let BGP pick the nearest. Virtualisation platforms use BGP with EVPN to distribute virtual machine addresses and layer-2 segments across a fabric. Edge sites and small data centres run FRR on Linux routers for transit and peering. Host-based BGP also underlies several cloud networking products. The same configuration patterns, prefix lists, maximum-prefix limits, BFD and graceful restart, carry across all of them.

## Examples

A first example is a two-node test with a Linux machine acting as the switch. The machine has two interfaces with routed links to the nodes, runs FRR with a listen range or two neighbours, and accepts only the nodes' Pod prefixes. Each node announces its Pod range. The operator checks the neighbour summary, sees two prefixes received, pings a Pod on one node from the other through the fake switch, and then stops the daemon on one node while watching the route disappear after the hold time or the BFD detection time.

A second example is a production checklist for a new rack. The network team assigns an AS number, a range for node loopbacks and a range for Pod CIDRs. The platform team configures the peers and advertisements, sets graceful restart, and agrees BFD timers. A pre-production test confirms that the switch accepts only the allowed prefixes by announcing a deliberately wrong prefix from one node and checking that the session is shut or the route filtered. A second test drains a node and verifies that traffic moves without loss.

A third example is a diagnosis. After a rolling upgrade, some Pods are unreachable from outside the cluster for a minute at a time. The switch's neighbour log shows sessions dropping at each restart, because graceful restart was enabled on the node but not on the switch. Enabling it on the switch, with a restart time longer than the agent restart, removes the gaps.

## Related problems and ideas

The topic connects to the MetalLB slice (T10), which supplies the cluster-side configuration, to the CNI comparison (T03), in which routed designs were weighed against overlays, to MTU (T12), since removing overlays removes the arithmetic, and to multi-cluster (T11), where routed reachability between sites is the usual way to meet the node-to-node requirement. The virtualization course returns to EVPN and to virtual machine addressing. The security course covers the trust boundary between servers and the fabric.

An idea worth carrying forward is that routing is the cheapest and most scalable form of mobility. If addresses are routed, then moving a workload means announcing its address from a new place, and the fabric does the rest. Overlays and layer-2 extensions are ways of faking that property on a network that lacks it.

## Active research

Work in this area includes better automation of fabric configuration so that servers and switches are configured from one source of truth, EVPN-based designs that extend to the host, segment routing for traffic engineering, hardware offload of the forwarding plane on smart network cards, faster convergence techniques, and signed routing data (RPKI) for external peering. The CNI projects continue to expand their BGP resources and to coordinate with load-balancer add-ons. Details of feature support differ by vendor and release and are "to verify" against the documentation of the exact switch and plugin in use.

## Future direction

Expect the routed host to become the default in on-premises clusters of any size, with unnumbered sessions, BFD and graceful restart as standard and with the CNI managing the speaker as part of the node's lifecycle. Platform teams should learn enough routing to review a configuration and read a neighbour summary, and network teams enough Kubernetes to know what a node announcing a Pod range means.
