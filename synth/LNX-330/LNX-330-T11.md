# A real upstream review cycle: from a first fix to a merged commit

## Define the problem

The earlier topics in this course teach the parts: the release process, configuration, a test machine, build checks, patch mail, tests and debugging. A newcomer still faces the hardest step, which is to combine them into a real contribution that strangers accept. The difficulty is less technical than social and procedural: finding a task that is wanted, matching a subsystem's habits, surviving criticism, answering reviewers who are busy and arriving at the right moment in the release cycle.

This topic describes how a first upstream change usually unfolds, what each stage costs in time and attention, and where beginners lose patches. It is deliberately about practice, so its lab is a rehearsal of the judgement involved, and the real exercise is a live contribution that the learner makes outside the course.

## History and approaches

**Gatekeepers and the open door.** The kernel has always accepted patches from outsiders, with maintainers acting as filters. The volume has grown to many thousands of changes per release from well over a thousand developers (counts are published after each release by LWN and others, to verify the latest figures), so the filter has become a reviewing workload more than a gate. Reviewer time is the scarce resource.

**Staging and entry points.** For newcomers the tree has historically offered lower-barrier places: the `drivers/staging` area for code not yet up to standard, documentation fixes, and cleanup tasks. The community has argued over the value of trivial cleanups as a first step: they teach the mechanics but burden maintainers if they are numerous, so many subsystems prefer fixes of real defects or work on actual hardware the contributor owns.

**Mentoring programmes.** The Linux Foundation's mentorship programme, Outreachy internships and Google Summer of Code projects place beginners with experienced developers for a few months, and the kernelnewbies community provides a guide and a mailing list. These help because the first series is much easier with someone to review it privately before the public list sees it.

**Bug-driven contributions.** Automated bug finders (syzbot, which runs the syzkaller fuzzer continuously, and other bots) publish reports with reproducers. These offer concrete, verifiable tasks: reproduce, find the cause, fix, and test with the reproducer. Fix patches carry a `Reported-by:` tag for the bot and often a `Fixes:` tag. Kernel bugzilla and mailing lists provide other leads.

A first contribution is usually a small patch, and the history of how contributors get started explains why. The community has long advised beginners to begin with cleanups in drivers and staging, and to review others' work, because these tasks allow learning the process at low risk. The process has not changed in essentials: write the change, check it, send it, respond to review, and send a revised version. What does change is the speed of tooling and the volume of mail.

## Current state

### The stages

1. **Choose the target.** Prefer something you can test. A bug you can reproduce in a virtual machine, a driver for hardware you own, or a documentation correction you verified against the code. Read the subsystem's `MAINTAINERS` entry, its recent patches on the list archive and any subsystem-specific documentation about how it takes patches (some have tree branches, private rules on commit messages or scheduling).
2. **Reproduce and understand.** Build the tree the maintainers use (often their own repository, or `linux-next`, rather than the latest stable), reproduce the problem and read enough of the code to explain it in plain words. Use the debug tools of the previous topics.
3. **Make the smallest correct change.** Follow the coding style and surrounding idioms. Add a test if the subsystem has a place for one. Write the commit message so that someone who has never seen the bug understands the cause, the effect and why the fix is right.
4. **Check before sending.** Build with warnings, run checkpatch and sparse, boot-test, run relevant selftests, and if possible compile on a second architecture or configuration.
5. **Send to the right people.** Use the patch workflow from the earlier topic, with maintainers and reviewers in To and lists in Cc. A cover letter introduces a series; a single patch needs no cover.
6. **Wait and watch.** Mail is asynchronous. A first reply commonly arrives within a few days if the maintainer is active; weeks of silence are common during a merge window or in a busy or understaffed subsystem. A polite single reminder after a couple of weeks is accepted.
7. **Review and revise.** Reviewers ask questions, request changes or reject the approach. Read every comment as a technical request, reply publicly, and group your changes into one revised series after waiting a day or more so that other time zones can comment. Carry forward approvals only for patches that did not change.
8. **Acceptance.** The maintainer applies the patch to a subsystem tree and may send a short note. The change shows up in `linux-next`, then in the pull request to the mainline at the next merge window, and finally in a release. For a fix tagged for stable, it also appears in stable updates after reaching mainline.
9. **Afterwards.** Respond to regression reports about your change promptly. Your name stays on the commit; later maintainers may contact you about it.

### How a review looks

A typical first-round review contains a mix of five kinds of comment:

- **Structure**: split the patch, reorder, explain better. Usually fix.
- **Style nits**: whitespace, naming, comments. Fix, and run checkpatch.
- **Real defects**: a leak, a race, a missing error path. Fix, and consider adding a test.
- **Out-of-scope ideas**: improvements that belong in another series. Acknowledge and defer.
- **Noise**: impatience, off-topic remarks. No action, or a brief polite answer.

Approvals arrive as trailers (`Reviewed-by`, `Acked-by`, `Tested-by`). The kernel's documentation explains what each means: a `Reviewed-by` is a statement that the reviewer examined the patch and found no objection, a stronger claim than an acknowledgement from a maintainer of related code. A maintainer's silence is not approval, and a maintainer's "looks fine" in the thread is usually followed by applying the patch.

### Timing and rules of thumb

Patches sent during a merge window (two weeks after a release) may wait until the next window, because maintainers' trees are closed to new material. Fixes can go in at any time. A common recommendation is to base new work on the maintainer's tree, state it with `--base`, and avoid sending patches over a weekend or holiday if a quick answer is wanted. Do not send the same patch to many maintainers separately. Do not send a revision before addressing earlier comments, and do not repeat resend after resend without changes.

### Failure modes and how to recover

- **No response.** Check that the address list matches `get_maintainer.pl`, that the mail reached the archive, and that the patch applies to the maintainer's tree. Ping politely once, then ask a related reviewer or the list.
- **Rejection of the approach.** Ask what the reviewer would accept. A rejection is information; many contributors' second approach is merged.
- **A conflict between reviewers.** Summarise the disagreement in your reply and ask the maintainer to decide; the maintainer has the last word for the subsystem.
- **A regression you caused.** Answer quickly, revert if needed, and learn from it. The project values contributors who clean up after themselves.
- **Burnout.** One merged patch is a real success. Pick a small first step.

### The generalised pattern: the cycle repeated until accepted

An upstream review is a loop. A patch is posted, reviewers reply with questions and requests, the author answers and sends an updated version, and the process repeats until the maintainer accepts or the idea is dropped. The generalised lesson is that the review is a conversation about the change, not a verdict on the author. Replies should be prompt, polite and specific; each revision should say what changed; and disagreement should be argued with evidence. Contributors who treat criticism as information, not as an attack, finish this loop more often.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

**The good.** The process is open and the standards are written. A good small fix with clear reasoning is welcome from anyone, and review teaches more than any course. The record is public, so a contributor builds a track record that outlasts any employer.

**The bad.** The workflow is slow and demanding, and feedback can be terse. Subsystems differ in speed and expectations. Some maintainers are overloaded, so patches sit unanswered. The bar for code quality is high, and a first patch commonly goes through two or three versions.

**The ugly.** Occasional harsh or dismissive replies, though the code of conduct and a more open culture have reduced them. Patches that never get a reply, with no explanation, discourage beginners. Companies sometimes push low-value patches to increase their contribution statistics, which burdens reviewers and affects how newcomers' patches are received.

## Applications beyond this domain

- **Other open-source projects** (QEMU, Git, GCC, many others) use mailing-list review with similar norms; QEMU's guide stresses testing before sending and using the list rather than a tracker or forum [src: qemu-docs 6fd701aa9ba56dc9].
- **Professional code review** shares the same skills: small changes, clear descriptions, responsiveness and receiving criticism.
- **Hiring and careers**: a public, reviewed contribution is a lasting credential in systems software.
- **Standards bodies and RFC processes** have analogous consensus-seeking, written, public discussion.

## Examples

**A bot report turned into a fix.** A beginner picks a syzbot report with a reproducer for a null dereference in a filesystem helper. They reproduce it in QEMU, find an unchecked return value, write a three-line fix with `Reported-by` and `Fixes:` tags and send it to the maintainers and list. The maintainer asks for a clearer commit message, the patch goes in as v2 and is applied two weeks later.

**A rejected cleanup.** Someone sends a patch that renames variables across a driver to satisfy a tool. The maintainer declines because it creates churn and merge conflicts for pending work. The contributor asks what the maintainer would like instead and takes a real bug from the maintainer's list.

**A series restructured.** A feature series with a rename and a fix mixed in one patch is asked to be split. The fix is taken for the current cycle, and the rest goes through two more rounds.

**An approval carried over.** A reviewer approves patch 2 of two. Between versions only patch 1 changes. The v2 series keeps the `Reviewed-by` on patch 2 and drops nothing else.

### Opposing approaches

Not every change is accepted, and the opposing outcomes deserve attention. A patch can be rejected because the idea conflicts with the subsystem's direction, because the problem should be solved elsewhere, or because it adds maintenance cost greater than its benefit. Silence is also a common outcome, since maintainers are busy; a polite reminder after a reasonable wait is accepted practice. Forking or maintaining a patch out of tree is the fallback, with the costs described earlier in the course. A realistic expectation is that several revisions and a few weeks are normal.

## Active research

Studies of the kernel's contribution process examine reviewer load, time-to-first-response and the effect of tooling and mentoring on newcomer retention. Experiments include bots that triage patches, tooling to turn forge pull requests into mailed series, and structured dashboards of patch state. Findings are discussed at the Maintainers Summit and in academic venues on open-source software engineering (to verify specific results before citing).

## Future direction

Expect more automation around the edges (build and test results posted automatically, trailers collected by tools, bots that route patches) while the central practice of public review by humans stays. The pool of reviewers is the long-term constraint, so projects will continue to look for ways to train and retain them.
