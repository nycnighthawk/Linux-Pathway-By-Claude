# Limiting lateral movement: segmentation and mutual TLS between workloads

## Define the problem

A Kubernetes cluster's network is flat by default. Any Pod can open a connection to any other Pod and to any Service, in every namespace, and most clusters never change that. A single compromised container, whether through a vulnerable web framework, a poisoned dependency or a stolen credential, therefore starts with network reach to the database, the internal admin interfaces, the metrics endpoints that expose configuration, the kubelet ports on the nodes and the API server. The attacker's usual next steps are to scan for reachable services, try default passwords, reuse service credentials found in environment variables and exploit unauthenticated internal APIs that developers assumed were "inside the perimeter". The term for this movement from one foothold to the next is lateral movement, and the flat network is what makes it cheap.

Two distinct controls address it, and they answer different questions. Segmentation decides which workload may open any connection to which other workload: it is a reachability question, enforced on packets by the network layer using network policy. Mutual TLS (mTLS) establishes who sits at the other end and keeps third parties from reading or changing the traffic: it is an identity and confidentiality question, enforced on connections by cryptography, either in the application, in a sidecar proxy, or in the node's data plane. A cluster with only segmentation relies on IP addresses and labels, which an attacker with network access inside an allowed segment can abuse by spoofing or by compromising a permitted peer. A cluster with only mTLS lets every workload attempt connections everywhere and depends entirely on correct authorisation in each service.

The slice covers the threat model, how Kubernetes NetworkPolicy implements default-deny segmentation and what its limits are, how workload identity is issued and verified (certificates, SPIFFE identities, service accounts), how service meshes and kernel-level data planes deliver mTLS and identity-based authorisation, transparent node-to-node encryption as the alternative, and the operational issues that cause outages. The mechanics of overlay networks, CNI plugins and mesh data planes belong to the container networking course; the focus here is the security design and what to verify.

## History and approaches

The first segmentation tools were host and network firewalls. In a traditional data centre, subnets and VLANs separated tiers, and firewall rules between them were written by IP address and port. Containers broke the assumption that IP addresses mean anything: addresses are allocated dynamically, change on every reschedule and are reused. Kubernetes introduced the NetworkPolicy API in 2016 as a way to say, in terms of labels and namespaces, which Pods may talk to which. The policy selects Pods in a namespace, and lists allowed ingress sources and egress destinations by Pod label, namespace label or address block, with ports and protocols. If any policy selects a Pod for a direction, only traffic allowed by some policy for that direction is permitted; with no policy, everything is allowed. The API does not enforce anything by itself: the network plugin must implement it, and several do not [src: kubernetes-docs d73efe3dcfd75727].

Within a few years the limits of the API showed. It is allow-only, with no explicit deny or priority, so teams build patterns around default-deny policies per namespace plus additive allows. It has no concept of cluster-wide policy, so every namespace needs its own default-deny. It matches layer 3 and 4 only, so no rules by DNS name, HTTP path or method, and it has no identity beyond labels. Plugin vendors extended it: Calico with global network policies and ordered rules, Cilium with identity-based policy, DNS-aware egress and L7 rules for HTTP, Kafka and gRPC. The Kubernetes network policy working group has added admin-level policy resources (AdminNetworkPolicy and BaselineAdminNetworkPolicy) to give cluster operators precedence over namespace owners; their maturity is to verify.

Encryption and identity came from a different direction. Early microservice platforms such as Netflix's stack and later Google's Istio project observed that every service team was implementing TLS, certificate rotation and service authentication differently and badly. Linkerd (2016) and Istio (2017) moved those functions into a sidecar proxy that is injected into each Pod: it intercepts the application's inbound and outbound traffic, establishes mutual TLS with the peer's proxy using a certificate issued to the workload, and enforces authorisation policy on the verified identity. The certificate carries an identity derived from the Pod's service account, in the SPIFFE format (spiffe://trust-domain/ns/<namespace>/sa/<service-account>). The control plane acts as a certificate authority and rotates certificates frequently, typically every day or less [web: spiffe.io].

SPIFFE (the Secure Production Identity Framework for Everyone) and its reference implementation SPIRE generalised this: a standard identity document (SVID, as an X.509 certificate or a JWT), a workload API through which a process obtains its identity without secrets baked into its image, and attestation: the node and workload prove what they are to the identity server, using the platform's own facts (for Kubernetes, the service account token and Pod metadata verified through the API). The same identity can then be used across clusters, clouds and virtual machines. Meshes can be configured to take identities from SPIRE.

Sidecars carry costs: each Pod gets an extra container, with memory and CPU overhead; injection must happen before the application starts; the extra hops add latency; upgrades require restarting every workload; and the proxy sees plaintext, adding a place for compromise. Responses moved the data plane lower. Istio's ambient mode splits the roles: a per-node proxy (ztunnel) handles L4 mTLS and identity for every Pod on the node, and optional per-namespace waypoint proxies provide L7 policy where needed. Linkerd stays sidecar-based with a very small Rust proxy. Cilium implements encryption and identity in the kernel data plane using eBPF, with transparent encryption of node-to-node and Pod-to-Pod traffic through WireGuard or IPsec, and a separate beta feature for mutual authentication, in which the agents authenticate the workload identities using SPIFFE before allowing a flow while the traffic is carried on the already encrypted tunnel [src: cilium-docs d1319397200614d4] [src: cilium-docs 59475b9110f802e0]. The node-level designs raise a question the sidecar did not: the identity is attested per workload, but the encryption terminates at nodes, so the guarantee is weaker against an attacker on the node.

The emerging pattern: default-deny segmentation as the baseline in every namespace, workload identities issued automatically with short lifetimes, mutual authentication enforced by a data plane that sits as close to the workload as the threat model needs, and authorisation expressed over identities rather than addresses.

## Current state

**Segmentation with NetworkPolicy.** The baseline is a default-deny policy in each namespace: a policy with an empty Pod selector and the Ingress and Egress policy types, with no rules, selects every Pod and permits nothing in that direction [src: kubernetes-docs d73efe3dcfd75727] [src: kubernetes-docs de8b190f4a77af83] [src: kubernetes-docs 4756580df879d916]. Allow rules are then added per application: the web tier accepts traffic from the ingress controller's namespace on its port; the API tier accepts only from the web tier; the database accepts only from the API tier; and every Pod must be allowed egress to cluster DNS or nothing resolves. Egress to external addresses is allowed narrowly, by address block where possible, or by DNS name using the plugin's extension. Namespace labels (kubernetes.io/metadata.name) allow selecting by namespace. Policies are additive, so a later accidental allow cannot be revoked by another policy. Hosts and node-level traffic (kubelet probes, which come from the node) have exceptions that depend on the plugin and must be tested.

**What policy does not do.** It does not authenticate. A Pod that can spoof a permitted source IP, or any Pod in a permitted label set, passes. The API does not control traffic from outside the cluster beyond what plugins add for node ports and load balancers, does not cover host-network Pods in most plugins, and gives no visibility: a denied flow is silent unless the plugin logs it. Hubble in Cilium, Calico flow logs and cloud flow logs provide that, and they are essential for building policy from observed traffic rather than guesses.

**Identity and certificates.** In a mesh, each Pod's proxy obtains a short-lived certificate from the mesh control plane by presenting its service account token, which the control plane validates through the API. The certificate's URI SAN holds the SPIFFE ID. Trust is anchored in a root or intermediate certificate authority, which can be the mesh's built-in CA or an external one (cert-manager with an enterprise CA, Vault, SPIRE). Rotation runs automatically; the root changes rarely and needs a managed procedure with an overlapping trust bundle. Outside meshes, cert-manager issues certificates for Services and applications that terminate TLS themselves, and the application is responsible for client verification.

**Mutual TLS enforcement.** In Istio, PeerAuthentication sets the mode per namespace or workload: PERMISSIVE accepts both plain text and mTLS, which allows migration, and STRICT accepts only mTLS. AuthorizationPolicy then allows or denies by verified source principal, namespace, HTTP method, path and headers, with a default-deny pattern similar to NetworkPolicy. Linkerd enables mTLS automatically between meshed Pods and uses its own Server and AuthorizationPolicy resources. In Cilium, a policy rule can require mutual authentication for a flow, so that the agents verify identities before the traffic is allowed.

**Transparent encryption without identity.** WireGuard or IPsec between nodes encrypts all Pod traffic without sidecars or application changes, which protects against network sniffing and tampering on the underlay. It authenticates nodes, not workloads, so it is not a substitute for identity-based authorisation, but it is cheap and uniformly applied, and in some regulated environments it satisfies a requirement for encryption in transit on its own.

**Operational checks.** Test every policy from the outside: the connection must fail from a disallowed Pod and succeed from an allowed one. Use dry-run or audit modes where available (Cilium policy audit mode, Istio's permissive mode with telemetry showing which flows are still plain text) before turning enforcement on. Track certificate expiry and CA rotation dates as monitored quantities. Include health checks and monitoring agents in the allow list, and keep DNS egress, which is the most common cause of mysterious breakage after default-deny.

**Generalised pattern.** Authorise on identity, not location; issue identities automatically and make them expire; enforce at the lowest layer that you can observe and test; default deny and add exceptions deliberately; observe real flows to write rules.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

The good: default-deny network policy is cheap and breaks the easy lateral moves of an intruder. It encodes the intended architecture of an application in a reviewable file. Mesh mTLS gives each connection confidentiality, integrity and a verified peer identity without any application change, and replaces long-lived shared secrets between services with certificates that expire in hours. Identity-based authorisation survives rescheduling and scaling, which address-based rules do not.

The bad: policy writing is laborious and policy drift is constant, as applications change their dependencies. Teams copy a permissive allow-all rule to make an outage stop and forget it. NetworkPolicy semantics are subtle (policies are additive and the empty selector means "all"; an empty rule list means "nothing"), and plugins differ in how they treat host traffic and named ports. Meshes add a substantial amount of complexity: control plane upgrades, injection webhooks, protocol detection issues for non-HTTP traffic, resource overhead, and failure modes that look like application bugs. Certificates and CAs are a new class of production outage when expiry is unmonitored. Strict mTLS breaks traffic from non-meshed clients, including the kubelet's HTTP probes unless the mesh rewrites them, and jobs that start before their sidecar.

The ugly: false assurance. A namespace can contain "default deny" and an allow rule that selects every Pod in every namespace, which makes the policy a no-op, and nobody notices without testing. In sidecar meshes a workload can bypass the proxy in several ways (traffic that is excluded by annotation, host networking, direct pod IP access in permissive mode), so mTLS exists on paper while a plain-text path remains. Identity depends on the service account: if several workloads share the default service account, the mesh identity is meaningless, and RBAC and mesh policy both degrade. Anyone who can create Pods in a namespace can run them as any service account there, which means the namespace, not the individual workload, is the real identity boundary. Beta features such as Cilium mutual authentication have documented limitations, among them gaps in what traffic is covered, and should not be treated as complete until the project says so. Ingress is another seam: the edge terminates TLS and re-originates traffic, so identity does not carry from the client through the ingress to the service unless explicitly propagated.

## Applications beyond this domain

Zero trust architecture, as described by NIST and applied in corporate networks, rests on the same two ideas: no implicit trust from network location, and authentication and authorisation on every request using strong workload and user identities. BeyondCorp at Google applied this to employee access, and the service identity counterpart (ALTS inside Google's data centres) is the ancestor of SPIFFE. Microsegmentation products in virtualised data centres apply identity-based firewall rules to VMs. Cloud virtual networks offer security groups and private service connections, which are the same policy model at VPC scale. Mutual TLS is also the base of device and IoT authentication (client certificates) and of database and message broker access in regulated industries. Short-lived certificates from an internal CA are the same pattern as SSH certificates replacing authorized_keys files.

## Examples

**Default deny rollout.** A team enables flow logging, collects two weeks of observed traffic per namespace, and generates a first policy set from it. They apply default-deny in audit or log-only mode where the plugin supports it, review what would have been dropped, add the missed legitimate flows (a batch job's weekly call, a monitoring scraper) and enable enforcement namespace by namespace, beginning with the least critical. The CI pipeline then includes a test that tries a forbidden connection from a test Pod and expects it to fail.

**mTLS migration.** A platform team installs a mesh and sets PERMISSIVE across the cluster. Telemetry shows which service-to-service calls still use plain text, mostly from a legacy job runner and a metrics scraper outside the mesh. After onboarding them, namespaces move to STRICT one at a time, with an AuthorizationPolicy that allows only the expected source principals. The database's namespace is first, since it has the clearest callers.

**A failing example.** An administrator applies a default-deny egress policy to a namespace on a Friday. Pods can no longer resolve names, and the policy blocks the webhook calls that admission relies on for the same namespace's Pods. Readiness probes pass but applications cannot reach their dependencies, and a rolling update stalls. The rollback is to delete the policy, and the lasting fix is a standard allow rule for DNS and for required platform services included in the template used for every namespace, tested in staging before it is applied.

## Related problems and ideas

Neighbouring problems: ingress and egress gateways for traffic that crosses the cluster edge, encryption of data at rest, authenticating users to workloads (end-user JWT validation in the mesh), and securing the control plane's own traffic. Alternatives to a mesh: libraries in the application that implement mTLS (gRPC with credentials, or SPIFFE helper libraries) which avoid a proxy at the cost of per-language work; and the node-level encryption approach described above. Newer ideas: eBPF-enforced identity without proxies, workload identity federated across clusters and clouds through SPIRE, post-quantum key exchange in service meshes as TLS libraries adopt hybrid algorithms, and policy generation from observed traffic with human review. Some projects explore capability-based designs where a service holds an explicit, unforgeable reference to each peer it may call, which replaces ambient network reach.

## Active research

Open topics include safe, automatic generation and maintenance of network policy from observed flows, with research on false allow and false deny rates; formal verification of policy sets, checking that no path exists from an internet-facing workload to a sensitive one; and performance of node-level and kernel-level encryption. On the identity side, work focuses on stronger attestation (binding a workload identity to hardware or to a verified image), on revocation for short-lived credentials, and on the interplay of ambient mesh designs with sandboxed runtimes. Standards work in the Kubernetes network policy group covers admin policies, FQDN and egress rules, and port ranges. Open questions: how much trust to put in node-level proxies compared with per-workload sidecars, how to keep policy understandable at hundreds of services, and how to present flow evidence to auditors.

## Future direction

Expect admin-level and cluster-wide network policy to become standard, ambient and eBPF-based meshes to take share from sidecars, and identity (SPIFFE-style) to reach beyond a single cluster into VMs and serverless. Default-deny templates and policy tests will be part of namespace provisioning. Maturity and release details for the features named above (AdminNetworkPolicy, ambient mesh status, Cilium mutual authentication, WireGuard modes, SPIFFE federation): to verify against project documentation, since several were beta during preparation of this text.
