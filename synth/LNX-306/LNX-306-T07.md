# Out-of-band management: IPMI, Redfish and how to practise without hardware

## Define the problem

A server that is powered off, hung, or without an operating system cannot be managed by anything running on it. Yet an administrator must still be able to turn it on, see its console, change what it boots from, read its hardware health and update its firmware, often from another continent. The component that makes this possible is the **baseboard management controller** (BMC): a small computer on the server's motherboard, with its own processor, memory, firmware, network port and power supply from standby power. It watches sensors (temperatures, fans, voltages), controls power and reset, provides a remote console (text through serial-over-LAN and graphical through KVM-over-IP), exposes virtual media (a remote ISO that appears as a local drive), and logs events. It is the foundation of every bare-metal provisioning platform described in the previous topic, and of fencing in high-availability clusters, of firmware lifecycle tools and of remote hands in data centres.

Two protocol families expose a BMC. **IPMI** (the Intelligent Platform Management Interface, from 1998) is a binary protocol, usually over UDP port 623, with a command-line client (`ipmitool`) found on every administrator's machine. **Redfish**, from the DMTF (2015), is a RESTful interface using HTTPS and JSON, designed to replace IPMI and to cover much more: inventory, firmware update, BIOS settings, accounts, certificates, telemetry and event subscriptions. Most current BMCs offer both, with vendor names such as iDRAC (Dell), iLO (HPE), XClarity Controller (Lenovo), Supermicro's IPMI and OpenBMC-based controllers.

The practical problems are two. First, the skills have to be learnt without a rack of servers; this is why the topic title says "simulated": the protocols can be practised against emulators (QEMU models of BMC chips, a mock Redfish service, `sushy-emulator` and `virtualbmc` for libvirt guests). Second, BMCs are a security weak point: they are always on, they run old embedded software, they have powerful capabilities (power, console, firmware flash, virtual media), they sit on a network that is often poorly protected, and their default passwords are well known. A compromised BMC is a compromised server, below the operating system and invisible to its tools.

Typical failure modes: BMC credentials left at vendor defaults; IPMI over LAN with weak cipher suites or null authentication enabled; the BMC network reachable from user networks or the internet; firmware that is years out of date; BMC clocks wrong so that event logs are useless; a script that sends `power cycle` to the wrong host because inventory and BMC addresses disagree; and automation that assumes every vendor implements the same Redfish resources and breaks on the first model that does not.

## History and approaches

IPMI 1.0 was published in 1998 by Intel, Hewlett-Packard, NEC and Dell; version 1.5 added LAN access, and 2.0 in 2004 added encryption (RMCP+) and serial-over-LAN. Its design is a set of commands addressed to the BMC by network (RMCP and RMCP+ over UDP 623) or by in-band access from the host operating system through a kernel interface. On Linux the in-band path uses the `ipmi_msghandler`, `ipmi_devintf` and `ipmi_si` modules and the device `/dev/ipmi0`; the Rocky Linux IPMI guide describes loading these modules and troubleshooting a missing `/dev/ipmi0`, including checking BIOS or UEFI settings that hide the interface [src: rocky-docs 17af261265b4285e] and [src: rocky-docs 43e76297512087a5]. The common out-of-band tool is `ipmitool`; FreeIPMI offers an alternative with commands such as `ipmipower`, `ipmi-sensors`, `ipmi-sel`, `bmc-config` and `ipmiconsole`, which the same guide lists [src: rocky-docs d0f33ef7b1d1ae63].

IPMI's weaknesses became widely known in the 2010s. Dan Farmer's 2013 analysis of IPMI security described flaws that remain structural: the RAKP authentication of IPMI 2.0 lets a remote party obtain a salted hash of any user's password for offline cracking; a "cipher suite 0" allows sessions with no authentication or encryption; passwords are stored in the BMC in recoverable form on many implementations; and the protocol is UDP with limited auditing. Several BMC vulnerabilities and attacks since (affecting products from various vendors) have shown the consequences. For this reason current guidance is to disable IPMI over LAN where Redfish is available, or to restrict it to a dedicated management network with strong passwords and cipher suite 17 (the strongest standard one) or equivalent (to verify per vendor).

**Redfish** appeared in 2015 from the DMTF's Scalable Platforms Management Forum, with the aim of a modern, secure, schema-defined interface that a normal HTTP client can use. A Redfish service root is at `/redfish/v1/`; from it, collections link to `Systems` (the computer as the operating system sees it: power state, boot settings, processors, memory, BIOS), `Chassis` (the physical enclosure: sensors, power, thermal), `Managers` (the BMC itself: network, logs, virtual media), `AccountService`, `SessionService`, `UpdateService`, `EventService`, `TelemetryService` and others. Resources are JSON documents with `@odata.id` links, `Actions` (such as `#ComputerSystem.Reset` with its `ResetType` allowable values) and ETags for concurrency. Authentication is HTTP Basic over TLS or session tokens obtained by posting to the `SessionService` (`X-Auth-Token` header). Schemas are published in a public bundle so that clients can validate and generate code, and the DMTF supplies a conformance test tool.

**OpenBMC** is the open-source BMC firmware project (started by Facebook, IBM, Google, Microsoft and Intel, and now hosted by the Linux Foundation), based on the Yocto build system, with `bmcweb` serving Redfish and the phosphor daemons managing hardware; it allows hyperscale operators and OEMs to use auditable firmware. QEMU includes machine models for BMC SoCs (Aspeed AST2500 and AST2600 boards used by OpenBMC, Nuvoton boards and others) and for IBM PowerNV machines with a simulated BMC, which lets the BMC firmware run on a laptop; the QEMU documentation lists these board families and their boot options [src: qemu-docs c546a50373a2929b], [src: qemu-docs de6d2fb9409d1184] and the PowerNV page covers BMC configuration for that platform [src: qemu-docs f7e3ddb5ce141847].

The approaches to practising without hardware follow from this. *Run a BMC firmware under QEMU* (OpenBMC for the Aspeed machine types) to see real firmware, which is slow but realistic. *Emulate the protocol in front of a libvirt VM*: **VirtualBMC** exposes IPMI for a libvirt domain, so that `ipmitool power on` starts the VM; **sushy-emulator** (from the OpenStack `sushy-tools` project) exposes a Redfish service for libvirt or other virtual machine back ends, with virtual media and boot device control. *Write a tiny mock service* (a few dozen lines of Python) that mimics the resources a script needs, which is quick for testing automation logic and is what the lab for this topic does.

## Current state

**IPMI with ipmitool.** Common out-of-band commands (`-I lanplus` selects IPMI 2.0; `-H`, `-U` and `-P` or `-E` (password from an environment variable) or `-f` (password file) name the target):

```
ipmitool -I lanplus -H bmc01 -U admin -E chassis power status
ipmitool -I lanplus -H bmc01 -U admin -E chassis power cycle
ipmitool -I lanplus -H bmc01 -U admin -E chassis bootdev pxe options=efiboot
ipmitool -I lanplus -H bmc01 -U admin -E sol activate
ipmitool -I lanplus -H bmc01 -U admin -E sdr elist
ipmitool -I lanplus -H bmc01 -U admin -E sel list
ipmitool -I lanplus -H bmc01 -U admin -E mc info
```

`chassis bootdev` with `options=persistent` makes the boot device permanent; the default is one-time, which is what provisioning needs. Serial-over-LAN, shown in the Rocky guide's common operations, gives a console that survives a reboot and is the standard way to watch installers [src: rocky-docs 90c4aaa25a46b6e3]. Do not put passwords on the command line (they appear in the process list); use `-E` with `IPMI_PASSWORD` or a password file with mode 0600. In-band commands (`ipmitool` with no `-H`, via `/dev/ipmi0`) can read sensors and configure the BMC network from the running OS, which is how a fleet's BMC addresses are set after installation (`ipmitool lan set 1 ipsrc static`, `ipaddr`, `netmask`, `defgw`, and `user set password`).

**Redfish with curl.** A session with Basic authentication over HTTPS:

```
curl -s -u "$U:$P" https://bmc01/redfish/v1/Systems/1 | jq '.PowerState, .Boot'
curl -s -u "$U:$P" -X PATCH -H 'Content-Type: application/json' \
  -d '{"Boot":{"BootSourceOverrideTarget":"Pxe","BootSourceOverrideEnabled":"Once"}}' \
  https://bmc01/redfish/v1/Systems/1
curl -s -u "$U:$P" -X POST -H 'Content-Type: application/json' \
  -d '{"ResetType":"ForceRestart"}' \
  https://bmc01/redfish/v1/Systems/1/Actions/ComputerSystem.Reset
```

Resource paths vary: the system may be `Systems/1`, `Systems/System.Embedded.1` (Dell), `Systems/Self` (OpenBMC) or a UUID; discover them by following links from the service root (`Members` of `Systems`), as an automation library does. Allowed values come from the resource itself, in annotations such as `BootSourceOverrideTarget@Redfish.AllowableValues` and `ResetType@Redfish.AllowableValues`; read them and do not hard-code. Use a TLS CA that you control (install your CA in the BMC and verify it) and avoid `-k` except in labs. Sessions (`POST /redfish/v1/SessionService/Sessions`) return a token to use instead of re-sending the password. Libraries: `sushy` (Python), `redfish` (python-redfish-library), `gofish` (Go), `redfishtool` and Ansible's `community.general.redfish_*` and `redfish_command` modules.

**Boot control and provisioning.** The boot override is the primary link to the network boot topic: set a one-time PXE or HTTP boot target, reset the system, and the machine boots the installer once and returns to its disk afterwards. The `BootSourceOverrideMode` (`UEFI` or `Legacy`) must match the firmware mode of the installer. Redfish also supports `UefiTarget` for specific entries and `HttpBoot` as a target in newer schemas (to verify per vendor), and VirtualMedia (`InsertMedia` action with an image URL) for installers delivered as ISOs; this works where the BMC can reach the image server and is the basis of many "no PXE" deployments, including those that boot a bootc ISO.

**Inventory, firmware and settings.** Redfish exposes processors, memory, storage controllers, network adapters and PCIe devices, which feed the inventory. `UpdateService` with `SimpleUpdate` (the image URI is given in the request) or multipart push updates firmware, and the same service lists the current firmware versions of every component, which is how a fleet's firmware drift is checked. BIOS settings appear under `Bios` with a pending-settings resource that applies at the next reboot. The details differ widely by vendor: DMTF profiles and the vendors' documentation define which properties exist. Automation should be written against capabilities discovered at run time, with per-vendor adapters.

**Fencing in clusters.** High-availability clusters use the BMC as a fence device: the cluster manager powers off a failed node before taking over its resources, so that two nodes never write to the same storage. Pacemaker fence agents (`fence_ipmilan`, `fence_redfish`, `fence_idrac`, `fence_ilo`) call the BMC, and the Cluster Labs documentation shows configuring a fencing device and an example configuration [src: clusterlabs-docs f7368bf0474fff13] and [src: clusterlabs-docs 505e85d94dc42c84]. The high-availability course returns to this; here the relevance is that the BMC network's reliability and security are part of the cluster's safety, and that a fence call needs the right host mapping.

**Security baseline.** (1) Put BMCs on a dedicated management network with no route from user or internet networks; reach it through a bastion or VPN. (2) Change all default credentials and use per-host or per-rack unique passwords generated and stored in a vault; rotate on a schedule. (3) Disable what you do not use: IPMI over LAN if Redfish suffices, cipher suite 0 always, anonymous accounts, Telnet and old SNMP, web interface features such as legacy Java KVM. (4) Install a trusted TLS certificate (many BMCs support CSR generation and import through Redfish). (5) Update firmware as part of the patch rings described in the packaging course (BMC firmware is a campaign, with a rollback plan: a failed BMC flash can render the BMC unusable). (6) Forward BMC logs (system event log, audit) to central logging and synchronise time (NTP). (7) Restrict who may do what: operators who need power control do not need firmware flash. (8) Include BMCs in vulnerability scanning with an appropriate profile.

**Practising without hardware.** Four options, in increasing realism. A mock service in a few lines of Python tests a script's logic. `sushy-emulator` or `virtualbmc` in front of libvirt VMs gives realistic power, boot-device and virtual-media behaviour for provisioning tests and is how Ironic, MAAS and Foreman integrations are tested in CI. QEMU's Aspeed machine models run OpenBMC images for a real Redfish and IPMI stack. A cheap physical BMC or a single-board device (Raspberry Pi with a USB serial and power relay running OpenBMC-like tools) trains the physical side. The lab for this topic uses the first option.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

**The good.** BMCs make remote operation of physical machines practical and are indispensable for provisioning and fencing. Redfish is a modern, discoverable, schema-based interface with good libraries, and OpenBMC provides an auditable alternative. The same tools work in emulation, so automation can be tested in CI. Standard commands (power, boot device, console, event log) are portable even across vendors.

**The bad.** IPMI is old and weak, and many fleets still depend on it. Redfish implementations differ in resource names, supported actions and bugs; DMTF profiles help but do not remove vendor quirks. BMC firmware quality is uneven, updates are slow and risky, and the BMC is a black box next to the operating system. Management networks are often forgotten in security reviews. Virtual media and remote KVM have long latency and plugin troubles on some products.

**The ugly.** Internet-exposed BMCs with default passwords are a standing source of compromises; cipher suite 0 and RAKP hash disclosure turn a reachable IPMI port into credential theft; a script that resets the wrong machine takes down a production server; and a firmware update that bricks a BMC means a datacentre visit. A shared password for all BMCs means one leak opens everything.

**Assumptions.** Protocol details (ports, cipher suites, resource names) follow the IPMI and Redfish specifications as commonly implemented and the cited guides; features such as HTTP boot targets and update methods vary by vendor and firmware version (to verify).

## Applications beyond this domain

The pattern of an always-on, separately powered management plane appears in laptops (Intel AMT and similar), network switches (management ports), storage arrays, blade chassis, GPUs and DPUs, and cloud providers' underlay control. Redfish's model of resources, actions and schemas is used for storage (Swordfish), for composable infrastructure and for data-centre power and cooling devices. Automation lessons transfer: discover rather than hard-code, authenticate every call, keep the management network separate, and test against an emulator.

## Examples

**Example 1: provisioning with a one-time PXE boot.** An orchestration script reads the host's BMC address and credentials from the vault, sets the one-time PXE boot override, issues a forced restart, opens serial-over-LAN to capture the installer log, and when the installation callback arrives, verifies that the boot override has cleared.

**Example 2: IPMI emulation for a lab.** `vbmc add vm01 --port 6230` and `vbmc start vm01` expose a libvirt VM, and `ipmitool -I lanplus -H 127.0.0.1 -p 6230 -U admin -P password chassis power status` answers for it; MAAS and Foreman can then treat the VM as bare metal.

**Example 3: a Redfish emulator in front of libvirt.** `sushy-emulator --port 8000` serves `/redfish/v1/Systems/<uuid>`; `curl` can set a one-time PXE boot and reset the system; a Foreman or Ironic test environment points at it.

**Example 4: firmware inventory.** A script walks `UpdateService/FirmwareInventory`, records component versions in the inventory and reports hosts below the approved level.

**Example 5: an IPMI security review.** `nmap -sU -p 623 --script ipmi-version,ipmi-cipher-zero 10.20.0.0/24` from the management network lists BMCs that accept IPMI 2.0 and any that allow cipher zero; the result goes into the patch campaign.

**Worked failure: the wrong host.** An inventory import swapped two BMC addresses after a rack was recabled. A reinstall script sent a forced restart and a PXE override to what it believed was a test machine but was a production database. The fix was a safety check: before any destructive BMC action, read the system's serial number (Redfish `SerialNumber` or IPMI `fru`) and compare it with the expected asset; refuse if they differ.

## Related problems and ideas

This topic supplies the power and console actions that **bare-metal platforms** and **network boot** depend on, and it connects forward to **secure wipe and decommissioning** (some drives support wipe through the BMC or storage controller), to **drift detection** (firmware versions), to **fencing in high-availability clusters** and to **monitoring** (sensors, event logs). It touches **secrets management** (BMC credentials), **network design** (management VLANs), **vulnerability management** and **firmware supply-chain security**.

## Active research

Open areas include secure and measured BMC firmware (signed images, hardware roots of trust such as OCP's Caliptra and DICE-based attestation), reducing the BMC's attack surface, standard telemetry streaming, Redfish extensions for accelerators and composable systems, and open firmware (OpenBMC) adoption outside hyperscalers. Researchers also study BMC-based attacks on cloud bare-metal services and mitigations at the platform level.

## Future direction

Expect Redfish to be the only management interface on new servers, expect attested BMC firmware and signed updates to become standard, and expect fleet tools to treat the BMC as a first-class managed device with its own lifecycle and patch rings. For the operator, the lasting practices are to isolate and authenticate the management plane, discover resources instead of hard-coding them, verify the identity of the target before any destructive action, and test automation against emulators before touching hardware.
