# Smart-card login on Linux with SSSD and certificate mapping

## Define the problem

A password is a shared secret that can be guessed, phished, reused, typed over a shoulder and copied from a keylogger.
Organisations that must meet strong-authentication requirements (government and defence, finance, healthcare, anyone
under a mandate for two-factor administrative access) need credentials that live in hardware and cannot be
exported: a private key generated on a smart card or a USB token, unlocked by a short PIN that is useless without
the device. Logging in then means proving possession of the key by signing a challenge, and the system must
decide who the certificate belongs to.

Linux has to solve four separable problems to support this. The hardware has to be reachable: a reader and a
card speak a protocol (CCID over USB, ISO 7816 at the card), and a daemon has to broker access. Applications need a
uniform way to use the keys, which is the PKCS#11 API. The system has to validate the certificate against a trust
chain and revocation status. And it has to map a certificate to an account: which user does a given subject name, or a given serial number and issuer pair, correspond to? The last problem is where most deployments fail. Failure looks
like a card that works for the browser but not for `login`, a PIN prompt that appears at the screen lock but the wrong
user is chosen, a card that is rejected because the intermediate CA is missing from the trust store, or a lockout
because three wrong PINs blocked the card.

This slice covers the stack from reader to PAM, the SSSD configuration that makes a card a login factor, the
certificate mapping rules that tie certificate to account in IdM and AD, Kerberos PKINIT as the route to single
sign-on after card login, and the checks to prove each layer. It is a supervised, manual lab: hardware (or a
virtual card) is required.

## History and approaches

Cryptographic tokens reached Unix in the late 1990s. The first Linux support was MUSCLE (Movement for the Use of
Smart Cards in a Linux Environment), producing `pcsc-lite`, the PC/SC resource manager daemon (`pcscd`), and drivers
for readers, and the OpenSC project (2001) which supplied a PKCS#15 and PKCS#11 implementation for common cards.
Login integration came through `pam_pkcs11` (2004), which asked a PKCS#11 module for the certificate on the card,
verified it against CA certificates in a directory, and mapped it to a user with mapper modules (by subject, by
`uid` extracted from an attribute, by login in an LDAP lookup, by Kerberos principal and others). It worked in lab
and in some government deployments, notably the United States DoD Common Access Card and the PIV cards defined by
FIPS 201, but configuration was a pile of text files per machine, revocation checking was fragile, and it was not
integrated with directories or offline caching. Debian and Fedora later dropped it as unmaintained.

SSSD supplied the modern route. Smart-card support appeared in SSSD 1.15 (2017) with a `p11_child` helper that talks
PKCS#11, and the setting `pam_cert_auth = True`. The mapping is no longer per host files but rules: in IdM, a
certificate can be added to the user entry (`ipa user-add-cert`), or a **certificate mapping rule** can match
certificate fields to directory attributes; in AD, the `altSecurityIdentities` attribute and the userPrincipalName
SAN are used. The mapping tooling is built into the system: `sssctl cert-show` and `sssctl cert-eval-rule` evaluate a
certificate against a matching and mapping rule and print the LDAP filter that results
[src: rhel-10-docs 8c081e6941dbb28f]. Red Hat's smart-card guide describes `authselect` features to enable smart-card
login in a controlled way, including options to require the card exclusively, to allow the card plus passwords, and to lock the session when the card is removed
[src: rhel-10-docs b87972d4036696d6].

Windows approaches shaped the expectations: the interactive logon with a smart card and PKINIT with `Kerberos
with certificates`, whose AD enforcement rules for strong certificate mapping (KB5014754, May 2022, with
full enforcement scheduled after a transition) changed how certificate-to-account bindings must look (to verify the
dates). Linux deployments against AD must tolerate the strong-mapping requirement, which is why the SID extension and
the explicit `altSecurityIdentities` formats matter. Alongside smart cards, the hardware-token world moved to FIDO2
and passkeys; SSSD and GDM added passkey and external identity provider paths as a technology preview in RHEL 10
documentation [src: rhel-10-docs dd6930f945d0e450] [src: rhel-10-docs 1eef1033105b4f5a].

The pattern: a standard key-use interface (PKCS#11), a daemon to arbitrate hardware (pcscd), an identity service that
owns the mapping (SSSD with the directory), and Kerberos PKINIT to exchange the card proof for a ticket.

## Current state

**Hardware and drivers.** The card sits in a CCID reader (or is a USB token that presents as one). `pcscd` (socket-activated
by `pcscd.socket`) must run, with polkit rules allowing the active user to use it. `pcsc_scan` shows the reader and
card ATR. The PKCS#11 module is `opensc-pkcs11.so` for most PIV, CAC and generic cards, or a vendor module (Yubico's
`libykcs11`, SafeNet, Thales). `p11-kit` registers modules: `p11-kit list-modules` shows which tokens are visible
and `/usr/share/p11-kit/modules/` holds the configs. Inspect tokens with `pkcs11-tool --list-slots`, `--list-objects`,
and `pkcs15-tool --list-certificates`, or for a YubiKey PIV applet `ykman piv info`. Generating a key and certificate
on the token is a CA-side exercise: the key is created on the card (`pkcs11-tool --keypairgen`, or `ykman piv keys
generate`), a CSR is signed by the CA and the certificate is written back (`ykman piv certificates import`).

**SSSD configuration.** In `sssd.conf` `[pam]` section: `pam_cert_auth = True`. Optional controls: `pam_p11_allowed_services`
to limit which PAM services (`login`, `sshd`, `gdm-smartcard`, `su`, `sudo`) offer card authentication; `p11_child_timeout`,
`p11_wait_for_card_timeout` to wait for insertion; and in `[sssd]` or per-domain `certificate_verification` with
`no_ocsp`, `soft_ocsp`, `no_verification` to tune revocation checking. Trust anchors go in
`/etc/sssd/pki/sssd_auth_ca_db.pem` (a PEM bundle) and the `ca_db` option in `[sssd]` or `[pam]`. For IdM clients,
`ipa-advise config-client-for-smart-card-auth` outputs a script that does these steps; Red Hat's documentation
covers the server and client set-up with the `ipa-certupdate` and `authselect` steps. Enable the system pieces with
`authselect select sssd with-smartcard` for card plus password, `with-smartcard-required` to demand a card for every
login (hardening, risk of lockout), and `with-smartcard-lock-on-removal` for session lock on card removal; the
documentation notes the options and the care needed before making a card mandatory [src: rhel-10-docs b87972d4036696d6].
PAM, as the manual page explains, is the dynamically configured module framework that applications such as `login` and
`su` defer to, so enabling a card is a matter of arranging which modules appear in the service stacks
[src: man-pages b135896b81be1aea].

**Mapping the certificate to a user.** The central design decision. In IdM, three approaches exist:

1. *Certificate stored in the user entry.* `ipa user-add-cert alice --certificate="$(base64 -w0 alice.der)"`. SSSD finds the
   user by comparing the whole certificate. It is simple, but rotation requires adding the new certificate before
   the old one expires.
2. *Certificate mapping rule* for identically structured certificates: `ipa certmaprule-add smartcard_rule
   --matchrule='<ISSUER>CN=Example CA' --maprule='(userCertificate;binary={cert!bin})'` or, for
   user-by-subject, `--maprule='(ipaCertSubject={subject_dn})'`. Matching rules choose which certificates the rule
   handles (by issuer, EKU, key usage), and mapping rules produce an LDAP filter from certificate fields via
   placeholders.
3. *Mapping to a Kerberos or UPN attribute* from a SAN: `(krbPrincipalName={subject_principal})`, which suits PIV
   cards carrying a UPN.

For AD the same placeholders build `altSecurityIdentities` filters with issuer and serial or subject key identifier,
and the Red Hat example evaluates a certificate with `sssctl cert-eval-rule` against a rule using
`altSecurityIdentities=X509:<I>...<SR>...` and tells the administrator to add the printed value to the user's attribute
in AD [src: rhel-10-docs 8c081e6941dbb28f]. The `sssd.conf` equivalents are `[certmap/<domain>/<rule>]` sections with
`matchrule`, `maprule`, `domains` and `priority` for non-IdM directories.

**Login flow.** At `login`, `gdm` or `sshd` with `ssh -I`, SSSD's PAM responder starts `p11_child`, which enumerates the
card certificates and filters by the matching rules; the user (or the service) selects one, SSSD looks up the
user, asks for the PIN, and has `p11_child` sign a challenge. Verified, the PAM stack completes. With IdM and Kerberos, the
`krb5` provider then acquires a TGT by PKINIT (`pkinit_identities = PKCS11:module_name=opensc-pkcs11.so`,
`pkinit_anchors` on the client side, and PKINIT enabled on the KDC, which IdM sets up with its CA), giving single
sign-on for later `ssh` and web access without further PIN prompts. SSH itself can use the card directly:
`ssh -I /usr/lib64/opensc-pkcs11.so host` or `PKCS11Provider` in `ssh_config`, with the public key published
through `sss_ssh_authorizedkeys` or from `ssh-keygen -D`. Graphical logins use the GDM smart-card plug-in and the
"passwordless" modes that RHEL 10 documents as technology preview, including passkey and external IdP choices
[src: rhel-10-docs dd6930f945d0e450].

**Verification steps, layer by layer.**

1. `pcsc_scan`: reader and card visible.
2. `pkcs11-tool --module /usr/lib64/pkcs11/opensc-pkcs11.so -L`: token present; `-O` lists objects; `--login --test` checks
   the PIN.
3. `openssl verify -CAfile /etc/sssd/pki/sssd_auth_ca_db.pem card-cert.pem`: the chain verifies.
4. `sssctl cert-show <base64>` and `sssctl cert-eval-rule`: SSSD would match the certificate and produce this filter.
5. `getent passwd` or `sssctl user-checks alice -a auth -s login`: the PAM stack accepts.
6. `journalctl -u sssd` plus `p11_child.log` at debug level 7 or higher.
7. `klist` after login: PKINIT TGT present (`kinit -X X509_user_identity=PKCS11:...` to test PKINIT alone).

The IdM web UI also supports certificate login (the browser presents the certificate to the server, which authenticates
the user without a password) for the management interface
[src: rhel-10-docs c9d46b2aff6f8889].

**Generalised pattern.** Hardware-bound private key; a challenge signed by the key; a verifier that checks the
chain and revocation; a mapping rule that is evaluated by the authority that owns identities, not by the machine; a
credential exchange (PKINIT) that turns a one-time card proof into a time-limited ticket.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

**Good.** Key material cannot be exfiltrated by malware on the host, and phishing a PIN is useless without the
card. Revocation by CA is fast and central. Smart-card login meets regulatory requirements for multi-factor
administrative access in a way that is auditable. With PKINIT, the card is used once and Kerberos provides SSO for the
session, so users do not enter the PIN every few minutes. SSSD's rule-based mapping is the same on desktops, servers
and the web UI.

**Bad.** The stack is long (reader, pcscd, polkit, driver, OpenSC or vendor module, p11-kit, SSSD, PAM, GDM or sshd, KDC) and
each layer has its own logs. Revocation checking needs OCSP or CRL access from every client, which fails on
isolated networks and turns into either slow logins or disabled checking. Certificates expire, and
renewal on the card means an operational process (user visits, remote re-enrolment). Mapping rules are easy to make
too permissive: a rule that matches a subject field alone can allow a certificate from a different CA that carries
the same name. Cards from different vendors need different modules, and driver packaging across distributions varies.
Remote access (ssh from a laptop) needs the card on the client, so jump-host patterns require PKCS#11 forwarding
(`ssh-agent` with PKCS#11 provider, or `ssh -J`).

**Ugly.** `with-smartcard-required` on a machine whose only card reader fails leaves nobody able to log in, short of the
rescue boot; keep a break-glass local account or console method and test before enforcing. Three failed PIN attempts
lock the card, with PUK recovery that is a help-desk process. A VM without USB passthrough or with a flaky `pcscd`
socket gets intermittent "no card" results that look random. Time-zone and clock drift break certificate validity
checks and Kerberos at once. Cloud and container workloads cannot see a physical card. A wrong `certificate_verification`
setting (`no_verification`) quietly disables the entire security benefit and survives in configurations because it
fixed a test.

Mitigations: keep the mapping rules narrow (issuer plus a unique field), enable OCSP with a reachable responder or
use `soft_ocsp` knowingly, document PUK handling, keep a break-glass account that is tested, enrol two cards per
administrator, and monitor certificate expiry on cards with a report from the CA.

## Applications beyond this domain

PKCS#11 and hardware keys protect far more than logins: code signing (`osslsigncode`, `rpm --addsign` with an HSM), TLS
server keys in HSMs, GPG and age keys, SSH keys on tokens, disk unlock (`systemd-cryptenroll` with a PKCS#11 or FIDO2 token), and
certificate authority root keys. The mapping problem (which identity does this credential name?) recurs in mutual TLS
for service meshes, in SPIFFE ID mapping to RBAC, and in cloud workload identity. Hardware tokens with a PIN are the
user-facing variant of the TPM- and HSM-bound keys used for machine identity, and the same revocation and enrolment
issues recur there.

## Examples

**A working IdM smart-card login.** The IdM CA issues PIV-style certificates to YubiKeys. The administrator imports the CA
chain on every client (`ipa-certupdate`), runs the advice script from `ipa-advise config-client-for-smart-card-auth`, and
adds a mapping rule `--matchrule='<ISSUER>CN=Certificate Authority,O=EXAMPLE.COM'` with `--maprule='LDAPU1:(userCertificate;binary={cert!bin})'`.
Alice's certificate is added to her user entry. At the login screen, she inserts the key, enters the PIN, and `klist`
afterwards shows a TGT obtained by PKINIT. An `sssctl user-checks` run with `-a auth` proves the path from the console.

**A card with an AD certificate.** An AD CA issues the certificates. The Linux client (joined as in LNX-301-T04) needs
a certificate mapping section with `maprule = (altSecurityIdentities=X509:<I>{issuer_dn!ad_x500}<SR>{serial_number!hex_ur})`,
and the AD team must populate `altSecurityIdentities` on each user with the value that `sssctl cert-eval-rule` prints.
After that, the same card works on Windows and on Linux.

**Failing: chain incomplete.** The login rejects the card with "certificate not trusted". `openssl verify` says
`unable to get local issuer certificate`; the intermediate CA was not added to `sssd_auth_ca_db.pem`. Adding the
intermediate and restarting SSSD fixes it.

**Failing: wrong user selected.** Two certificates on the same card match two accounts (a personal and an admin
account). The login picks the first. Narrow the matching rules by key usage or EKU, or give the admin account its own
card and rule priority.

**Failing: lockout after enforcement.** `authselect select sssd with-smartcard-required` is applied fleet-wide, and a
batch of USB readers lacks the driver in the minimal image. Nobody can log in on those hosts. Recovery is console access
with the break-glass account or a rescue boot to run `authselect select sssd`. The lesson is to enforce per host group
after a canary and to keep an out-of-band path.

## Related problems and ideas

Alternatives: FIDO2 and passkeys with `pam_u2f` (local key file) or SSSD's passkey support, which uses the same
hardware convenience without a PKI; one-time passwords in IdM (`ipa otptoken-add`), which are weaker but cheap;
Kerberos with PKINIT for services; short-lived SSH certificates issued after an OIDC login (no card at all);
and Windows Hello for Business, whose Linux equivalent is the device-bound credential in an agent. Related problems:
smart-card removal policy for shared kiosks, remote-desktop smart-card redirection (RDP, SPICE), and
PIV applet administration.

## Active research

Research and engineering focus on post-quantum signatures in PKCS#11 and X.509 (hybrid certificates for PKINIT and
TLS), on the integration of passkeys as first-class factors in SSSD and GDM, and on moving mapping rules and revocation
off the client with online services. Questions remain about the best user experience for enrolment and renewal across
multiple operating systems, and how to bind a login to the physical presence of the user in remote sessions.

## Future direction

Expect the card-based flows to coexist with passkeys and external IdPs in the same SSSD framework. RHEL 10 documentation
lists the GDM passwordless methods (external identity providers, passkeys) as technology preview, which will likely graduate
over minor releases (to verify per release) [src: rhel-10-docs 1eef1033105b4f5a]. Microsoft's strong certificate mapping
enforcement will keep pushing AD-based deployments toward explicit mappings. Watch OpenSC releases, SSSD release notes for
passkey and smart-card changes, and distribution defaults for OCSP behaviour.
