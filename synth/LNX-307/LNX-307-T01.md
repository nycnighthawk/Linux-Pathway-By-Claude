# Thinking methodically about performance: the USE and RED methods and the runbooks that capture them

## Define the problem

A slow or failing Linux service presents the same trap to almost everyone who meets it. There are hundreds of tools, thousands of counters and a strong urge to start typing commands. The usual result is the "streetlight" pattern: the engineer looks where the light is good, which means the tool they know best, and either finds a red herring or finds nothing. Another common result is the "random change" pattern, where tunables are altered until the symptom moves, and nobody can say afterwards what was wrong. A third is simple overload of the human: during an outage the person on call has to remember which twenty commands matter, and they do it at three in the morning.

Methodologies exist to replace that improvisation with a procedure. This slice covers two of them and the document that turns them into team practice. The **USE method** looks at machines and their parts: for every resource, check utilisation, saturation and errors. The **RED method** looks at request-driven services: for every service, check request rate, error rate and duration. A **runbook** is the written procedure that links a symptom or alert to the checks, the decision points and the actions, so that the method does not live only in one expert's head. The slice stops short of the tools that gather the numbers (the sysstat family, Prometheus, tracing), which later topics in this course take up; it concentrates on what to ask and in what order.

The gap being filled is one of structure. Counters tell you what a kernel is doing, and a dashboard tells you what happened, but neither says which question to ask next. A method is a cheap way to guarantee coverage (have I covered every resource) and to bound effort (how many checks rule a layer out).

## History and approaches

Performance work began as capacity planning on mainframes, where queueing theory (Erlang's telephone work, then Little's law in 1961) gave a mathematical frame: a resource has a service time, an arrival rate and a queue, and response time explodes as utilisation nears one. Those ideas are still the root of "saturation". On early Unix systems the practical equivalent was a small kit of commands, `vmstat`, `iostat`, `sar` and `top`, each printing a table of counters. The kit worked, but it said nothing about where to start, so skill was mostly apprenticeship.

In the 2000s and early 2010s Brendan Gregg, working at Sun Microsystems and later at Joyent and Netflix, wrote down the habits of the better analysts as named methodologies. The USE method was described in a 2012 ACM Queue article and again in his 2013 book on systems performance; it is summarised on his site as a rule to check utilisation, saturation and errors for every resource [web: brendangregg.com]. It sat beside anti-methods that he named for contrast: the street-light anti-method, the random-change anti-method and the blame-someone-else anti-method. The last of these is a reminder that organisational habits are part of the problem.

Meanwhile a different school grew out of web operations. Google's site reliability engineers described four **golden signals** for user-facing systems (latency, traffic, errors and saturation) in the book on monitoring distributed systems [web: sre.google]. In 2015 Tom Wilkie, then at Weaveworks and later at Grafana Labs, simplified this for microservices into the RED method: rate, errors and duration, with the argument that it can be applied uniformly to every service so that dashboards and alerts become templates (to verify the exact year and talk). RED deliberately drops saturation because a request-oriented service cannot always measure it from the outside; USE is its complement for the machines underneath.

The runbook has an older and separate lineage: operations manuals in telecommunications and mainframe shops, then the "standard operating procedure" of incident management. Its modern form follows from the incident-response practice described in Google's SRE material and the alerting guidance in Prometheus documentation, which says that alerts should link to dashboards and consoles that help find the faulty component [web: prometheus.io]. Several attempts to automate runbooks followed: run-the-script wikis, ChatOps bots and, more recently, "runbook automation" products. They partly succeeded, but all share a known failure mode: the document rots faster than the system changes.

The emerging pattern is a layering. A service-level method (RED) tells you whether users are hurt and which service is guilty. A resource-level method (USE) tells you which machine part is the bottleneck. A workload or code-level technique (profiling, tracing, covered in LNX-307-T05) tells you why. A runbook ties the layers to one named alert.

## Current state

### USE in detail

The method is a loop over a resource inventory. Resources are the physical or logical parts that perform work: CPUs, memory capacity, network interfaces, storage devices, storage controllers, interconnects, and also software resources that behave like them, such as thread pools, file descriptor tables and connection limits. For each resource you need three numbers.

- **Utilisation** is the fraction of time the resource was busy servicing work, or for capacity-type resources, the fraction used. A disk that spent 95% of an interval with requests in flight has 95% utilisation.
- **Saturation** is work that is waiting because the resource could not service it: the run queue for a CPU, the swap activity for memory, the request queue for a disk, the dropped or queued packets on an interface.
- **Errors** are counted events that signal a fault: corrected memory errors, disk I/O errors, interface CRC errors, retransmits that indicate loss.

Gregg's suggestion is to check errors first, because they are quick to read and rarely ambiguous, then saturation, then utilisation [web: brendangregg.com]. Saturation usually matters more than utilisation, because a resource at 100% with no queue is a good thing, while a resource at 60% with a deep queue is the signature of bursty load.

On Linux the mapping from resource to counter is the working knowledge of this topic. The following sketch uses commands that the next topic (LNX-307-T02) explains in depth.

| Resource | Utilisation | Saturation | Errors |
|---|---|---|---|
| CPU | `mpstat -P ALL 1` busy per core; `vmstat 1` us+sy | run queue (`vmstat` column `r`) above core count; load average; `/proc/pressure/cpu` | rare: machine-check events in `dmesg` |
| Memory | `free -m`, `/proc/meminfo` | swap-in and swap-out (`vmstat` `si`, `so`), page scan rates in `sar -B`, `/proc/pressure/memory` | OOM kills in the kernel log, ECC counters (`edac-util`) |
| Disk | `iostat -x` `%util` per device | `aqu-sz`/avgqu-sz and `await`, `/proc/pressure/io` | kernel log I/O errors, SMART |
| Network | `sar -n DEV` against link speed | drops, overruns, socket backlog (`ss -ltn`), `nstat` | `ip -s link` errors, `ethtool -S` |
| Tasks and files | `/proc/sys/fs/file-nr`, PID count | blocked-task counts (`vmstat` `b`) | EMFILE and EAGAIN in application logs |

The kernel's pressure stall information deserves a special mention. The `/proc/pressure/cpu`, `memory` and `io` files report, as averages over 10, 60 and 300 seconds, the share of time in which at least one task ("some") or all non-idle tasks ("full") were stalled on that resource, plus a cumulative microsecond total [web: docs.kernel.org]. They are the nearest thing Linux has to a direct saturation metric and they exist per cgroup too (`cpu.pressure`, `memory.pressure`, `io.pressure`), which makes them valuable in containers; Kubernetes now exposes them as node metrics [src: kubernetes-docs 901f411e192be4ed]. The cgroup interface and its limits are the subject of LNX-307-T08.

### RED in detail

For each service that handles requests, record three signals per time window: how many requests arrive (rate), how many fail (errors, usually as a ratio), and how long they take (duration, as a distribution, not an average). The distribution matters because a mean hides the tail; the usual presentation is percentiles or a histogram, from which Prometheus can compute quantiles. With a metrics system, the three signals become three queries on instrumented counters and histograms, covered in LNX-307-T03. Because the three are standard, a team can generate one dashboard per service from a template.

RED answers whether a user is hurt and which service is guilty. USE answers which resource is the culprit. The two chain: a RED alert on checkout latency leads to the dependency graph, then to the slow service's host, then to a USE pass over that host.

### Runbooks

A good runbook is the method made concrete for one alert. It states the alert and why it matters, the user-visible impact, the first checks in order (with the exact commands and what each outcome means), the decision to escalate or act, the safe mitigation steps and their rollback, and a link to where the owners are. The checks come from the method: for a host-level alert they are literally the USE table for that host. The best runbooks are short, executable and tested in drills (see LNX-307-T07), and they are tied to the alert definition so that the alert annotation carries a link to the runbook. In Prometheus-style rules that link is an annotation field, a pattern shown in LNX-307-T03.

The generalised pattern is: **inventory, then metrics per item, then ordered checks, then a decision**. It applies whether the items are CPUs or microservices.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

The good: both methods are cheap, teachable and finite. USE bounds the search; for a typical server there are perhaps a dozen resources and three questions each. RED makes alerts consistent across hundreds of services. A runbook turns tacit knowledge into something a newer engineer can follow and a reviewer can criticise. Because the methods describe the same data that monitoring systems collect, they also double as a specification for what to instrument.

The bad: USE is weak at software and application causes. A lock convoy, a slow query or a bad retry policy may leave every hardware resource idle; the method tells you the machine is healthy and the problem is elsewhere, which is a useful answer but not a diagnosis. Utilisation figures can mislead: "100% busy" on a modern NVMe device or a multi-queue NIC does not mean saturated, because the device serves many requests in parallel, and `%util` was defined for single-queue disks (the `iostat` manual itself warns about this, to verify against the installed sysstat version). Load average mixes runnable and uninterruptible tasks and varies by kernel, so it is a poor stand-alone saturation signal. RED tells you where the pain is, but a high error rate can be the result of a client abusing the service rather than a defect.

The ugly: runbooks decay. A runbook written for last year's architecture sends the responder to a host that no longer exists. A runbook that is too long is not read; one that is too terse relies on the very expertise it was meant to replace. Alert fatigue compounds it: the Prometheus guidance to alert on symptoms and keep the count low exists because pages for every possible cause train people to ignore pages [web: prometheus.io]. Finally, the "check everything" instinct can become a ritual, with engineers working through a checklist without thinking about what the numbers imply. The method is a starting framework, not a substitute for a model of the system.

Mitigations: attach a runbook to each page and treat an unhelpful runbook as a bug to fix in the post-incident review; test runbooks by game days; keep a "last verified" date; generate USE tables from the host inventory rather than hand-writing them; and keep the first screen of a runbook to a decision tree that fits on one page.

## Applications beyond this domain

USE is a resource-accounting idea, and it transfers wherever work flows through limited capacity. In a database, the resources are connections, buffer pool, lock slots and replication lag. In a Kubernetes cluster, they are node CPU and memory, pod count, API server request budgets, and persistent volumes; the pressure stall metrics and the kubelet metrics provide the saturation signals [src: kubernetes-docs 901f411e192be4ed]. In a CI system, the build queue is a textbook saturation measure. Outside software, hospital emergency departments and call centres apply the same triple (occupancy, waiting, failure) with different vocabulary.

RED transfers to any service with discrete requests: message consumers (messages per second, failures, processing time), batch pipelines (items per window, failed items, latency per item), and even human processes such as ticket queues. The golden-signal and RED families are the basis of service-level indicators and service-level objectives, so the same three numbers become the contract with the customer.

Runbooks transfer almost unchanged to security incident response, disaster recovery and compliance procedures (a compliance audit is a runbook run on a schedule). The key carry-over from method to team practice is that written procedures plus periodic drills beat memory; LNX-307-T10 returns to this in the context of on-call rotation.

## Examples

**A host-level pass.** A web server reports slow responses. The engineer opens the USE table and reads the three columns for each resource in order. Errors: `dmesg -T` shows no I/O or memory errors; `ip -s link` shows no drops. Saturation: `vmstat 1` shows `r` of 3 on an eight-core host, `si` and `so` at zero, `b` at zero; `/proc/pressure/io` shows `some avg10` at 0.00. Utilisation: `mpstat -P ALL 1` shows one core at 100% and the others near idle. The resource inventory points at a single-threaded process, not at capacity. The next step is `pidstat -u 1` (LNX-307-T02) to find it, then profiling (LNX-307-T05) to see why it spins.

**A service-level pass.** The RED dashboard for the API shows a flat request rate, an error ratio that has risen from near zero to two percent, and a 99th-percentile duration that has tripled. The error labels show status 503 from one upstream. The USE pass then moves to the machines hosting that upstream rather than the whole fleet, saving most of the work.

**A failing example, a misapplied method.** A team set an alert on CPU utilisation above 80% for each node and wrote a runbook that said "add nodes". During a batch job the alert fired nightly and the on-call engineer scaled out each time, yet users never noticed. The utilisation alert was a cause alert, not a symptom alert, and the runbook mixed up utilisation with saturation. The fix was to page on user-visible latency and keep CPU utilisation as a dashboard graph, with an alert only on sustained run-queue length or CPU pressure. This is exactly the issue that the Prometheus alerting advice warns about [web: prometheus.io].

**A runbook skeleton** (the lab for this topic asks you to complete one):

```text
Alert: DiskQueueDeep on <host>   Impact: slow writes for services on this host
1. Confirm: iostat -x 1 3  -> is aqu-sz > 4 for the device? (no: stop, false alarm)
2. Errors:  dmesg -T | grep -i -E 'i/o error|reset'  -> yes: escalate to storage
3. Who:     pidstat -d 1 5 -> top writer; is it a known job?
4. Act:     known job -> throttle (io.max, see T08); unknown -> capture strace -p (see T05) and escalate
5. Rollback and owner: ...
```

## Related problems and ideas

Several neighbouring methods fill the gaps in USE and RED. Gregg's own catalogue includes workload characterisation (who is calling, what, how much), drill-down analysis, the latency-oriented "time-in-state" approach, and the TSA method (thread state analysis), which accounts for where a thread spends its time. Queueing-theory based capacity planning uses the same resource model with predictive power. The Google-style golden signals are a superset of RED because they include saturation.

On the alerting side, multi-window multi-burn-rate alerts on error budgets replace raw thresholds with an alert on how fast the budget of an objective is being spent; this approach, described in the SRE workbook (to verify the chapter), is meant to reduce false positives. Related to runbooks are decision trees, "playbooks" in the incident-command sense, and post-incident reviews that feed new checks into the runbook.

Alternative and not-yet-mainstream ideas include automated root-cause analysis from topology graphs and anomaly detection, which promise to skip the manual walk but have a track record of producing noisy suggestions, and executable runbooks (notebooks and scripts with embedded tests) that can be checked in CI so they cannot silently rot.

## Active research

Work on automated diagnosis goes in two directions. One uses causal inference on service dependency graphs and traces to locate a faulty component from RED-like signals; benchmark datasets for microservice fault localisation have appeared in the research literature (to verify specific datasets). The other applies large language models to incident triage, retrieving runbook text and suggesting steps; the open questions are accuracy, the risk of confident wrong answers and how to keep a human in the loop. In the kernel community, pressure stall information is the active area: refinements such as the per-cgroup triggers, the use of pressure to drive userspace out-of-memory daemons, and its export to cluster schedulers are open questions. Separately, work on saturation for solid-state and virtual devices asks what the replacement for `%util` should be when devices are parallel.

## Future direction

Expect three changes. First, pressure metrics will replace load average as the default saturation signal in dashboards and alerts, as more distributions enable them by default and as exporters surface them. Second, alerting will continue to move from static thresholds to objective-based burn-rate alerts, with runbooks linked directly from each. Third, runbooks will become executable and testable, with chat and agent front ends. What to watch: whether automated triage can cite the checks it ran, so that a responder can audit it, and whether eBPF-based observability (LNX-307-T05) provides universal saturation numbers for software resources such as locks and queues, which USE has historically handled poorly. The principle of inventory, three signals, ordered checks and a decision is unlikely to change.
