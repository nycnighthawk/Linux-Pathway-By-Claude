# Production on-call: carrying the pager, running an incident and learning from it

## Define the problem

A service that matters has to be looked after at times when its builders are asleep, travelling or at dinner. Someone must be reachable, must know what to do, and must have the authority and access to do it. On-call is the arrangement that provides this: a rotation of people who take responsibility for the health of a set of services for a period, are alerted when a defined condition occurs, and are expected to acknowledge and act within an agreed time. It is also the part of the job where all the technical skills of this course meet human limits: tiredness, stress, unclear ownership and the pull between fixing the system now and understanding it later.

The problem splits into four. **Design**: how should rotations, escalation paths and alert policies be arranged so that outages are covered without wrecking the people covering them? **Response**: what should a person do in the first minutes, how are decisions and communications handled when several people are involved, and when should they escalate? **Recovery**: how to restore service safely, preferring mitigation over a deep fix at 3 a.m. **Learning**: how to turn each incident into improvements in the system, the alerts and the runbooks. This slice covers all four at an introductory depth for an engineer who is about to join a rotation, on Linux servers and their services. The technical diagnosis it relies on is in the earlier topics: methods and runbooks (T01), counters (T02), alerting (T03), logs (T04), tracing (T05), crash dumps (T06), drills (T07) and memory limits (T08).

## History and approaches

Duty rosters are older than computing: physicians on call, telephone exchange engineers, and mainframe operators with a pager and a thick manual. In the early Unix and internet era, operations were often the same few people who built the systems, reachable by phone, with escalation by reputation. Dedicated operations teams (sysadmins, "ops") arose as systems grew, and a wall formed between those who wrote the code and those who ran it; incidents were thrown over the wall and the people woken were not those who could fix the cause. Incentives diverged: developers were rewarded for features, operators for stability.

Two movements reshaped this. **DevOps** (2008 onward, from conferences organised by Patrick Debois and talks such as the 2009 "10 deploys per day" Velocity presentation) argued that the people who build a service should share responsibility for running it. **Site reliability engineering** at Google, described in the SRE book (2016), made reliability an engineering discipline with explicit service-level objectives, error budgets, limits on operational load (the practice of capping on-call and toil at a fraction of an engineer's time, with 25 percent on-call time cited as a ceiling) and a formal postmortem culture [web: sre.google]. Amazon's principle that the builders also run their service, and similar rules in other companies put development teams on the pager. Tooling grew accordingly: PagerDuty (2009), Opsgenie and VictorOps managed schedules, escalation and notification across phone, SMS and push; chat-based operations ("ChatOps", GitHub's Hubot, then Slack integrations) moved incident coordination into shared channels.

The structured approach to the incident itself came from emergency services. The **Incident Command System**, developed by US fire agencies in the 1970s, assigned clear roles (commander, operations, planning, communications) to handle large events, and it was adapted for operations teams by Google ("Incident Management at Google") and by PagerDuty's open incident response documentation, which defines roles such as incident commander, scribe and communications lead, with a rule that the commander coordinates rather than debugs [web: response.pagerduty.com]. The postmortem tradition, borrowed from aviation and healthcare safety, matured into the **blameless postmortem**: examining how a system and its processes allowed a person to make an error, instead of who erred. John Allspaw's writing on blameless postmortems at Etsy and Sidney Dekker's "just culture" are the intellectual background (to verify the specific references).

Approaches that partly failed include heroics (a single expert who is always called, burns out and leaves), "follow the sun" rotations that work only with several well-staffed time zones, alerts on every metric that wake people for non-actionable conditions, and handing the pager to a junior without runbooks or authority. A notable lesson from the SRE material is that if more than half of pages are not actionable, or pages exceed roughly two incidents per shift, the problem is the system, not the person. The pattern that emerged: **bounded load, clear roles, actionable alerts, written procedures, blameless learning**.

## Current state

### The rotation

A usual arrangement has a primary and a secondary on-call per service or team, weekly shifts (or split day/night), a manager or senior escalation tier above them, and a written handover at shift change that names open issues, risky changes in flight and noisy alerts. Fairness measures include compensation or time off, a cap on consecutive nights, and shadowing before a first solo shift. The **paging policy** defines severity levels: for example, sev1 (user-facing outage, respond within five minutes, page immediately), sev2 (degraded, respond in 30 minutes), sev3 (ticket, business hours). Alert routing is via the alert manager's labels (LNX-307-T03) to the paging service, with escalation if the primary does not acknowledge within a set time.

### Readiness

Before a first shift an engineer should have: working access (VPN, SSH, cloud console, hardware token) tested from a laptop and a phone; the list of services and owners; links to dashboards (RED and USE views, T01) and the runbook index; the paging tool installed and tested; familiarity with the rollback and deployment tools; and knowledge of the limits of authority (may I restart production, fail over a database, disable a feature flag?). A "break-glass" access path with audit logging should exist for emergencies. Drills (T07) and game days exist to practise exactly this.

### Working an incident

A concise sequence for the first minutes:

1. **Acknowledge** the page so others know it is owned, and note the time. Do not start debugging before reading the alert text and its runbook link.
2. **Assess impact and severity.** Are users affected? How many? Is data at risk? Check the RED dashboard (T01, T03). Declare an incident in the shared channel if the severity warrants, and announce who is the incident commander.
3. **Mitigate first, diagnose second.** Rolling back the last deploy, failing over, shedding load, restarting a stuck service or disabling a feature flag usually restores service faster than finding a root cause. But **preserve evidence** before destructive mitigation: export the journal (`journalctl -b > file`), capture `ps`, `free`, `ss -s`, pressure files, a heap or thread dump, and check for OOM kills (T08) and kernel messages (`dmesg -T`). A restart that clears the symptom and destroys the evidence leaves the incident open to recur.
4. **Follow the method.** USE on the host, then dependencies (T01, T02), with traces and logs (T04, T05) for detail. Write what you see and what you change in the incident channel with timestamps; a scribe does this in larger incidents.
5. **Communicate.** Post updates on a regular schedule (every 30 minutes, even if there is nothing new), to stakeholders and a status page for external users; use plain language and state what is known, what is being done and when the next update will come.
6. **Escalate early.** If you do not have a hypothesis after 15 to 30 minutes, or the impact grows, or the issue is outside your knowledge, bring in the secondary or the service owner. Escalation is not failure.
7. **Resolve and verify.** Confirm recovery through the user-facing signals, not only the host metrics, and watch for relapse. Downgrade and close the incident formally.
8. **Hand over** if the incident spans a shift boundary, with a written state.

Linux-specific habits: use `tmux` or `screen` so that a dropped connection does not lose a session; use `script` or the shell history timestamps (`HISTTIMEFORMAT`) to keep a record; prefer read-only commands first; use `systemctl` and package rollbacks (`dnf history undo`, LNX-104) rather than editing files by hand; and take a configuration snapshot (`cp -a`) before changes.

### After the incident

Within a few days write a **postmortem**: summary and impact, timeline in UTC, root causes and contributing factors, what went well, what went badly, where we got lucky, and action items with owners and due dates, each classed as prevent, detect or mitigate. The review is blameless. Items go into the same tracker as other work, with a rule that reliability actions are done. The new knowledge flows back: update the runbook (T01), add or tune an alert (T03), add a drill (T07), and add a missing metric or log field (T04). The Google SRE postmortem guidance also recommends sharing postmortems widely and rewarding good ones [web: sre.google].

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

The good: a well-run rotation shortens outages, spreads knowledge, and gives the people who build a service direct feedback about its operational cost, which tends to improve design. Clear roles reduce chaos in large incidents. Blameless postmortems produce durable fixes and a culture where problems are reported early. The skills transfer to any technical leadership.

The bad: on-call is stressful and intrusive. Sleep interruption has measurable health and performance costs, and it affects retention, particularly for people with caring responsibilities. Alert fatigue is the main technical cause: noisy, non-actionable pages wear down trust and speed of response. Knowledge concentrates in a few people; documentation lags; junior engineers face high-stakes decisions with little context. Tooling sprawl (five dashboards, three chat tools, two ticket systems) slows response, and permission problems at 3 a.m. (a locked token, no sudo) are common.

The ugly: blame creeping back; postmortems whose action items are never done; and "hero culture", where the person who saves the night is praised while the work that would have prevented it is not. Cognitive pitfalls recur: tunnel vision on the first hypothesis, restarting things at random, changing several variables at once, and the temptation to hide mistakes. There is also a legal and security dimension: incident data may contain personal data, and an incident may be a security breach requiring a different process (evidence preservation, legal notification) from an availability event. Mitigations include enforcing a page budget, reviewing every page for actionability each week, an explicit right to escalate and to refuse unsafe changes, a "second pair of eyes" rule for destructive commands, rest after a night incident, and leadership attention to the on-call load as a metric.

## Applications beyond this domain

The incident command structure is used by emergency services, hospitals (code teams), aviation (crew resource management) and disaster relief. The blameless review idea comes from aviation and medicine and is used in manufacturing as the Toyota "five whys". Rotating duty with escalation and handover is found in healthcare, security operations centres and customer support. Runbooks and checklists have a strong evidence base in surgery and aviation (checklists reduce errors under stress). Chatops and status pages are also used in public-sector crisis communication. In software teams, the same discipline applies to security incident response, data-quality incidents in analytics pipelines, and ML model incidents, where the on-call may be a data scientist.

## Examples

**A good night.** At 02:10 an alert, high error ratio on the checkout service, pages the primary. The runbook link points to a decision tree. The responder acknowledges, sees on the dashboard that errors began at 02:05 when a deployment finished, and rolls back with the documented command. Errors fall within three minutes; the responder takes a journal export and a thread dump from one failing instance first, posts a summary in the channel, and opens a ticket for the owning team to investigate in daylight. Total time awake: 25 minutes.

**An escalation done right.** A database host shows rising I/O wait and the application timeouts grow. The responder runs `iostat -x` and `pidstat -d` (T02), sees a backup job saturating the disk, but is unsure whether pausing it will corrupt the backup. They page the data-platform secondary after 15 minutes, who confirms that `kill -STOP` of the job is safe. Total impact is reduced because the question went to the right person quickly.

**A postmortem fragment.** Timeline in UTC; contributing factors: (1) the alert on disk use was set at 95% so there were only 20 minutes left at the fill rate, (2) the log rotation (T04) was disabled in a recent configuration change, (3) the runbook referred to a host that had been retired. Actions: alert on `predict_linear` fill time (prevent, T03), restore rotation and add a config test (prevent), update the runbook and add a drill (detect and mitigate, T07). No individual is named as the cause.

**A failing example.** A responder at 03:00 saw a service with a memory leak and restarted it, which fixed the symptom. They closed the incident. The same service was killed by the OOM killer twice the following week at busier times. In the postmortem the lack of evidence from the first event made the diagnosis longer. The corrective rule was added to the runbook: collect a heap dump and `memory.stat` before restarting (T08), and alert on `oom_kill` events.

## Related problems and ideas

Neighbouring practices include service-level objectives with error budgets (decide when to freeze features), alert design and burn-rate alerts (T03), chaos engineering and game days (T07), incident management software and automation of repetitive response, and security incident response with its different rules (NIST SP 800-61 gives a four-phase lifecycle that runs from preparation through detection, containment and recovery to review). Related organisational ideas include follow-the-sun rotations, "reliability champions", dedicated platform and SRE teams and the debate over whether developers should be on call for all services. Not yet mainstream: AI assistants that summarise an incident from chat and metrics, or propose rollbacks, and "automated remediation" where a safe, tested runbook step runs without a human; both need strong safeguards, since automation can amplify mistakes.

## Active research

Research looks at the human factors of incident response: how cognitive load, sleep loss and team communication affect outcomes; how experienced responders form hypotheses; and how to build tools that support, not replace, their thinking. Resilience engineering (Hollnagel, Woods, Dekker; the Learning from Incidents community) argues that success and failure arise from the same adaptive work, and that postmortems should look at how normal work produced a bad outcome. Studies on alert quality, incident taxonomies and large language model triage are active; results so far suggest assistance with summarising and retrieval but unreliable root-cause suggestions (to verify). Another area is measuring the effect of on-call load on retention and well-being.

## Future direction

Expect more automation in the first minutes of an incident (enrichment of alerts with recent deploys, graphs and runbook steps; one-click rollbacks) and AI summarisation of timelines and drafting of postmortems, with humans retaining decisions. Practices will keep moving toward service-level objectives, fewer and better alerts, and a wider sharing of the pager with developers supported by platform tooling. Regulatory pressure (operational resilience rules in finance, incident notification rules in security law) will formalise incident records and review. What to watch is whether automation reduces toil without removing the understanding that on-call builds, and whether organisations measure and cap on-call load as seriously as they measure uptime.
