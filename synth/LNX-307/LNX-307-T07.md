# Break/fix drills: building troubleshooting skill by diagnosing deliberately broken systems

## Define the problem

Everyone who runs Linux for a living learns that reading about a failure and meeting it are different experiences. In the manual, a service that will not start has one cause, stated clearly. On a real host at an inconvenient hour it presents as a vague symptom (the site is down, login is slow, the disk is full), several things are wrong at once, the obvious explanation is usually not the real one, and the tools you need are the ones you have not used for six months. Troubleshooting is a skill made of knowledge (how the parts work), method (what to check in what order) and practice (fluency under pressure). The first two can be taught from text. The third can only be acquired by repetition against failures.

Break/fix drilling answers that: an instructor, or a script, plants a known fault in a working system, and the learner must find and repair it using only the evidence available, within a time limit, then explain the cause. The same technique serves teams. Production-style faults are rehearsed in a safe place so that the runbooks of LNX-307-T01 are tested before they are needed, and on-call engineers (LNX-307-T10) build the reflexes they will use. This slice covers how drills are built and run, the common fault families on Linux and the diagnostic path through each, how to verify a fix, and how to review the result. It assumes the tools of the other topics in the course: counters (T02), metrics (T03), logs (T04), tracing (T05), crash dumps (T06), and memory limits (T08). It is also the course's integration topic, with depth four: more is expected of the learner than recall.

## History and approaches

Hands-on fault training is old. Aviation uses simulators for engine failures and checklists for response; the medical profession uses mannequins and case drills; the military uses exercises with a hostile "red team". In computing, the closest ancestors are the operations apprenticeships in which a junior shadowed a senior, and certification exams that give a candidate a live machine with a task. The Red Hat practical exams (RHCSA, RHCE) and the Linux Foundation certified administrator exam (LFCS) place the candidate at a command line in a broken or unconfigured system with a task list, which pushed training providers towards lab environments with scripted faults. The Rocky Linux lab series includes a networking lab with an exercise on troubleshooting a broken network setup, a typical sample of the type [src: rocky-docs 1c0df21dd9efe0d6].

In operations practice, the major development was chaos engineering. Netflix's Chaos Monkey (2011) randomly terminated production instances to force services to tolerate loss; the Simian Army extended this to latency and whole-region failures, and the later Principles of Chaos Engineering described hypothesis-driven experiments in production. Google's DiRT (Disaster Recovery Testing) exercises and Amazon's GameDay events scheduled large-scale failures against real systems with staff watching. Open source tools such as Gremlin, Chaos Mesh, LitmusChaos and Pumba inject faults into containers and clusters. These approaches differ from a classroom drill in purpose: chaos tests the system, drills test the people. In practice the two merge on a **game day**, where a planted fault tests both the automation and the responders.

Another strand is the capture-the-flag and "broken server" scenario. Several open projects publish broken systems for self-study. Exercise collections such as the "SadServers" platform (browser-based Linux and DevOps troubleshooting scenarios; to verify the current format) and `linuxupskillchallenge` give timed scenarios with an automated check of whether the problem was fixed. Educators build their own with shell scripts that apply a fault: change a permission, edit a unit file, fill a filesystem, add a bad firewall rule, stop a daemon, poison `/etc/fstab`. The failure mode of these early drills was that the fault was unrealistic (an obvious typo with an obvious error message) or that the check tested a particular command rather than a working outcome.

Practitioners also found that the **method** mattered more than the fault list. The literature on incident response, such as the Google SRE material on effective troubleshooting, describes a hypothetico-deductive loop: observe, form a hypothesis, test it with the cheapest experiment, narrow, and when stuck, bisect the system by splitting it at a midpoint [web: sre.google]. That is the structure drills teach. The lesson that closed the gap between teaching and reality is that a good drill is judged by an outcome check (the service answers correctly), not by the exact fix, and by the quality of the write-up afterwards.

## Current state

### The structure of a drill

A drill has five parts. The **scenario** states the symptom and the business impact in the words a user would use (for example, the intranet page has failed since the reboot), never the cause. The **environment** is a disposable system, usually a container, a virtual machine or a directory tree with a simulated host, that can be reset in seconds. The **fault** is a change applied by a script, ideally a combination: a visible fault that masks a deeper one. The **grader** checks the outcome (can I fetch the page, does the unit stay active across a restart, is the disk below 80%) rather than a specific command. And the **debrief** compares the learner's diagnosis with the planted cause and the timeline.

In the labs of this course the grader is written first, as with every lab, which forces the designer to say what "fixed" means in observable terms. The break-fix lab for this topic simulates a host as a directory tree with configuration, a unit file, a log and a mini filesystem table, so that it runs anywhere without root; the same graders work against a real VM.

### The method

A reusable path, with the commands used at each step:

1. **Define the symptom precisely.** What fails, for whom, since when, how often? Reproduce it: `curl -sv`, `systemctl status`, `journalctl -u UNIT -b`. Capture the exact error text.
2. **Ask what changed.** Last package update (`rpm -qa --last`, `grep upgrade /var/log/dpkg.log`), last config edit (`ls -lt /etc | head`), last reboot (`last -x | head`, `uptime -s`), and recent deployments. Most faults follow a change.
3. **Apply USE** to the host (LNX-307-T01): errors first (`dmesg -T`, `journalctl -p err -b`), then saturation, then utilisation (T02). The manual for `dmesg` lists the useful filters for facility, level and human-readable times [src: man-pages f8e0abef9527f6cd].
4. **Walk the dependency chain from the symptom down.** For a web request: DNS, TCP connect, TLS, proxy, application, database, disk. For a service start: unit file, executable, user and permissions, configuration, ports, dependencies, resources. Test each link with the simplest tool that can show it (`dig`, `ss -ltnp`, `curl --resolve`, `namei -l`, `systemd-analyze verify`).
5. **Bisect.** Where several components are involved, split at the midpoint and test: can I reach the backend directly from the proxy host? Does the program run by hand as the service user (`sudo -u svc /path/app`)? Does it work with the default config?
6. **Change one thing at a time, record it, and be ready to undo it.** Copy a file before editing (`cp -a f f.orig`), write down each command.
7. **Verify the outcome, then the persistence.** Does it work now, and does it survive a restart and a reboot? Add the check to monitoring (T03) so that it will alert next time.
8. **Write it up**: symptom, timeline, root cause, fix, how it was detected, what would have detected it sooner.

### Common fault families and their diagnostic paths

**A service will not start.** `systemctl status UNIT` and `journalctl -xeu UNIT` give the exit status. Exit 203/EXEC means the `ExecStart` path or its permissions are wrong, 200-series codes point to a setup error in the environment (user, directory, namespace), 217/USER means a missing user, and 226/NAMESPACE a sandboxing directive that fails. The Arch wiki's systemd page lists techniques for investigating failed services and the case of short-lived processes that leave no output [src: archwiki f84242a602a21360]. Then `systemd-analyze verify UNIT`, `systemctl cat UNIT` (includes drop-ins), and `systemctl show -p ExecStart,User UNIT`. If the program starts by hand but not as a service, suspect the environment: SELinux (`ausearch -m avc -ts recent`, `getenforce`), the working directory, `ProtectSystem=`, or limits.

**The disk is full, or looks full.** `df -h` and `df -i` (inode exhaustion gives ENOSPC with free space), `du -xh --max-depth=1 / | sort -h`, and the sneaky case of deleted-but-open files (`lsof +L1` shows a file removed while a process keeps it open; the space returns only when the process is restarted or the descriptor truncated through `/proc/PID/fd/N`). Also check quotas (`repquota`), reserved blocks (`tune2fs -l`), a full `/var/log` from an unrotated file (LNX-307-T04) and a filesystem mounted over a populated directory, which hides files.

**The network is "broken".** The ladder: link (`ip -br link`), address (`ip -br addr`), route (`ip route get X`), neighbour (`ip neigh`), name (`getent hosts name`, which follows `nsswitch.conf`, versus `dig`, which does not), port (`ss -ltnp`), firewall (`nft list ruleset`, `firewall-cmd --list-all`), then the path (`mtr`, `tracepath`, `tcpdump -ni IFACE host X`). A mismatch of MTU shows as small packets working and large ones hanging. Asymmetric routing and reverse path filtering (`rp_filter`) drop replies silently. The topics of LNX-202 and LNX-312 provide the detail for each layer.

**The system is slow.** Follow T01 and T02: pressure files, `vmstat`, `iostat`, `pidstat`; then trace the offender with T05's tools. Common root causes: swapping (memory limit, a leak), a runaway process, an I/O storm from a backup or a log, CPU throttling in a cgroup (`cpu.stat` `nr_throttled`, T08), a noisy neighbour in a VM (`steal`), DNS timeouts, and a full connection table.

**It will not boot.** From the boot loader or the emergency shell: `journalctl -b -1`, check `/etc/fstab` (a missing device with no `nofail` drops to emergency mode), initramfs (rebuilt with `dracut -f` or `update-initramfs -u`), the boot loader entries, a full `/boot`, SELinux relabel (`touch /.autorelabel`), a broken kernel (boot the previous entry), and file system errors (`fsck` from rescue media). The `rd.break` and `systemd.unit=rescue.target` kernel arguments are the standard entry points.

**Permissions and identity.** `namei -l PATH` shows every directory permission on the path; `getfacl`, `ls -Z`, `id USER`, `sudo -l`, `ulimit -a` and PAM logs in the journal cover the rest. Most "permission denied" errors that survive `chmod` are SELinux denials or missing execute bits on a parent directory.

**Resource exhaustion.** File descriptors (`ulimit -n`, `/proc/PID/limits`, `cat /proc/sys/fs/file-nr`), process counts (`ps -eLf | wc -l`, `pids.max`), ephemeral ports (`ss -s`), conntrack tables (`conntrack -S`), inotify watches, and OOM kills (T08).

### Designing and running drills

Good drills are small (10 to 30 minutes), layered (one visible and one hidden fault), realistic (copied from past incidents), and reversible (a reset script returns the system to the working baseline). They are run in pairs or with an observer who watches the method rather than the solution, and graded on three things: outcome (fixed and persistent), evidence (the learner can show what pointed to the cause), and safety (no destructive command run without a backup). Teams add time pressure gradually, rotate roles (responder, scribe, incident commander) and keep a catalogue of past drills. The generalised pattern: **plant, observe, hypothesise, test, fix, verify, record**.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

The good: drills build transferable intuition faster than any other form of study, expose weak points in documentation and monitoring, and give new engineers confidence before they carry a pager. Because graders check outcomes, they are objective, and the same scenarios can be reused in CI to test the runbooks and the recovery automation. They also encourage the habit of recording commands and timelines, which is what good incident response requires.

The bad: a drill teaches the faults it contains. A fixed catalogue becomes a quiz that learners memorise (the answer is always the unit file), which trains pattern matching rather than method. A synthetic fault is cleaner than the real world, where symptoms are noisy, logs are missing, several causes interact and the person who knows the system is on holiday. Drills in containers cannot reproduce kernel or hardware faults (LNX-307-T06's territory), and VM drills are slower to reset. Scoring by time encourages recklessness: rebooting to see if it helps, editing without backup, or fixing the symptom (restarting the service) while leaving the cause.

The ugly: drills and chaos experiments in production can cause real incidents, damage data, or trigger alerts and pages for people not in the exercise. Blast radius, abort conditions and communication are the safety rules; Netflix's own principles say to start with a hypothesis about steady state, limit the blast radius and be able to stop at once [web: principlesofchaos.org]. A culture issue also looms: drills used to evaluate individuals rather than systems erode the blameless attitude on which good incident review depends. And a drill library that nobody maintains diverges from the production architecture and teaches obsolete habits, the same decay seen in runbooks (T01).

Mitigations: randomise parameters (which file, which port) in setup scripts so that answers cannot be memorised; require a short written diagnosis; include no-fault scenarios, where the right answer is that nothing is broken; keep a facilitator rule that the first ten minutes are for observation only; and review each drill in a blameless format.

## Applications beyond this domain

The method (observe, hypothesise, bisect, change one variable, verify) is the same in debugging software, where `git bisect` automates the halving of a history, and in hardware repair, medicine's differential diagnosis, and aircraft maintenance. Fault injection is used in distributed-systems testing (Jepsen subjects databases to partitions and clock skew and checks their promises), in storage testing (failing disks via `dm-flakey` and `dm-error`, or the kernel's fault-injection framework under `/sys/kernel/debug/fail_*`) and in security testing, where a red team plays the attacker. Tabletop exercises in disaster recovery and compliance (practising a ransomware response) are break/fix drills without the computers. The structure of a drill, with scenario, environment, fault, grader and debrief, matches an automated test: an arrange-act-assert cycle whose assertion is the outcome.

## Examples

**Drill 1: the service that never starts.** Symptom: after a config change the application is down. Fault 1: `ExecStart=/usr/local/bin/myapp` points to a directory renamed to `/opt/myapp/bin`. Fault 2 (hidden): the configuration file's listen port is already taken by another service. The learner runs `systemctl status myapp` (exit 203/EXEC), repairs the path with a drop-in (`systemctl edit myapp`), restarts, sees a second failure in `journalctl -u myapp` (address already in use), finds the owner with `ss -ltnp`, and chooses to change the app's port rather than killing the other service. The grader fetches the page on the intended port and checks that the unit is enabled.

**Drill 2: a full disk that is not full.** `df -h` says 60% used, but writes fail. `df -i` shows 100% inode use: a cron job creates millions of tiny session files in `/var/spool/app`. The fix is deleting old files with `find -delete -mtime +1` and fixing the job, then adding an inode alert in monitoring (LNX-307-T03).

**Drill 3: slow logins.** SSH takes 30 seconds to prompt. `ssh -vvv` shows the delay after the key exchange; the cause is reverse DNS in `sshd` (`UseDNS`) with an unreachable resolver. `strace -f -tt -p $(pidof sshd)` (T05) would show the blocked `connect` to port 53. Fix the resolver or set `UseDNS no`. The drill chains four tools.

**Drill 4: the trap of the helpful reboot.** A learner restarts the host and the web app returns. In the debrief the facilitator shows the log: a memory leak triggered an OOM kill that morning (T08); the reboot cleared the symptom and destroyed the evidence. The debrief rule is: collect evidence before a mitigating restart (journal export, `ps`, `free`, a heap or core dump where relevant).

**A failing drill design.** A facilitator planted `chmod 000 /etc/passwd` on a lab VM. Learners could not log in, could not run `sudo`, and the console login failed, so no tool could be used to diagnose. The scenario was unwinnable by design. The fix was to leave one working administrative path (a root console or rescue target) and to describe it in the brief.

## Related problems and ideas

Chaos engineering (hypothesis-driven fault injection at scale), game days, wheel-of-misfortune role-play exercises (a Google practice in which a volunteer plays the on-call and a facilitator describes a past outage), capture-the-flag events and certification practicals all overlap. Post-incident reviews and the blameless culture are the other half of learning from real failures; "five whys" and contributing-factor analysis give structure, with the caveat that root cause is often plural. Automation ideas include record-and-replay of real faults (snapshot a broken machine and let learners explore it), LLM-assisted triage tools that propose hypotheses from logs and metrics, and runbooks as code, tested in CI against injected faults. Not mainstream yet: continuous verification in production (periodic automated fault injection with automatic abort), and synthetic users that probe functionality constantly (blackbox probes in LNX-307-T03).

## Active research

Research on troubleshooting covers how experts debug (cognitive studies of incident responders find that experienced engineers form few, good hypotheses early and test cheap ones first), automatic fault localisation (statistical debugging, spectrum-based techniques), and root-cause analysis on microservice graphs using traces and metrics. Education research evaluates simulation fidelity and scaffolding: whether hints improve learning, and how much realism is needed. Automated generation of broken-system scenarios from real incident reports with language models is being explored, along with the question of whether AI assistants make learners rely on guesses instead of building a diagnostic model. The open question for operations teams is how to measure whether drills reduce time to recover in real incidents, since the effect is hard to isolate.

## Future direction

Expect drills to become continuous and automated: scenario libraries running in ephemeral environments, with graders in CI, scheduled game days, and chaos experiments wired to service-level objectives and automatic abort. Learner assistance will include assistants that coach without giving the answer, and traces that record the learner's command history for the debrief. The emphasis will shift further from knowing commands to knowing how to ask: bisecting a system, reading pressure and latency, and using traces. In the near term the practical steps are to keep a drill catalogue per service, run one a month, convert each real incident into a drill and a monitoring check, and review the results blamelessly.
