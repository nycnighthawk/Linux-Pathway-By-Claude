# Watching and shaping hostile traffic: Suricata and fail2ban

## Define the problem

A host on a network meets traffic from parties it does not trust. Some of it is a nuisance that can be recognised by repetition, such as an automated tool trying ten thousand passwords against SSH. Some of it is a deliberate attack that is visible only by inspecting what the packets contain: an exploit string in an HTTP request, a command-and-control beacon with a known pattern, a DNS query for a malicious domain, a TLS client fingerprint belonging to known malware. The defender needs ways to *detect* hostile traffic, to *record* it for later analysis, and in some cases to *block* it, without breaking legitimate use and without becoming a new target.

Two tools address the two halves of this problem on Linux. **Fail2ban** works from the host's logs: it follows files or the systemd journal, applies regular expressions to detect failed authentication or abusive requests, counts occurrences per source address, and instructs the firewall to ban an address for a while. It sees only what applications write down, but it works anywhere, needs no special network position and is simple to understand. **Suricata** works from packets: it reads traffic from a network interface (or a capture file), reassembles flows, decodes application protocols, and matches rules against both the byte stream and the decoded fields. It can run as a passive sensor that writes alerts and structured metadata, or inline as a prevention system that drops packets. It sees the traffic of a whole network segment, including hosts that do not log at all, but it can see only what is not encrypted and only where it is placed.

The practical difficulties are well known. A ban rule that matches too loosely locks out legitimate users, including an administrator behind the same NAT address as an attacker. A rule set that is not updated misses current threats, and one that is updated but not tuned produces so many alerts that none is read. Encryption now covers most traffic, which blinds payload matching. Sensors can be bypassed by fragmentation tricks, overloaded by volume or placed on a switch port that does not receive the interesting traffic. And an attacker who knows the defence may use it as a weapon: spoofed or crafted log lines can make fail2ban ban a victim's address. This slice covers the architecture and configuration of both tools, rule and filter writing, deployment positions, tuning, and the limits that set what they can and cannot promise.

## History and approaches

Network intrusion detection started as a research topic in the 1980s and became a practical Unix tool with Martin Roesch's **Snort** (1998), a lightweight packet sniffer with a pattern-matching rule language. Snort's rule syntax, a header describing action, protocol, addresses and ports followed by options in parentheses, became the lingua franca of signature-based detection, and communities began to write rule sets: Sourcefire's VRT rules, and the free **Emerging Threats** rule collection from 2003. Snort was single-threaded for most of its life, which became a real limit as network speeds grew, and the OISF (Open Information Security Foundation, 2009), funded by the US Department of Homeland Security and industry partners, created **Suricata** as a multithreaded engine that reads the Snort-style rule syntax but adds native application-layer parsers, flow tracking, file extraction and later a structured JSON event output (EVE). Cisco's Snort 3 later rewrote its engine along similar lines, so the two projects converged in design while remaining distinct. The Debian handbook describes Suricata as the successor of Snort, which was removed from that distribution, and notes the decisive limit that its effectiveness depends on the traffic seen on the monitored interface [src: debian-handbook 80a9617797aae097].

Signature detection met its own failures. Evasion research in the late 1990s (Ptacek and Newsham's 1998 paper on insertion, evasion and denial of service) showed that an IDS that reassembled packets differently from the destination host could be fooled by overlapping fragments, unusual TTLs and ambiguous TCP segments; modern engines normalise traffic and model host behaviour to reduce this. Rule sets were noisy: a signature written for a specific exploit also matched benign look-alikes. Inline prevention (IPS) was tried and often turned off, because a false positive that drops production traffic is more costly than a missed alert. The wider shift to encryption, first TLS 1.2 with its readable certificates and then TLS 1.3 with encrypted handshake parts and QUIC, pushed detection towards metadata: server name indication (when not encrypted), certificate fields, TLS client fingerprints such as JA3 (and its successor JA4), flow size and timing patterns, and DNS queries. Parallel systems such as **Zeek** (formerly Bro) focused less on alerts and more on rich logs of protocol behaviour from which analysts derive detections.

Host-based banning has a different lineage. Early defences against brute-force login attempts were TCP wrappers with `hosts.deny` edited by shell scripts, and `sshdfilter` and `denyhosts`, which parsed SSH logs. **Fail2ban** (Cyril Jaquier, 2004) generalised the pattern with a client-server design, filters written as Python regular expressions with a `<HOST>` placeholder, actions defined as command templates, and jails that bind a filter to a log, thresholds and actions. Fail2ban's weaknesses were also instructive: reading logs by polling was slow and later replaced by `pyinotify`, `gamin` and the systemd journal back end; IPv6 support lagged; and a crafted log message could inject text into a matched field. Fail2ban has had security advisories of this kind, including command injection through poorly quoted mail actions that were fixed in releases after 2021 (to verify the details). A newer approach, **CrowdSec**, applies the same detect-and-ban idea but shares signals among participants, so that an address banned by many others is blocked pre-emptively. The Debian handbook summarises fail2ban's shape: four kinds of configuration file for global settings, filters, actions and jails, with defaults such as a one-hour ban time, ten-minute window and five retries for SSH [src: debian-handbook a8d839d5ad7cef20].

## Current state

**Fail2ban structure.** The server monitors a *jail* made of a log source (`logpath`, or `backend = systemd` with a `journalmatch`), a *filter* (`/etc/fail2ban/filter.d/NAME.conf`) with one or more `failregex` lines and optional `ignoreregex`, and an *action* (`/etc/fail2ban/action.d/`) that bans and unbans. Settings in `jail.local` or `jail.d/*.local` override the packaged `jail.conf`: `maxretry`, `findtime` and `bantime` define the threshold, and `bantime.increment = true` with `bantime.factor` makes repeat offenders wait longer; the built-in `recidive` jail watches fail2ban's own log for addresses banned several times. `ignoreip` lists addresses that must never be banned, such as the administrators' bastion. Typical actions call `nftables` (with a set of banned addresses, `nftables-multiport`, `nftables-allports`) or `firewalld` rich rules, and they take `banaction`, `port`, `protocol` and `chain`. `fail2ban-client status sshd` lists the jail's counts and banned addresses, `fail2ban-client set sshd unbanip 192.0.2.4` removes a ban, and `fail2ban-regex /var/log/auth.log /etc/fail2ban/filter.d/sshd.conf` is the essential test tool: it replays a log against a filter and reports how many lines matched, which did not, and the unique addresses found. A custom filter is a short file:

```ini
[Definition]
failregex = ^<HOST> .* "POST /login HTTP/[0-9.]+" 401
ignoreregex =
```

**Suricata structure.** A Suricata process uses *capture* threads (AF_PACKET with multiple rings and flow-hashing for load distribution, or PF_RING, DPDK, AF_XDP or NFQUEUE for inline), *decode* and *stream* modules for TCP reassembly, an *application-layer detection* stage with parsers for HTTP/1 and 2, TLS, DNS, SMB, SSH, FTP, SMTP, NFS, Modbus and more, a *detection engine* that matches rules, and *output* modules. Configuration is `/etc/suricata/suricata.yaml`: it sets `HOME_NET` and `EXTERNAL_NET`, the capture interface and thread model, the rule paths and the outputs. `eve-log` writes JSON events of types alert, http, dns, tls, flow, fileinfo and anomaly to `/var/log/suricata/eve.json`, from where they go to Elasticsearch, Splunk or a file shipper. The command `suricata -T -c /etc/suricata/suricata.yaml` validates configuration and rules, and `suricata -r capture.pcap -l /tmp/out` replays a capture, which is how rules are tested.

**Rules.** The rule syntax has a header and options:

```
alert http $HOME_NET any -> $EXTERNAL_NET any (msg:"LAB admin panel probe"; flow:established,to_server; http.uri; content:"/admin/config.php"; nocase; classtype:web-application-attack; sid:1000001; rev:1;)
```

The header holds the action (`alert`, `drop`, `reject`, `pass`), the protocol, source and destination with ports and direction. Options include `msg`, buffers that select decoded fields (`http.uri`, `http.header`, `dns.query`, `tls.sni`, `file.data`), `content` and `pcre` matches, `flow` and `flowbits` for state across packets, `threshold` and `detection_filter` for rate-based rules, `classtype`, `reference`, a unique `sid` and a revision. Local rules use sid values from 1,000,000 upward to avoid collisions. `suricata-update` downloads and merges rule sources (the Emerging Threats Open set by default, plus others from the OISF index), applies `disable.conf`, `enable.conf` and `modify.conf` to tune them, and writes `/var/lib/suricata/rules/suricata.rules`. A `suricatasc` socket can reload rules without restarting.

**Placement.** A sensor needs to see the traffic: a SPAN or mirror port on a switch, a network TAP, or the host's own interface for host-level monitoring. Inline mode uses NFQUEUE (with an `nftables` rule sending selected traffic to the queue) or an AF_PACKET bridge, and requires a bypass design so that a crashed sensor does not cut the network. Modern Suricata also supports eBPF/XDP bypass for large trusted flows to save CPU. Hardware sizing is mainly a function of rule count, traffic mix and threads; the guidance is to measure with `suricata --engine-analysis`, the stats log and `perf`.

**Together.** The two tools complement each other. Suricata can see a scan or an exploit against a host that never logs it, and fail2ban can react to a log pattern that is invisible on the wire because it is encrypted. A combined design sends Suricata alerts to a log that fail2ban also watches, so repeated high-severity alerts from one address produce a firewall ban; this is useful and also dangerous, since spoofed sources can then be used to ban legitimate addresses, and it should be limited to TCP traffic where the handshake confirms the source.

**The generalised pattern.** Both tools run the same loop: observe an event stream, match it against a pattern library, accumulate counters per entity, and decide on a response (log, alert, block). The quality of such a system depends on four things the pattern does not supply: the vantage point, the quality of patterns, the thresholds, and the safety of the response.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

**The good.** Both tools are open source, stable and widely documented. Fail2ban stops the bulk of commodity password guessing for a few lines of configuration, and its filters for common services are well maintained. Suricata provides application-layer visibility, community rules for known malware and exploit traffic, and a standard JSON log that feeds every analytics stack. The `fail2ban-regex` and `suricata -r` tools make both testable offline. Together they support the audit evidence required by many frameworks (monitoring of network boundaries, brute-force protection).

**The bad.** Signature-based detection finds only what someone wrote a rule for. Rule sets lag new threats and attract noise. Encryption removes payload visibility, so TLS-wrapped attacks surface only through metadata. Suricata needs real engineering effort at high rates: per-core load, packet drops, offload settings (generic receive offload must be disabled on the capture interface so reassembled packets do not exceed the MTU the engine expects), and memory for flows. Fail2ban's limits are those of logs: only after the failure is logged, only for services that log client addresses, and with an unbanning policy that must be thought through. A distributed attack from thousands of addresses, each trying once, never trips a per-address threshold.

**The ugly.** NAT and proxies confuse both tools. Behind a reverse proxy the log shows the proxy's address, so fail2ban bans the proxy (the entire site) unless the real client address is taken from `X-Forwarded-For` or the PROXY protocol; carrier-grade NAT puts thousands of users behind one address, so banning one bans all. Log injection is the sharpest edge: if a service logs attacker-controlled text, such as a username, a regex that matches too freely can be steered into banning an arbitrary address, including the administrators' own or the DNS resolver. IPv6 makes bans by single address nearly useless unless whole prefixes are banned. Suricata inline can break traffic, and a rule-update push that includes a bad rule can drop production flows at once. Both tools, if compromised or misconfigured, become a denial-of-service lever.

**Mitigations.** Allow-list essential addresses with `ignoreip`. Parse the real client address behind proxies. Start Suricata in alert-only mode and promote to `drop` rule by rule after reviewing a week of data. Keep a staging pipeline for rule updates (`suricata -T` in CI). Ban on a prefix for IPv6 where the policy permits. Use rate-limiting in the firewall (`nftables` meters, `limit rate`) as a first layer, key-based SSH so that password guessing is moot, and fail2ban as defence in depth rather than the main control. Review ban lists for self-inflicted entries.

## Applications beyond this domain

The same detect-count-respond loop appears at other layers. Web application firewalls (ModSecurity with the OWASP Core Rule Set, Cloudflare and AWS WAF) match request patterns and apply rate limits. Cloud security groups and DDoS protections use thresholds per source. Email security gateways such as SpamAssassin and rspamd apply scored rules. Fraud detection in payments and login services is a banking-grade version of "N failures in T minutes." Industrial control networks use passive protocol-aware monitors, with Suricata's Modbus and DNP3 parsers an open-source example. The approach of sharing indicators between sites, which CrowdSec makes routine, has parallels in threat-intelligence platforms using STIX and TAXII and in spam blocklists.

## Examples

**Stopping SSH guessing.** A server exposed to the Internet enables the `sshd` jail with `maxretry = 3`, `findtime = 10m`, `bantime = 1h` and increment on. Attempts drop from thousands a day to tens. The administrator also disables password authentication, so a successful guess is impossible, and keeps the jail to reduce log noise and connection load.

**A custom web login filter.** A web application returns 401 for failed logins and logs the client address. A filter regex counts those lines, and `fail2ban-regex` is run against a week of logs to confirm that it catches the attack and ignores normal typos (one or two failures). The jail uses `maxretry = 8` to spare forgetful users.

**Suricata as a campus sensor.** A university mirrors its border switch to a Suricata host with eight capture threads, loads Emerging Threats Open through `suricata-update`, and ships EVE JSON to a search cluster. A rule on `tls.sni` matching a known command-and-control domain fires on a laptop, and the DNS log shows the earlier lookup. The incident team isolates the laptop before data leaves.

**A failing example: the proxy ban.** A site behind a load balancer enables a login-abuse jail. Every request in the log carries the load balancer's address, so after five failures the jail bans the load balancer and the whole site goes dark. The fix is to log the forwarded client address and to add the balancer to `ignoreip`.

**A failing example: the evasion by TLS.** An exploit string is delivered over TLS 1.3. The sensor sees no payload, and a rule for the cleartext string never fires. The detection moves to endpoint telemetry and to behaviour at the server, such as the Falco rule from the previous topic.

## Related problems and ideas

Alternatives and neighbours include **Zeek** for protocol logs and scripted analysis, **Snort 3** as the other major signature engine, **Arkime** (formerly Moloch) for full packet capture and search, and **Security Onion**, a distribution that bundles Suricata, Zeek and analysis tools. Host-side controls include **nftables rate limits** and `ct` rules, **port knocking** and **SSH certificates or MFA**, which remove the target rather than watch it. **CrowdSec** adds shared reputation. **Machine-learning-based detection** of anomalous flows and **JA4 fingerprint databases** are growing areas but are not a replacement for signatures. Proposals such as **encrypted client hello** (ECH) change what a sensor may see, and **eBPF-based network observability** (Cilium Hubble, Pixie) shifts some of the sensor into the host.

## Active research

Open topics include detection in encrypted traffic from metadata and timing (classifying flows without payload), evasion-resistant reassembly, accelerated pattern matching (Hyperscan and GPU or SmartNIC offload), and rule quality: measuring which signatures produce real alerts, and generating rules automatically from malware analysis. For fail2ban-like systems, current questions concern shared reputation without poisoning, handling IPv6 at scale, and replacing log parsing with structured events. The effect of QUIC and ECH on network monitoring is under discussion in industry groups (specifics to verify).

## Future direction

Expect signature engines to keep their role for known threats while detection shifts to flow metadata, DNS and endpoint telemetry because of encryption. Expect Suricata to continue moving parsers to Rust and to extend eBPF-based bypass and load balancing, and fail2ban-style bans to move into the firewall as dynamic sets with expiry, driven by structured logs. Expect shared-reputation services to expand. For operators the dependable advice is stable: place sensors where they see the traffic that matters, tune before enforcing, let the firewall do rate limiting first, and treat each automated response as something an attacker may try to trigger.
