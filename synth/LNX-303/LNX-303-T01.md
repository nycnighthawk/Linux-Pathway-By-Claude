# Running a certificate authority: issuance, revocation and trust on Linux

## Define the problem

Public-key cryptography solves the problem of sharing secrets with strangers, but it creates a new one: how does anyone know that a public key really belongs to the server, person or device that claims it? An X.509 certificate is the standard answer. It is a signed statement that binds a subject name, and a list of permitted uses, to a public key, and the signature comes from an issuer that the relying party already trusts. A **public key infrastructure** (PKI) is everything around that statement: the authorities that sign, the rules for what they may sign, the procedures for proving identity before signing, the channels that publish certificates, and the machinery for taking a statement back when the key or the facts change.

Linux administrators meet this problem from three directions. They are consumers, because every TLS connection, signed package repository, LDAP over TLS bind and smart-card login depends on a trust store that someone must curate. They are issuers, because internal services, mutual-TLS service meshes, VPN endpoints, SSH host certificates and device identities all need certificates that no public authority will sign for a private name. And they are auditors, because the compliance regimes covered in the rest of this course (STIG, CIS, PCI) contain numerous controls that amount to a demand for full certification-path validation including revocation status.

Failure in this area is rarely a broken algorithm. It is an expired intermediate nobody tracked, a root key left on an internet-connected laptop, a revocation list that grew too large to fetch, a client that quietly accepts anything when the revocation server is unreachable, a wildcard certificate copied to forty hosts, or a name constraint missing so that one department's CA can vouch for every domain in the company. The gap that practical PKI work fills is therefore operational: producing certificates with the right fields, structuring authorities so that a compromise stays contained, publishing validity information that clients can actually use, and replacing material on a schedule short enough that mistakes heal on their own.

This slice covers the data model (certificates, extensions, chains), the tools for building a small authority with OpenSSL and for running a managed one (FreeIPA's Dogtag, step-ca, cfssl), revocation through CRLs and OCSP, the client side on Linux (trust stores, `update-ca-trust`, `update-ca-certificates`, PAM and SSSD smart-card settings) and the surrounding practice of short-lived certificates and automation with ACME. Hardware-bound keys appear in the LUKS and TPM topic; certificate-based signing of kernels and modules appears under Secure Boot.

## History and approaches

The X.509 standard dates from 1988 as part of the X.500 directory project, which imagined a global hierarchical directory with certificates as its authentication layer. The directory never arrived; the certificate format survived and was adapted. Version 3 (1996) added extensions, the mechanism that makes modern profiles possible, and the IETF PKIX working group produced the profile that the Internet uses, now RFC 5280. Early deployments reflected the directory heritage: distinguished names with country, organisation and common name, and revocation expressed as periodically published lists.

Revocation has the most instructive history, because each mechanism solved one problem and exposed the next. The **certificate revocation list** is a signed document, issued by the CA, naming the serial numbers of certificates that must no longer be trusted. It is simple and works offline, but it grows without bound until the revoked certificates expire, it is only as fresh as its last publication, and clients must download the whole thing to answer a question about one serial. **Delta CRLs** shipped only changes, **partitioned CRLs** split the list by distribution point, and neither achieved wide support in browsers. The **Online Certificate Status Protocol** (RFC 6960 and its predecessor) asks a responder about one serial and receives a short signed answer. It removes the size problem but adds a latency and privacy problem: the responder learns which sites each client visits, and a slow or unreachable responder stalls connections. Clients answered by **soft-failing**: if no answer arrived, they accepted the certificate, which made revocation useless against exactly the attacker who can also block the responder. **OCSP stapling** moved the query to the server, which fetches a signed, time-limited response and attaches it to the handshake, and the **Must-Staple** extension lets a certificate demand that a response be present. Browsers meanwhile abandoned live checks in favour of curated, compressed revocation sets pushed with browser updates (CRLSets in Chrome, CRLite in Firefox). Let's Encrypt announced the end of its OCSP service in favour of CRLs, with the change rolled out through 2025; the exact schedule should be confirmed (to verify). The lesson that pushed the field is that revocation checking works well in closed environments where you control both ends, and poorly across the open Internet.

The structure of authorities also matured through failures. Public CAs were compromised or misused repeatedly (DigiNotar in 2011 being the best known), and the result was a bundle of reforms: **Certificate Transparency** logs that make every publicly trusted issuance auditable, CAA DNS records that let a domain owner name its permitted issuers, mandatory validity limits that shrank from years to 398 days and are scheduled to fall further under the CA/Browser Forum ballot adopted in 2025 (the planned step-down to 47 days by 2029 should be checked against the current baseline requirements, to verify), and the practice of keeping root keys offline and issuing from intermediates. For private PKI the same ideas apply without the public audit: an offline root, one or more online intermediates scoped by purpose, name constraints to bound what each can sign, and short lifetimes.

Tooling followed the same arc. Hand-run `openssl ca` with a configuration file and flat-file database was the first Unix answer and is still the clearest way to learn what a CA does. Wrappers such as easy-rsa made the same sequence easier to repeat; the Debian handbook walks through an easy-rsa authority for OpenVPN-style deployments [src: debian-handbook 8bd606cf6d57506e]. Enterprise suites embedded a CA inside identity management: FreeIPA runs Dogtag as its certificate system, and Red Hat's documentation describes how the IdM CA generates its CRL on a schedule and how the CRL generation role is assigned to one server of a replicated deployment [src: rhel-10-docs 9ce10ce45c6c46af]. The newest wave is automation: ACME (RFC 8555) lets a client prove control of a name and receive a certificate with no human, and private CAs such as smallstep's step-ca expose the same protocol inside a company.

## Current state

**The certificate.** A certificate contains a version, a serial number unique per issuer, the signature algorithm, the issuer name, a validity interval, the subject name, the subject public key, and extensions. The extensions carry most of the meaning. *Basic Constraints* says whether the subject is a CA and how many further CA levels may sit beneath it (`pathlen`). *Key Usage* and *Extended Key Usage* restrict purpose: digital signature, key encipherment, server authentication, client authentication, code signing. *Subject Alternative Name* (SAN) lists the DNS names, IP addresses, email addresses or URIs that the certificate is valid for, and modern clients ignore the common name when a SAN exists. *Name Constraints* limits the namespaces an intermediate may issue for. *Authority Key Identifier* and *Subject Key Identifier* let a verifier find the right parent. *CRL Distribution Points* and *Authority Information Access* point to where the revocation list and the OCSP responder live, and where the issuer's own certificate can be fetched.

**The chain.** A relying party starts from a certificate it must evaluate and builds a path to a **trust anchor** it already holds, normally a self-signed root. Each link is verified by signature, validity dates, constraints and revocation status, and the verifier enforces that CA flags and path-length limits hold along the way. A server should send its own certificate and the intermediates, not the root. The Ubuntu STIG makes the requirement explicit for PAM and SSSD: path validation must construct the whole chain including status information, since without it a trust decision on an unknown certificate cannot be made [src: disa-stig 95cb2d8e767d4b9e].

**Building an authority with OpenSSL.** The minimal private PKI has four artefacts: a root key and self-signed root certificate, an intermediate key with a certificate signed by the root, and leaf certificates signed by the intermediate. With the OpenSSL command line this reduces to a handful of verbs: `openssl genpkey` creates keys, `openssl req -new` creates a certificate signing request, `openssl req -x509` creates a self-signed certificate, `openssl x509 -req` or `openssl ca` signs, and `openssl verify -CAfile` tests a chain. The extensions that matter are set either in a configuration section passed with `-extfile` or `-config`, or on the command line with `-addext`. A leaf for a web service needs `basicConstraints=CA:FALSE`, `keyUsage=digitalSignature`, `extendedKeyUsage=serverAuth` and a `subjectAltName`. An intermediate needs `basicConstraints=critical,CA:TRUE,pathlen:0` and `keyUsage=keyCertSign,cRLSign`. The `openssl ca` command adds the part that raw signing lacks: an `index.txt` database of issued certificates, a serial counter, and the `-revoke` and `-gencrl` actions that produce a CRL.

**Revocation in practice.** To revoke, the CA records the serial and a reason code (keyCompromise, cessationOfOperation, superseded and others) in its database and publishes a new CRL whose `nextUpdate` field tells clients how long the list may be cached. `openssl crl -in file -noout -text` prints one; `openssl verify -crl_check -CRLfile` makes verification require it. For OCSP, a responder either belongs to the CA (`openssl ocsp -index index.txt -rsigner ... -port 2560` serves a toy responder from the CA database, intended for testing) or is part of a CA product. A client queries with `openssl ocsp -issuer ca.pem -cert leaf.pem -url http://...` and receives good, revoked or unknown plus the response's `thisUpdate` and `nextUpdate`. On a TLS server, `ssl_stapling on` in nginx or `SSLUseStapling on` in Apache enable stapling, and `openssl s_client -status` shows whether a stapled response arrived. Red Hat's IdM documentation notes that the IdM CA regenerates its CRL every four hours by default and that the interval is set in the CA configuration after stopping the generating service [src: rhel-10-docs 3400636d0fce0949].

**Trust stores and clients.** A Linux system keeps a bundle of trusted roots. On Red Hat family systems you drop a PEM file into `/etc/pki/ca-trust/source/anchors/` and run `update-ca-trust`; on Debian family systems you place a `.crt` file under `/usr/local/share/ca-certificates/` and run `update-ca-certificates`. The result is a combined bundle (and a Java keystore) that most libraries read. Applications that ship their own store, notably Firefox, Java and Python's `certifi`, need separate treatment. For smart-card and PKI-based login, the settings live in PAM and SSSD. The `cert_policy` entries in the PAM PKCS#11 configuration can include `crl_auto`, `crl_offline` or `ocsp_on`, and the STIG rule for a local cache of revocation data makes the compliance logic explicit: if the network is unavailable, authentication must use cached revocation data, because otherwise a revoked card keeps working or a roaming user is locked out [src: compliance-as-code a079a9a49a310677]. The rule appears in an Ubuntu benchmark as well [src: disa-stig 2afbc4c7cfd13d92].

**The generalised pattern.** Every PKI, however it is implemented, reduces to the same questions: who may issue (a hierarchy with constraints), what may be issued (profiles), how a requester proves its right (enrolment, with a human, a domain challenge, or a device attestation), how long the statement lasts (lifetime), how it is withdrawn early (revocation), and how clients learn the answers (publication). The skill is choosing, for each question, an answer that the failure of the next question cannot defeat.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

**The good.** X.509 is universal. Every TLS library, every browser, every VPN, every directory, every smart-card stack and every code-signing scheme speaks it, so a single internal authority can serve all of them. Chains give delegation and containment: an intermediate compromised in one environment can be revoked without replacing the root, and a name-constrained intermediate cannot be abused to impersonate unrelated names. Because the validation algorithm is deterministic and the data are signed, verification works offline with nothing but the anchor. Short-lived certificates change the economics entirely: a certificate that lives for hours needs no revocation infrastructure, since its natural expiry is the revocation.

**The bad.** The format is intricate. ASN.1 and DER encoding, a long history of extensions and a tolerance for vendor quirks have repeatedly produced parser bugs, and different libraries disagree about corner cases such as name constraints on email addresses or the treatment of the common name. Revocation is the weakest link: CRLs are heavy, OCSP leaks browsing patterns and fails open, and stapling depends on server support. Many Linux tools do not check revocation at all by default; `curl` with OpenSSL, for example, does not consult CRLs unless explicitly told, and a library that does support checking may treat a missing answer as success (the defaults differ by library and version, to verify). Expiry is the most common outage, and it typically strikes a certificate that was issued years ago by someone who left.

**The ugly.** Key handling is where authorities die. A root key stored on a shared file server, an intermediate whose passphrase sits in a script, an `index.txt` database that was edited by hand and lost its serial counter, or a CRL signer that was never rotated are common stories. Private CAs also accumulate trust that cannot be scoped: installing a company root into every trust store means that its compromise endangers every connection that user makes, including those to unrelated external sites, unless name constraints are present and honoured, and honouring is uneven across clients (to verify for each client in use). Rotating a root is painful because each trust store, container image, Java keystore and appliance holds a copy. Finally, compliance checkers assert that revocation checking is configured but cannot show it works; a certificate that has been revoked and still logs a user in is the real test.

**Mitigations.** Keep the root offline, ideally on hardware (an HSM or a YubiKey-class token), and bring it out only to sign intermediates and the CRL. Issue leaves with short lifetimes and automate renewal so that a lapse is noticed before users do. Constrain intermediates by name and path length. Monitor expiry with a scheduled script or an exporter, and alert at a week and at a day. Publish both CRL and OCSP when clients differ, and measure whether clients fetch them. Test revocation as a drill: revoke a test certificate and confirm that the services you rely on reject it.

## Applications beyond this domain

The certificate-chain idea is reused wherever a party must act on a claim from someone it has never met. Code and package signing use X.509 or its relatives: Authenticode and the kernel module signing keys discussed in the Secure Boot topic chain to a vendor certificate, while RPM and APT rely on bare OpenPGP keys instead and so face the same distribution and revocation questions without the hierarchy. SSH certificates, which are not X.509 but follow the same model, let one CA key sign short-lived user and host keys and replace sprawling `authorized_keys` files. Service meshes and SPIFFE issue workload certificates whose identity is a URI in the SAN and whose lifetime is minutes. Email signing with S/MIME, document signing, mobile device management enrolment, IEEE 802.1X network access with EAP-TLS, and IoT device identity (IEEE 802.1AR) all follow it. Certificate Transparency's append-only logs have been adopted as the pattern for other transparency systems, such as software signing transparency in Sigstore's Rekor. In every case the same trade-offs recur: a trust anchor that is hard to change, an expiry period chosen to balance outage risk against exposure, and a revocation story that must work when the network does not.

## Examples

**A working private CA.** An operator wants TLS between internal services. They generate an offline root with a 20-year life and `CA:TRUE`, sign an intermediate with `pathlen:0`, a 5-year life and a name constraint permitting only `.internal.example`, and issue leaves for 90 days. The root certificate is added to the trust store on each host with `update-ca-trust` or `update-ca-certificates`. `openssl verify -CAfile root.pem -untrusted intermediate.pem leaf.pem` returns OK, and `openssl x509 -noout -ext subjectAltName,extendedKeyUsage` confirms the leaf's fields.

**An enterprise CA through FreeIPA.** In an IdM domain, `ipa-server-install --setup-ca` creates Dogtag as the CA, hosts get certificates through `certmonger`, which also renews them before expiry, and the IdM server generates the CRL on one designated replica. A failure mode appears when that replica is retired without moving the CRL generation role: the published CRL stops updating, its `nextUpdate` passes, and clients that insist on a fresh list begin to reject every certificate at once. The role is moved with `ipa-crlgen-manage`, as the Red Hat guide describes [src: rhel-10-docs 9ce10ce45c6c46af].

**A failing example: the missing intermediate.** A web server presents only its leaf certificate. Browsers on desktops succeed because they cache intermediates or fetch them through the Authority Information Access URL; a Python script using `requests`, a Java client or `curl` on a minimal container fails with an unable-to-get-local-issuer error. The fix is on the server, which must present the full chain, and `openssl s_client -connect host:443 -showcerts` is the diagnostic that shows what is actually sent.

**A failing example: fail-open revocation.** An administrator revokes a stolen employee card and publishes the CRL. The employee's card still logs in at a remote office, because the PAM configuration had `cert_policy = ca,signature` without a revocation check and the CRL was never fetched. The remedy is `crl_auto` or `ocsp_on` with a local cache, as the compliance rule requires, and a test that proves the revoked card is rejected.

**Short-lived certificates.** A team replaces long-lived server certificates with ACME-issued certificates valid for seven days, renewed daily by `certbot` or `lego` against an internal ACME CA. Revocation becomes a non-issue for most cases; the new risk is dependence on the ACME endpoint, so monitoring and a manual issuance path are retained.

## Related problems and ideas

Alternatives to the CA hierarchy address its cost. The **web of trust** of OpenPGP has no central authority but depends on users to certify each other, which scales poorly outside communities. **Trust on first use** (SSH host keys without certificates) is cheap and vulnerable at first contact. **DANE** publishes keys or certificate hashes in DNSSEC-signed DNS records, moving trust to the DNS hierarchy; it is used for SMTP between mail servers but little elsewhere on the web. **Pinning** fixes an expected key in the client and fails when keys rotate, so it was withdrawn from browsers. **Key transparency** and **Certificate Transparency** audit issuers rather than eliminating them.

Related technical problems include proving possession and attestation of keys (TPM-based and hardware-backed enrolment), certificate lifecycle management at scale (inventory, ownership, and renewal across thousands of endpoints), and post-quantum transition. Signature algorithms in X.509 will need replacing, and hybrid certificates that carry both a classical and a post-quantum signature are under standardisation in the IETF LAMPS working group (status to verify). Large-scale revocation has produced proposals such as CRLite's Bloom-filter cascades and the short-lived-certificate alternative that dispenses with it.

## Active research

Work in this area concentrates on four questions. First, how to make revocation reliable at Internet scale: browser vendors maintain compact filter schemes, and the CA/Browser Forum and individual CAs are moving from OCSP towards CRLs partitioned by shard. Second, how to shorten lifetimes without breaking operations, which drives automation research and standards for ACME extensions such as profile selection and renewal information (ARI). Third, how to migrate to post-quantum signatures; the open questions are certificate size, hybrid composition and the effect on constrained protocols. Fourth, how to verify implementations: certificate path validation has a long record of divergent behaviour between libraries, and differential-testing projects keep finding new disagreements, including on name constraints and on malformed encodings.

On Linux specifically, the open practical questions are how to scope a private root so that its compromise has bounded impact, how containers should inherit and rotate trust stores, and how to report revocation-check results from PAM and SSSD in a way that compliance scanners can verify rather than assume.

## Future direction

Expect shorter public certificate lifetimes through 2026 to 2029, which makes automation a prerequisite instead of an option (schedule to verify against the current CA/Browser Forum baseline). Expect CRLs to become the principal public revocation mechanism and OCSP to be retired by major issuers, with stapling kept where servers still use it. Expect private PKI to adopt the same pattern: ACME-based issuance, workload identity through SPIFFE-style URIs, and certificates measured in hours. Post-quantum and hybrid certificates will appear first in closed environments where both ends can be upgraded together. For the Linux administrator, the durable skills are the ones that do not depend on the product: reading certificate fields, building and verifying a chain, understanding what a client does when revocation data are missing, and treating expiry and key custody as the primary risks.
