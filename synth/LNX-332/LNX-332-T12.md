# Upstreaming to KVM and QEMU: getting a change accepted

## Define the problem

A fix that works on your machine is not yet part of the project. Both KVM and QEMU are maintained by volunteers and company engineers who review by mail, apply strict expectations about style and testing, and treat every line as something they will carry for years. A contributor has to find the right place and the right people, split the work into reviewable steps, explain the reason and the risk, accept criticism, and iterate. Failing at any step costs months, or leaves the contributor with a private fork to maintain, and a fork of a fast-moving kernel subsystem is a heavy burden.

What makes the problem interesting is that the obstacle is rarely technical. A correct patch that is badly explained, too large, untested or sent to the wrong list will wait or be refused. This essay follows the rules that grew up in the two projects, sets out the workflow in order, and explains what a reviewer is looking for, so that a first contribution can be shaped to succeed.

## History and approaches

### Two projects, two cultures

KVM is part of the Linux kernel and follows the kernel's process. Patches go by email to the `kvm` list and to the lists of the architecture involved, addressed to the maintainers that the script `get_maintainer.pl` identifies. They are collected in maintainer trees and merged during the next merge window. The kernel's own documents describe all of this in detail. QEMU, developed as a separate project, also reviews on mailing lists (`qemu-devel`), with a patch-series workflow and a file that names the maintainers of each area. QEMU later added forge-based merge requests to part of its flow, and the current policy should be checked in its developer documentation.

### Shared rules

Both projects require a `Signed-off-by` line, which certifies under the Developer Certificate of Origin that the contributor has the right to submit the work. Each has its own coding style. The kernel uses tabs and a documented style with a checker, `checkpatch.pl`; QEMU uses four spaces, a documented style and its own checking script. Over time both projects added continuous integration, test requirements (selftests for KVM, qtest or functional tests for QEMU), and bots that run builds and report. The pattern behind these rules is the same. A project that depends on thousands of contributors cannot rely on trust, so it relies on small, checkable steps, public discussion, and a trail that shows who agreed to what.

## Current state

### The workflow, step by step

1. **Research first.** Read the documents on submitting patches that come with the tree, search the list archives for earlier attempts and for discussions of the same topic, and read the maintainers file for the area. A change that has been rejected before is a change to rethink, not to resend.
2. **Make the change small and complete.** One logical change per patch. For a larger piece of work, send a series ordered so that each patch builds and works on its own, with no unrelated cleanups mixed in. Add a test: a selftest or unit test for KVM, a qtest or a functional test for QEMU.
3. **Write the commit message.** A subject with a prefix for the subsystem (`KVM: x86: ...`, `target/i386: ...`, `hw/virtio: ...`) and a short imperative summary. A body that says what the problem is, why this fix was chosen, what the alternatives were, and how it was tested. References to related commits, such as a `Fixes:` tag with a commit hash for a bug fix, and a `Signed-off-by:` line.
4. **Check it.** Run the style checker, build with warnings enabled, run the project's tests, and send the series to yourself first to see how it renders.
5. **Send it.** Use `git format-patch` and `git send-email`, or tools such as `b4`, with a cover letter for a series. Address the maintainers directly and copy the relevant lists.
6. **Respond to review.** Reply inline, in plain text, quoting the lines you answer. Accept a point or argue it with evidence. Send a new version (v2, v3) with a changelog below the three-dash line, and do not repost faster than reviewers can read.
7. **Follow through.** After acceptance, watch for reports from bots and for regressions that others find, because the patch is now yours in the eyes of the project.

### What reviewers look for

Reviewers ask a consistent set of questions. Is it correct on every architecture and configuration? Does it remain compatible with live migration and saved state? Is any guest-controlled value used without checking? Does it add user-visible behaviour without documenting it? Is the programming interface as small as it can be? Can it be maintained by someone who did not write it? Is there a test, and is the explanation clear? For QEMU there are also device-model conventions (state descriptions, properties, compatibility with machine types); for KVM, the promise that the user-space interface does not break.

### The generalised pattern

Small reviewable steps, with a test and a rationale in each, sent as structured text, with patience in the follow-up. The same pattern governs any change that others must accept into code they will maintain.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

**The good.** The process is transparent. Every comment is public, a patch is judged on its technical merits, the style tools catch much before a human reads a line, and maintainers often give detailed, instructive feedback to newcomers.

**The bad.** Review capacity is limited, so patches wait, sometimes for weeks. Email workflows are hard to learn, and inline replies require discipline, because a badly quoted reply is easy to misread. Style rules differ between the two projects, so a habit formed in one is a mistake in the other.

**The ugly.** A patch can stall without a single reply. Reviewers sometimes disagree with each other, and the contributor is left to find the compromise. Large features take years and many versions, and maintainers may be unreachable for a stretch. Tone can be blunt in ways that discourage newcomers, and a change that touches several areas needs agreement from several maintainers, which multiplies every delay.

## Applications beyond this domain

- **Any large open-source project with mailing-list review**, including the kernel as a whole, the GNU compiler collection, Git and many toolchains, works in much the same way.
- **Pull-request projects** apply the same expectations (small changes, tests, rationale) through different tools, so the habits carry over.
- **Corporate engineering teams** benefit from the same discipline: small changes, written reasons and tests make internal review faster too.
- **Standards bodies and RFC processes** use iterative public review, with the same need to answer every comment.
- **Documentation and translation projects** run on the same principles, since a large body of text also needs small changes and a record of who agreed.

## Examples

**A one-line fix, in full.** A KVM ioctl uses a guest-supplied index without checking its range. The patch has the subject `KVM: x86: ...` naming the area, a body that describes the failing input and what happens (an out-of-range read), a `Fixes:` tag naming the commit that introduced the code, and a selftest that passes the offending value and expects an error. The reviewer can read the problem, the fix and the proof in a single screen, and that is why such patches are accepted quickly.

**A QEMU device fix.** A bounds check is added on a register write in a device model, with a qtest that writes the offending value and asserts that the device state does not change. The test makes the fix permanent: any later change that reintroduces the problem will break the build.

**A failing one.** A series of five patches in which patch 2 breaks the build and patch 5 undoes part of patch 3. Reviewers ask for a reorganisation before they read the content, because a series that does not build at every step cannot be bisected later. The lab for this topic flags several such mechanical problems automatically, which saves the contributor a round of review.

### Opposing approaches

- **Maintain a fork.** Keeping changes private avoids review entirely and gives full control of timing. The cost grows with every upstream release, and the fork's own bugs become the owner's alone. Teams that start this way usually end up upstreaming anyway, at greater cost.
- **Forge-based review.** Pull requests with automated checks, inline comments and visible state are easier for newcomers than mail. Their critics note that the review record is tied to one host and that long discussion threads are harder to follow than email. Several projects are moving part of their flow in this direction.
- **Corporate-sponsored maintainers.** Some argue that maintainer time should be funded, since volunteer attention is the bottleneck. Others reply that funding changes whose changes get priority. Both points are fair, and the debate has no settled answer.

## Active research

Work in this area includes tooling that automates mechanical review checks and bot-assisted triage, better search of past discussions so contributors can find prior art, and studies of what makes patches succeed or stall, often drawn from the public archives. The state of each should be checked against recent conference talks on kernel and QEMU development processes.

## Future direction

Expect more bot-assisted review, forge integration running alongside email, and stricter test requirements for new features. For a first-time contributor, the practical advice has been stable for years and will likely remain so: read the submission documents, send a small and well-explained change with a test to the right people, answer every comment, and be patient.
