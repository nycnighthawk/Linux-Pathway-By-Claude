# Planning multi-week fleet rollouts: waves, gates, stop conditions and communication

## Define the problem

A weekly patch cycle can be run on rings and automation, as the earlier topics describe. Some changes, however, are too large for that routine: a major operating system upgrade (an in-place move from one Enterprise Linux release to the next, or a migration to image mode), a kernel or firmware campaign that touches every server and needs reboots, a rotation of repository signing keys on every client, a change of configuration management agent, a move to a new repository or registry, or the replacement of a monitoring or security agent. These are **campaigns**: they affect most of the fleet, they are not repeatable on a weekly timetable, they take weeks or months, they involve many teams, and a mistake in the plan costs far more than a mistake in one patch run. The task of this topic is to plan and run such a campaign, which is partly technical, partly project management and mostly about deciding in advance what will make you stop.

The topic is a "plan it" exercise: the skills involved are not commands but documents and decisions. A good plan answers eight questions. What exactly is changing and why now? Which hosts are in scope, and how do we know the list is right? In what order and in what size of waves will they move? What are the gates between waves, and what conditions stop the campaign? Who is on call, who approves, and how are service owners told? What is the rollback and how long does it stay possible? How is progress measured and reported? What do we do with the machines that cannot move?

Typical failure modes: the inventory is wrong, so a quarter of the hosts are discovered halfway through; the first wave is too large to be safe or too unrepresentative to teach anything; the plan has dates but no stop conditions, so the campaign continues through warning signs because the schedule says so; service owners learn of their window the day before; the maintenance windows of different teams collide with business peaks or freezes; the rollback exists on paper but the snapshot was deleted after a week; the long tail (the last five percent of hosts) is never finished and remains permanently exposed; and the campaign ends with no review, so the next one repeats the errors.

## History and approaches

Large coordinated changes are as old as computing, and the discipline for them grew from three sources. **Release engineering and operations research** produced the concepts of staged deployment, change windows, freezes and rollback plans, formalised in frameworks such as ITIL change management, with its change advisory boards, standard and emergency change categories and post-implementation review. **Site reliability engineering** contributed *progressive rollout with measured risk*: begin with a very small share, watch service-level indicators, double the share if healthy, and stop automatically if not. The canonical software-delivery guidance in Kubernetes documents the same pattern for applications: deploy a canary version beside the stable one, route a small fraction of traffic to it, and roll back by removing the canary if it misbehaves [src: kubernetes-docs ddd68a5a094b39b0] and [src: kubernetes-docs 768a6c9214be1b23]. **Project management** contributed work breakdown, dependency charts, RACI roles, risk registers and burn-down tracking.

For operating systems, there is a particular history of painful major-version moves. Reinstallation was the norm until in-place upgrade tools matured: Debian's `dist-upgrade` has long supported release-to-release upgrades; Red Hat's Leapp-based in-place upgrade for Enterprise Linux (from release 7 to 8, 8 to 9, and 9 to 10) arrived as a supported path later, with strict preconditions. The RHEL upgrade documentation for systems managed by Satellite makes the planning dependency plain: the content for both the source and target releases must be synchronised and made available in the view and key that each host uses, or the upgrade is blocked [src: rhel-10-docs 0454e83d367f2bf6]. Operators learned that the technical upgrade is the smaller part of the project; the larger part is discovering what runs on each host and whether it will work afterwards. The alternative, rebuild by replacement (bring up a new host from an image and move the workload), became attractive wherever the workload is stateless or automated, and image mode formalises it. The documentation on automatic updates in image mode shows how the update stream itself can be controlled by timers and tags, which makes the campaign's unit a registry tag promoted through waves [src: rhel-10-docs 263909235650002e].

Another tradition is **virtualisation and cluster rolling updates**. In Kubernetes, a StatefulSet's rolling update goes through pods in reverse ordinal order with a *partition* value, which lets an operator update only pods at or above a number and hold the rest back, so the rollout can be staged by hand [src: kubernetes-docs a16b1a7f29fe382c]. KubeVirt documents rollout strategies and their limits for virtual machines, such as whether VMs are live-migrated or restarted when the underlying components change [src: kubevirt-user-guide a35497a2a63d351e]. These show the common idea: update in an order that preserves capacity and quorum, in controlled steps, with a place to stop.

## Current state

**Write the one-page charter.** Before anything else, write down the objective (a named release on every host by a stated date), the reason (end of support, vulnerability class, cost), the owner, the sponsor who can say stop or go, the scope, the non-goals and the success measures. A charter prevents scope creep and lets people outside the project understand what is happening to their hosts. Include the *drop-dead date* after which the old state is unsupported, because it determines how much risk the plan may take with schedule.

**Know the fleet.** The inventory is the foundation. Combine sources (configuration management, the content server's host list, cloud APIs, hypervisor lists, network scans, DHCP and certificate records) and reconcile; hosts in only one source are suspects. For each host, record at least owner, service and tier, environment, location and failure domain, operating system version, installed third-party software, whether it is redundant, whether it has special constraints (certification, freeze periods, appliance) and a contact. Gaps in this data are the project's first task, and the first reported number is the *count of hosts with no owner*.

**Classify by effort and risk.** Group hosts by what the change involves for them: no impact (standard stateless nodes), reboot required, service restart with brief outage, application compatibility testing required, vendor involvement required, cannot be done (retire or replace). The classes decide the waves. Treat the "application owner must test" class as a separate stream with its own deadline set early, because it takes the longest.

**Design the waves.** Waves are cohorts of hosts that move together, defined by risk and by dependency. A usual shape is wave 0 (the project team's own hosts and lab), wave 1 (a few representative production hosts, one of each major class), then waves growing geometrically (for example 1 %, 2 %, 5 %, 10 %, 20 %, then the rest in batches the system can absorb). Growth should stop being geometric at the point where a failure would exceed what the on-call team can handle in parallel: capacity of the *humans and systems that recover* limits wave size as much as risk. Honour dependencies: update the systems that others depend on in the order that minimises incompatibility (typically consumers first for protocol upgrades, or providers first for backward-compatible changes, depending on the case), keep redundancy intact (never both nodes of a pair in the same window; maintain quorum; one availability zone at a time) and respect calendars (month-end, sales events, freezes).

**Gates and stop conditions.** Each wave has an *entry gate* (the previous wave completed with no open severity-1 or -2 issues, the soak time passed, the checklist for the wave is complete) and an *exit check* (all hosts healthy, metrics within thresholds, no unexplained alerts). The plan lists **stop conditions** in advance: for example, more than one failed host in a wave; any data-loss event; a service-level breach attributed to the change; a failure mode that has no known fix; the rollback tested and found not to work. A stop condition must name who can invoke it (anyone on the on-call team) and who can lift it (the sponsor, on advice from the owner). The distinction between *pause* (hold the current state, investigate) and *abort* (return to the old state) should be defined.

**Prepare the rollback.** For in-place operating system upgrades, rollback usually means restoring a storage snapshot, a virtual machine snapshot or a backup, or reinstalling from a known image. For each class of host, decide which mechanism applies, test it on the wave-0 hosts, and check that the rollback window (the time during which the mechanism is valid) is long enough to cover the soak period of the wave. Snapshots of virtual machines that are kept for weeks degrade performance and consume space (the virtualisation courses cover this), so set a deletion date and a person responsible. Where the change cannot be undone (a data format migration, a key rotation after the old key is retired) the plan needs a *point of no return* marker, a separate approval to cross it and a record of when it was crossed.

**Automate, then supervise.** Use an orchestration tool (Ansible with AWX or Automation Controller workflows, Satellite remote execution, Rundeck, a pipeline) to run each wave from a host list, and keep the runs idempotent so that re-running a failed batch is safe. Automation should implement the gate and stop conditions: refuse to start a wave whose entry gate is false, and stop on the failure threshold. A *control tower* view shows wave status, per-host state (pending, in progress, done, failed, excepted), health metrics and issues. Supervision is a job: assign a "campaign operator" for each wave who watches the dashboard and owns decisions in real time.

**Communicate.** Prepare templates and a calendar. Service owners get notice of their window early (two weeks or more for large changes), a reminder, a start and an end message, and a post-change summary. State clearly what they must do (test, approve, be on call), what will change, how long the outage may last and how to reach the project. Keep a single place with current status and a single channel for incidents. Executives need a weekly summary with percentage complete against the plan, issues and risks, decisions needed and the forecast date.

**Staff and cover.** Work that spans weeks needs rested people. Assign on-call per wave, avoid scheduling large waves on the days before holidays, and make sure that the people who can fix an unexpected failure (database, network, application) are reachable during each window. A campaign should have at least two people who know the full plan.

**Measure.** Report completion as the share of in-scope hosts at the target state, but also the *remaining risk-weighted exposure* (the number of hosts still on the old state and their criticality), the age of the oldest unconverted host, the failure and rollback rates per wave, the time to recover from failures and the count of exceptions with owner and expiry. A burn-down chart compared with plan reveals slippage early. Do not let the percentage hide the tail.

**The long tail.** The last 5 to 10 percent of hosts always take most of the effort: appliances, hosts with a vendor application, systems owned by someone who has left, and machines under change freeze. Handle them with a standing process, not heroics: an exceptions register (host, reason, owner, compensating controls such as isolation or extra monitoring, review date), a decision on each (upgrade, replace, isolate, retire) with a deadline, and escalation to the sponsor when a deadline passes. Retire the old state's infrastructure only when the exceptions list is empty or each remaining item is isolated.

**Close and learn.** At the end, run a review: what the plan assumed, what happened, what stopped the campaign (if anything), how accurate the inventory was, which gates and stops were useful, and what would shorten the next campaign. Update the runbooks, the inventory process and the templates. Archive the evidence (host lists, wave logs, approvals) for the audit trail.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

**The good.** A campaign plan with waves, gates and stop conditions turns a frightening change into a sequence of small, reversible steps with learning at each stage. Early waves find the problems cheaply; geometric growth gives confidence where it matters; stop conditions protect the business; communication keeps the organisation on side. The artefacts (inventory, classification, exceptions register) have lasting value beyond the project.

**The bad.** Planning takes time that urgent drivers (an end-of-support date, a vulnerability) may not allow; the plan depends on an inventory that is usually incomplete; human capacity and calendar constraints dominate the schedule more than machine speed; and the long tail is expensive. Over-engineered governance can slow a rollout so much that the exposure it was meant to remove persists. Many stop conditions are subjective, and teams under schedule pressure may argue them away.

**The ugly.** Campaigns fail through social causes: no sponsor with the authority to say stop; an owner who treats the date as fixed whatever happens; a first wave chosen because it was easy, which teaches nothing; failures hidden because reporting them is penalised; and a quiet decision to skip the remaining hosts, leaving a permanent unmanaged tail that will be the first thing an attacker finds.

**Assumptions.** The wave sizes, notice periods and thresholds are common practice and illustrative, not standards. They should be adjusted to your fleet, tooling and risk tolerance (to verify).

## Applications beyond this domain

The same planning applies to database engine upgrades, network device software upgrades, Kubernetes version upgrades across many clusters, firmware campaigns, certificate authority migrations, identity-provider changes, SaaS migrations and any large change to shared infrastructure. In all of them the questions are the same: scope, order, gates, stops, rollback, communication, tail. The Kubernetes documents on canary deployments and StatefulSet partitions are small, concrete instances of wave design with an explicit rollback [src: kubernetes-docs d6f9535056884aea].

## Examples

**Example 1: a 6-week OS upgrade campaign for 600 hosts.** Week 0: charter, inventory reconciliation, classification (350 stateless nodes, 120 services with a reboot, 80 needing application owner tests, 30 appliances or exceptions, 20 to retire). Week 1: wave 0 (12 lab and project hosts), rollback tested. Week 2: wave 1 (12 representative production hosts) and soak. Weeks 3 to 5: waves at 5 %, 10 %, 20 % and 30 %, one failure domain per night. Week 6: remaining stream and exceptions review; the long tail is registered with owners and dates.

**Example 2: stop conditions.** (1) More than one failed host in a wave of up to 20, or more than 5 % in a larger wave. (2) Any sustained service-level breach in a service touched by the wave within the soak. (3) A rollback that did not complete. (4) Any security finding in the new state. Pause and abort are defined; the sponsor lifts a stop after a written root cause.

**Example 3: a key rotation campaign.** Rotating a repository signing key on 2,000 clients follows the overlap plan from the signing topic: deliver the new key to all clients (wave tracking: percentage of clients with the key, from an inventory query), only then start signing with it; remove the old key only when the percentage reaches the agreed level and the remaining clients are on the exceptions register.

**Example 4: a control-tower table.** Columns: wave, hosts planned, started, completed, failed, excepted, health status, gate state, operator. A glance shows whether the next wave may start.

**Example 5: image-mode rollout.** Hosts follow tags; the campaign promotes digest D from `candidate` to `stable` by waves of hosts switched with `bootc switch`, with the previous digest retained as the rollback target for the whole campaign.

**Worked failure: the schedule that could not stop.** A campaign had a fixed end date set by the end of vendor support. In week 3 a second host in a wave failed to boot after the upgrade because of a driver issue. No stop condition had been written, the date pressure was strong, and the wave continued; three more hosts failed over the weekend. After the incident the team added the stop conditions above, put a driver check into the wave's entry gate, and the sponsor agreed that a two-week slip was acceptable against a data-centre outage.

## Active research

Open questions include how to forecast campaign duration from fleet data, how to pick representative early waves automatically from workload telemetry, how to use automated canary analysis for infrastructure changes as well as for application releases, and how to model the human capacity of on-call teams as a constraint in schedule planning. Industry reports on migration programmes repeatedly identify inventory quality and ownership gaps as the main causes of delay, which suggests that improvements in discovery tooling would pay more than faster upgrade tools (to verify).

## Future direction

Expect more campaigns to be expressed as promotions of immutable artefacts through waves of hosts, with the orchestration and gates in code and the human work concentrated on exceptions. Expect continuous discovery to keep inventories current, and expect policy engines to enforce gates and stop conditions. The role of the planner will remain: deciding what could go wrong, what will show it, who can stop the work and how everyone finds out.
