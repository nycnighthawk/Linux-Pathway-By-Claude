# What a Linux distribution is, and the licences that make it possible

## Define the problem

A kernel on its own does nothing a person can use. To get from a program that manages memory, processes and devices to a machine that serves web pages, you need a compiler, a C library, a shell, an init system, hundreds of utilities, an installer, a way to fetch updates and someone who promises to keep all of it consistent for years. The first problem is therefore one of assembly and upkeep: who chooses the parts, builds them together, tests the combination and answers when a security hole is found.

The second problem is legal and social. Software that thousands of strangers contribute to, and that companies redistribute for money, needs terms everyone can rely on. Without such terms a contributor cannot know whether a competitor may take the work private, a company cannot know whether shipping a product exposes it to a lawsuit, and a user cannot know whether the tool they depend on can be taken away. The gap this topic fills is the gap between having downloaded something that runs and understanding what you may do with it, what you owe in return, and who stands behind it.

This slice stays at the level an administrator needs. It covers what a distribution consists of, how distributions differ, what the main licence families demand, and how to find out what is installed on a machine and under which terms. It does not cover legal advice, patent law in depth or the details of any one packaging tool, which later topics treat.

## History and approaches

The story begins before Linux. In the early 1980s source code for operating systems was increasingly sold under restrictive terms. Richard Stallman announced the GNU project in 1983 with the aim of a complete, freely modifiable Unix-like system, and set up the Free Software Foundation in 1985 to fund it [web: gnu.org]. His central move was legal rather than technical: a licence that used copyright to guarantee freedoms instead of withholding them. The first General Public License appeared in 1989, a second version in 1991, and a third in 2007 after long public drafting. The freedoms behind it are four: to run the program for any purpose, to study and change it, to redistribute copies, and to distribute modified versions. The first of these was numbered zero because it was added after the other three were already in circulation [web: gnu.org].

By 1991 GNU had nearly everything except a working kernel. The Berkeley Software Distribution lineage offered another route, a permissive licence that asked only for credit, but it spent the early 1990s under a legal cloud from a dispute with the owner of the original Unix code. In that gap a Finnish student, Linus Torvalds, released the first Linux kernel in 1991. He moved it to the GPL version 2 the following year, and the combination of the GNU userland with that kernel gave the world its first fully free Unix-like system. The Rocky Linux administrator guide still tells the story in the same shape: Unix, then the free-software movement, then a Finnish kernel [src: rocky-docs 68efee00a9dffb1c].

Distributions appeared almost at once, because assembling the parts was the next obvious pain. Slackware and Debian both began in 1993. Debian's founder, Ian Murdock, wrote a manifesto that asked for an openly developed system, and the project later set down a social contract and a set of free-software guidelines to decide which software could enter its main archive [src: debian-handbook d5ffd79784563fa3] [src: debian-handbook b43a76b237bcce09]. Red Hat followed in 1995 with a commercial model built on packaging, support and certification rather than on selling licences, and SUSE grew in Germany in parallel. In 1997 an essay contrasting cathedral-style and bazaar-style development gave the movement an argument aimed at businesses, and in 1998 the Open Source Initiative adopted a definition whose ten criteria were derived from the Debian guidelines. The two vocabularies have coexisted ever since: "free software" stresses user freedom as an ethical goal, and "open source" stresses the practical benefits of shared development [web: opensource.org].

Several approaches to licensing then settled into a spectrum. Permissive licences such as the BSD and MIT families allow almost anything, including closing the modified code, as long as the notice is kept. Weak copyleft licences such as the LGPL and the Mozilla licence require that changes to the licensed files stay open but let other code link to them. Strong copyleft, the GPL, requires that a distributed work containing GPL code be offered under the GPL with its source. Network copyleft, the AGPL of 2007, closes a gap the GPL left: a company can run modified GPL software as a hosted service without ever distributing it, so the AGPL extends the source obligation to users who interact over a network.

Not every attempt worked. Early commercial Linux vendors found that selling a free product is hard, and several faded. Some companies tried custom "source-available" licences to protect hosted services and met a backlash. The 2020s produced a visible cycle of such changes and forks, covered below.

The emerging pattern is a division of labour. The kernel and core tools are a shared commons under copyleft. Distributions package that commons and add a policy about what to include, how long to support it, and how to deliver updates. Users choose a distribution as much for its policy as for its software.

## Current state

A modern distribution is a set of components with a clear structure. At the bottom is the Linux kernel, with its loadable modules and a firmware bundle. Above it sits the core userland: the C library, the shell and the standard command-line tools, most of which still come from the GNU project, although some distributions use lighter replacements for small systems. The init system and service manager, now systemd on nearly every mainstream server distribution, starts and supervises everything else. A package manager and one or more repositories deliver software, with signing keys proving that a package came from the distributor. An installer lays the system down, and a release process decides when a snapshot becomes a supported version.

How the parts work together is mostly a matter of conventions that the distribution enforces. The package manager records which files belong to which package and which other packages are required. Repositories are organised into channels, such as a stable stream that changes slowly and a development stream where new versions arrive first; Debian's handbook describes how a package moves from its unstable channel through testing into stable as bugs are cleared [src: debian-handbook a2785fe361b947d1]. Each release carries a support window, and the length of that window is the main product difference between distributions.

Three families dominate server work. The Debian family, which includes Ubuntu, uses the deb format and the apt tools, and favours a community-governed stable release. The Red Hat family uses rpm and dnf, and splits into the commercial Red Hat Enterprise Linux, the community-driven Fedora where changes land first, and the rebuilds and downstream projects that track RHEL. SUSE has its own commercial and community lines. Outside these families are rolling-release distributions such as Arch, which ship current software continuously, and minimal or immutable images aimed at containers and appliances.

On the licensing side, the practical map is simple. The kernel is licensed under GPL version 2 only, and its source tree labels each file with a machine-readable identifier in the SPDX style. The GNU userland is mostly GPL or LGPL. Most language runtimes, web servers, databases and libraries come under permissive or weak-copyleft terms. A distribution is a collection of independent works, so it is not one licence but hundreds, and the distributor's duty is to pass on source and notices for each one. Redistributing a derivative of a GPL component, for example a rebuilt installer or a modified kernel, triggers the duty to provide the corresponding source; merely running or using the software triggers nothing.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

The good is plain. Because the licences guarantee that anyone can read, fix and rebuild the software, a customer is never locked to one vendor's survival. The security community can audit code, and a government compliance catalogue even names source inspectability as the main security benefit of open-source software [src: nist-sp800-53 9ea3c2be2252203e]. A bug fixed once benefits every distribution that carries the component. Competition between distributions has produced a wide range of life-cycle choices, from rolling releases to decade-long support.

The bad is mostly about friction and ambiguity. Licence compatibility is real: GPL version 2 and GPL version 3 code cannot always be combined, and the CDDL used by the ZFS filesystem cannot be shipped in the mainline kernel, which is why ZFS on Linux lives outside it and why Docker's own documentation hedges its advice about the matching storage driver [src: docker-docs 11b1d5449b210dc3]. Fragmentation is a cost: package names, file locations and defaults differ across families, so knowledge and automation do not transfer cleanly. Compliance takes effort, because a product shipping hundreds of components has hundreds of notices to carry and source offers to honour. Some of this is also a matter of precise vocabulary. Using the software and distributing it are different acts, and the obligations differ.

The ugly is the set of cases where goodwill and law strain. Binary firmware blobs and proprietary drivers sit uneasily inside a free kernel; the kernel marks itself as "tainted" when a non-free module is loaded, which tells maintainers not to chase bugs they cannot inspect. Companies that built businesses on openly licensed code have, on several occasions, relicensed to block cloud competitors, and the community responded with forks: a database company's server-side licence in 2018, a search company's change in 2021, an infrastructure-automation vendor's business licence in 2023 that led to the OpenTofu fork, and a key-value store's change in 2024 that led to the Valkey fork. In 2023 Red Hat limited public access to RHEL source, which prompted rebuild distributions to adapt and some vendors to form a new consortium for shared sources. Whatever one thinks of each decision, they show that a permissive or copyleft licence on the code does not bind the future behaviour of the copyright holder for new versions. A related, quieter ugliness is attribution drift: large vendored trees and containers can carry components whose licences nobody recorded.

## Applications beyond this domain

The same legal-technical idea, using copyright to attach conditions that travel with the work, recurs far outside operating systems. Creative Commons applies it to text, images and teaching material; several of the corpora behind this very curriculum are published under share-alike terms, which require derivatives to stay under the same licence. Open hardware licences use the structure for circuit designs. Scientific software and datasets increasingly use licences that mix openness with credit requirements.

The packaging model carries over too. Language ecosystems such as Python, Rust and JavaScript are distributions of a kind: a registry, signed or checksummed artefacts, a resolver that assembles a consistent set, and a release cadence. Container base images are small distributions whose maintainers make the same policy choices about channels and support. The governance model, a foundation or a company that stewards a commons while contributors keep their copyright, is the template for projects across cloud-native computing. Regulators have begun to treat this pattern as a supply-chain topic: software bills of materials list every component and its licence, and recent European legislation on product cyber-security assigns obligations to organisations that steward open-source software.

## Examples

A first practical check is identification. The file that names the running distribution and its version is a small text file in the system configuration directory, and the package manager can list every installed package with its declared licence. On a Red Hat family host, one query format prints a package's name and licence field for every package at once; on a Debian family host, each package carries a copyright file under the documentation directory, and that file is the authoritative licence statement. Comparing the two shows how the same duty is met in two conventions.

A second example is the kernel itself. Every loadable module declares its own licence, and the module information tool prints it. A module whose declared licence is not a GPL-compatible one causes the kernel's taint flag to be set, which can be read from a virtual file in the process information tree. An administrator who sees that flag set after loading a vendor driver now knows both what happened and why a kernel developer might decline to debug the crash.

A failing example is a derived work shipped without its source. A company that modifies a GPL-licensed network daemon, bakes it into an appliance image and sells the appliance, but offers no way to obtain the corresponding source, is in breach of the licence. The usual cure is a written source offer or a published tree, and projects such as the Software Freedom Conservancy have long pursued such cases through discussion first and litigation as a last resort. A parallel mistake is the reverse: assuming that internal use or private modification triggers an obligation, when the licence obliges nothing until a copy leaves the organisation.

A last example concerns the source-provenance rules that projects write for themselves. The QEMU project, which is itself GPL-licensed, publishes rules about what code it may accept, including that generated files must come with the preferred editable source [src: qemu-docs b680ef14c53f1a7c]. Projects this size need such rules because a single imported file with an incompatible licence can poison a release.

## Related problems and ideas

Several neighbouring problems are worth naming. Contributor licence agreements and the developer certificate of origin address the question of who may contribute what; the kernel uses a lightweight sign-off line instead of a signed agreement. Trademark policy is a separate lever: a project can allow everyone to rebuild the code while still controlling the use of its name, which is how rebuild distributions can exist alongside the vendors they track. Software patents are a persistent threat that the third GPL version tries to address with explicit patent terms. Dual licensing, where the same code is available under a copyleft licence and a commercial one, funds some projects, and a well-known database followed that route, with a community fork emerging when its ownership changed [src: rocky-docs f1a1379afbb3c4ab].

Ideas for improvement are mostly about making the facts machine-checkable. Machine-readable licence identifiers in every source file, tools that scan a tree and produce a bill of materials, and reproducible builds that let a third party confirm that a binary matches its source are all attempts to turn a trust relationship into a verification step. Another idea is to separate ethical goals from distribution terms by using licences that restrict harmful uses; such licences are not accepted as open source under the established definition, because the definition forbids discrimination against fields of endeavour [web: opensource.org], and the debate over them has not settled.

## Active research

Active work clusters around five questions. The first is how to scale licence scanning and provenance tracking, including for code produced with machine-learning assistance, where the training-data and attribution questions are unsettled in courts and in project policies. The second is reproducible and bootstrappable builds, with the goal that every artefact in a distribution can be rebuilt bit for bit from source and a small trusted seed. The third is supply-chain integrity: signing of packages and images, transparency logs and attestation formats that let a deployment verify where each piece came from. The fourth is sustainability, meaning how critical but under-funded components are maintained, an issue that incidents in widely used compression and cryptography libraries made vivid. The fifth is the legal treatment of network use, where researchers and lawyers compare the AGPL, the source-available licences and delayed-open-source models against the needs of hosted services.

## Future direction

Expect three trends to continue. First, distributions will keep shifting from mutable, package-by-package installs towards image-based and immutable systems, so that "the distribution" becomes a signed, versioned image that a machine boots, with packages used to build it. Second, regulation will make provenance a routine obligation: lists of components and their licences, and named responsibility for vulnerability handling, are moving from best practice towards legal requirement in several jurisdictions, with phased deadlines running through the second half of the 2020s. Third, the relicensing cycle will probably continue, and so will the forks that answer it, which makes it prudent for an administrator to prefer components that are governed by a neutral foundation or that have a viable community fork. The kernel's own position looks stable: GPL version 2, a maintainers' hierarchy and a very long track record. What to watch is not the kernel's terms but the layers above it, where businesses, regulators and communities are still negotiating who pays for the commons.
