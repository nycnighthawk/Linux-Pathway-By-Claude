# TLS in practice: certificates, the openssl tool and system crypto policy

## Define the problem

Almost every service on a network has to answer two questions for its clients: am I really talking to the right server, and can anyone else read or change what we exchange? Public-key cryptography answers both, but only if keys are generated safely, bound to names by a trusted signer, validated correctly by the client, and renewed before they expire. Failure looks like a certificate that expired on a Sunday and took down an internal API, a client that accepts any certificate because verification was switched off "for testing", a private key committed to a repository, a chain missing its intermediate so some clients fail and others do not, or a legacy protocol that a compliance scan flags on every endpoint.

This slice covers what an administrator needs to run TLS on Linux: the X.509 certificate model, creating keys, certificate signing requests and certificates with `openssl`, building a small private certificate authority, inspecting servers with `openssl s_client`, trust stores and system-wide crypto policy, and the common failure patterns. Application-level concerns (HTTP servers, reverse proxies, mTLS in service meshes) are in later courses; SSH, which has its own certificate format, is LNX-204-T03.

## History and approaches

SSL was designed at Netscape in the mid 1990s; SSL 2.0 and 3.0 were broken by design flaws, and the IETF's TLS 1.0 (1999), 1.1 and 1.2 (2008) followed, the last with a long life. The history is a sequence of attacks and rushed mitigations: BEAST and CRIME against CBC and compression, Lucky 13 and POODLE against padding, FREAK, Logjam and DROWN against export-grade and weak parameters, and Heartbleed (2014), an implementation bug in OpenSSL's heartbeat extension that exposed memory including private keys. Heartbleed produced two lasting changes: funding and governance for OpenSSL, and forks such as LibreSSL and BoringSSL. TLS 1.3 (2018) removed the weak features wholesale, cut the handshake to one round trip, and made forward secrecy mandatory.

Trust distribution has its own history. Early certificates were issued by a small set of commercial authorities with weak validation; compromised authorities (DigiNotar, 2011) issued fraudulent certificates for real domains, which led to certificate transparency logs, CAA DNS records, shorter lifetimes and browser enforcement. The Let's Encrypt service (2015) and the ACME protocol (RFC 8555) made issuance free and automatic and pushed lifetimes down; the CA/Browser Forum has scheduled a step-down of maximum public certificate validity to 200 days from March 2026, 100 days in 2027 and 47 days in 2029 (to verify). Inside organisations, private CAs, from hand-made `openssl ca` setups to Smallstep, Vault and FreeIPA, issue short-lived certificates for services and for mutual TLS.

Administration practice moved from per-application configuration (each daemon with its own protocol and cipher list) to **system-wide crypto policy**: Red Hat family systems provide `update-crypto-policies` with profiles (DEFAULT, LEGACY, FUTURE, FIPS) that configure OpenSSL, GnuTLS, NSS, OpenSSH and Java together [src: rhel-10-docs ea1e7ce0e8d65665]; compliance content checks that the policy is not overridden [src: compliance-as-code 02c851226064086d].

## Current state

**The certificate model.** An X.509 certificate binds a public key to a subject, signed by an issuer. Important fields: subject and *Subject Alternative Names* (SAN, the only names modern clients check; the common name is ignored), validity window, key usage and extended key usage (serverAuth, clientAuth), `basicConstraints` (is this a CA, with what path length), issuer and authority key identifiers, and revocation pointers (CRL, OCSP). A chain runs from the *leaf* through *intermediates* to a *root* in the client's trust store. Servers should send leaf and intermediates, not the root. Validation includes the signature chain, validity dates, name match, purpose, and revocation policy where enforced.

**The openssl command.** Generate a key: `openssl genpkey -algorithm ed25519 -out key.pem`, or `openssl genpkey -algorithm RSA -pkeyopt rsa_keygen_bits:3072`, or an EC key with `-algorithm EC -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256`. Make a request with SAN: `openssl req -new -key key.pem -subj "/CN=app.example.org" -addext "subjectAltName=DNS:app.example.org,DNS:app" -out app.csr`. Self-signed in one step: `openssl req -x509 -newkey ... -days 30 -nodes -keyout key.pem -out cert.pem -addext "subjectAltName=..."`. A small CA: create a CA key and a self-signed CA certificate with `-addext basicConstraints=critical,CA:TRUE`, then sign requests with `openssl x509 -req -in app.csr -CA ca.pem -CAkey ca.key -CAcreateserial -days 90 -copy_extensions copy -out app.pem` (the `-copy_extensions` option exists in OpenSSL 3.x) or use the fuller `openssl ca` with a configuration file. A hands-on guide to building a CA and issuing certificates for a cluster exists in the Rocky Linux material [src: rocky-docs 483e702a6ad8554f], and Kubernetes documents generating certificates manually with openssl [src: kubernetes-docs 9c83792267be5680]. Red Hat's networking guide covers creating and managing keys and certificates with certmonger, `openssl` and the `certutil`/NSS tools [src: rhel-10-docs 505b5ff564a7bffc].

**Inspecting.** `openssl x509 -in cert.pem -noout -text` (or `-subject -issuer -dates -ext subjectAltName -fingerprint -sha256`), `openssl req -in app.csr -noout -text -verify`, `openssl verify -CAfile ca.pem -untrusted inter.pem leaf.pem`, `openssl s_client -connect host:443 -servername host -showcerts` (add `-verify_return_error`, `-tls1_3`, `-brief`), and `openssl s_client -connect host:25 -starttls smtp`. To check that a certificate and a key match, compare the public key digests: `openssl x509 -in cert.pem -noout -pubkey | sha256sum` against `openssl pkey -in key.pem -pubout | sha256sum`. `openssl pkcs12 -export` bundles key and chain for Java and Windows, `openssl dgst -sha256`, `openssl enc`, `openssl rand -hex 32` and `openssl speed` cover digests, symmetric encryption, random bytes and benchmarks (prefer purpose-built tools like `age` and GnuPG for file encryption). `curl --cacert ca.pem https://...` and `curl -v` show handshake detail, and curl's own manual lists its TLS options [src: man-pages d01ec58372d22236].

**Trust stores and policy.** System trust anchors live in `/etc/pki/ca-trust/source/anchors/` (Red Hat family; add with `update-ca-trust`) or `/usr/local/share/ca-certificates/` (Debian family; add with `update-ca-certificates`). The result is a consolidated bundle (`/etc/pki/tls/certs/ca-bundle.crt`, `/etc/ssl/certs/ca-certificates.crt`). Java and some language runtimes keep separate stores. Applications can pin to a private CA with an explicit `cafile` rather than trusting the whole system store, which Python's `ssl` module supports through `create_default_context(cafile=...)`, while setting `check_hostname` and `verify_mode` correctly [src: python-docs f73f208c4a2aca23]. Docker, Kubernetes and Cilium documents show the same pattern: a daemon protected by a private CA and client certificates [src: docker-docs 61cf9b8b94f6707d] [src: cilium-docs b682ce0e80e1b4e8].

**Protocols and algorithms.** Allow TLS 1.2 and 1.3; disable TLS 1.0 and 1.1 and SSL; prefer AEAD suites (AES-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305) with ECDHE for forward secrecy; use ECDSA P-256 or RSA of at least 2048 (3072 for long life) and SHA-256 or stronger. OpenSSL 3.x organises code into *providers* (default, legacy, FIPS) and OpenSSL 3.5, the long-term-support release supported until April 2030, adds post-quantum algorithms (ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA) and a hybrid key-exchange group enabled by default (to verify) [web: openssl-library.org]. The DISA content requires that the system's crypto policy be set and that OpenSSL use TLS [src: compliance-as-code d7636b71cee9deaa] [src: disa-stig e7ee5c18cdeea454].

**Operations.** Automate issuance and renewal (ACME clients such as certbot, `lego`, `acme.sh`; or `certmonger`/`getcert` with IPA or SCEP); monitor expiry with a probe (`openssl x509 -checkend 1209600` exits non-zero if it expires within two weeks); reload services after renewal (`systemctl reload`) because many daemons read certificates only at start; store private keys with mode 0600 owned by the service account and consider hardware or KMS-backed keys.

The generalised pattern is **a signed binding between a name and a key, validated through a chain to a locally trusted anchor**, with lifetimes short enough to make revocation mostly moot.

**Two cipher lists in one tool.** OpenSSL configures TLS 1.2 and earlier cipher suites separately from TLS 1.3 suites. In the command line and in many configuration files, the first group is set with a `-cipher` string (or `cipher` option) written in OpenSSL's own mini language, and the second with `-ciphersuites`, which takes a colon-separated list of the five defined suite names. Administrators who restrict the legacy list and assume they have restricted everything leave TLS 1.3 at its defaults, which is acceptable, but a policy that must exclude a particular suite has to name it in both places. The practical test is to try a connection that should fail: `openssl s_client -connect host:443 -servername host -tls1_2 -cipher 'AES128-SHA'` ought to end in a handshake failure, and `-tls1_1` should be refused outright.

**One policy for the host, and its limits.** On Red Hat family systems `update-crypto-policies --show` prints the active profile and `update-crypto-policies --set FUTURE` switches it; a sub-policy can be layered on a base, written `DEFAULT:NO-SHA1`, so a single algorithm family is removed without a custom file [src: rhel-10-docs ea1e7ce0e8d65665]. The policy reaches OpenSSL, GnuTLS, NSS, libssh and OpenSSH through generated configuration files, but programs that bundle their own TLS stack (many Go and Rust binaries, Node.js builds, some vendor appliances) ignore it, so a scan from outside, not a trust in the setting, is the verification. Compliance content checks both that the policy is not set to `LEGACY` and that applications are not overriding it [src: compliance-as-code 02c851226064086d]. A policy change is a fleet-wide behavioural change: before moving to a stricter profile, collect the protocol and cipher actually negotiated by clients (server logs can record `$ssl_protocol` and `$ssl_cipher` in nginx) so that the set of broken clients is known in advance.

**Hardening a TLS endpoint.** For web servers the useful defaults are published as generated configurations in three tiers (modern, intermediate, old), maintained by the Mozilla security team; the intermediate tier is the common production choice, allowing TLS 1.2 and 1.3 [web: mozilla.org]. The usual extras are HTTP Strict Transport Security with a long `max-age` once HTTPS works everywhere, session tickets limited or rotated (a long-lived ticket key undermines forward secrecy for the sessions it protects), OCSP stapling where the CA still provides OCSP responses, and ALPN for HTTP/2. Check the result from outside with a scanner such as `testssl.sh` or `sslyze`, which report protocol versions, suites, chain problems, and known weaknesses in one run; schedule the scan, since drift appears when someone installs a package that rewrites a configuration fragment.

**Private CAs, correctly.** A private CA that only needs to vouch for `*.corp.example` should say so in the certificate: the `nameConstraints` extension on the CA (or the intermediate) lists permitted and excluded DNS names and IP ranges, and a client that supports it then rejects a certificate for any other domain even if the signature is valid. Add `basicConstraints=critical,CA:TRUE,pathlen:0` to an issuing intermediate so it cannot create further CAs, keep the root key offline, and issue from an intermediate whose key lives in an HSM, TPM or cloud KMS. With OpenSSL the extensions are given with `-addext` on `req` or `x509`, or through a section of `openssl.cnf` named by `-extfile` and `-extensions`; the `-copy_extensions copy` option on `x509 -req` carries requested SAN entries across from the CSR, which is convenient and also the reason to inspect a CSR before signing it. Name constraints are honoured by modern clients but not every embedded library, so test the clients that matter.

**Mutual TLS.** Client authentication uses the same machinery in reverse: the server asks for a certificate (`openssl s_server -Verify 1`, nginx `ssl_verify_client on`, Apache `SSLVerifyClient require`) and trusts it only if it chains to a CA configured for clients, which should be a different CA from the one that issues server certificates. The client certificate needs the `clientAuth` extended key usage; a certificate issued only for `serverAuth` is refused by strict stacks. Identity then comes from the subject or a SAN, and the application must map it to an account explicitly. Short lifetimes again matter more than revocation: a service-to-service certificate valid for a day, renewed automatically, limits the damage of theft with no CRL to distribute.

**Revocation in 2025 and after.** Revocation tooling is changing. Let's Encrypt announced that it would stop its OCSP service and rely on certificate revocation lists, and the browser ecosystem is moving the same way, with certificate lifetimes shortening on the published schedule (200 days from 15 March 2026, 100 days from 15 March 2027, and 47 days from 15 March 2029, with domain-validation reuse falling to ten days at the last step) [web: digicert.com]. Operationally, this makes automation mandatory rather than advisable: a manually renewed certificate that lasts 47 days means about eight renewals a year per name. Monitoring should alert on time remaining (the `openssl x509 -checkend` probe or a Prometheus exporter) well before the renewal job's retry window ends, and on failure of the renewal job itself, which is the earlier signal.

## Assessment: the good, the bad and the ugly

The good: TLS 1.3 with sane defaults is fast and hard to misconfigure; automated issuance has turned expiry outages from routine into avoidable; transparency logs and CAA reduce the risk of rogue issuance; `openssl` is available everywhere and scriptable; system crypto policy gives one dial for a whole host.

The bad: the `openssl` command line is notoriously inconsistent between versions (1.1.1 versus 3.x differ in defaults, option names and legacy algorithms such as RC4 and MD4 now living in a provider), and its configuration file syntax is arcane. Name checking is subtle (SAN, wildcards, IP addresses), revocation is weak in practice (CRLs are large, OCSP stapling is optional, soft-fail is common), and private-CA trust is all-or-nothing in most clients, so adding an internal root to the system store lets that CA vouch for any domain unless name constraints are set.

The ugly: key handling. Private keys end up in container images, backups and chat messages. Verification disabled in client code (`-k`, `verify=False`, `InsecureSkipVerify`) is the single most common TLS bug in practice and survives code review because "it is only for dev". A missing intermediate works in browsers that fetch it and fails in `curl`. Clock skew makes valid certificates "not yet valid". Wildcard certificates distributed to many servers widen the blast radius of one theft. The FIPS provider and crypto-policy interactions can reject algorithms silently so a connection fails with a vague "handshake failure". And lifetimes are shrinking faster than some organisations' manual processes.

## Applications beyond this domain

The same certificate chains protect HTTPS, mail transport, LDAP, databases, Kubernetes control planes (every component has client and server certificates), service meshes (SPIFFE/SPIRE identities with mTLS), VPNs (OpenVPN, IKEv2), code signing, and secure boot (X.509 in firmware databases). Libvirt's TLS transport and Ansible's WinRM certificate authentication use the same trust model [src: ansible-docs 2eb469c07eeb408c]. The principle of short-lived credentials issued by a signing authority is identical to SSH certificates (LNX-204-T03), cloud workload identity tokens and package-repository signing (LNX-104).

## Examples

Build a private CA and issue a server certificate in a lab: create `ca.key` (ECDSA P-256) and `ca.pem` valid ten years with `CA:TRUE, pathlen:0`; create `app.key` and a CSR with `subjectAltName`; sign with `openssl x509 -req ... -days 90 -copy_extensions copy`; check with `openssl verify -CAfile ca.pem app.pem` and `openssl x509 -noout -ext subjectAltName -in app.pem`; run a test server with `openssl s_server -cert app.pem -key app.key -www` and connect with `openssl s_client -connect localhost:4433 -CAfile ca.pem -verify_hostname app.example.org`.

Diagnosis example: a client reports a verification failure because the local issuer certificate cannot be found. `openssl s_client -showcerts` shows only the leaf; the server forgot to send the intermediate. Concatenate leaf then intermediate into the server's chain file.

Failing example: a self-signed certificate is generated with a `CN` only and no SAN; curl and browsers reject it with a name mismatch even though the CN matches. Another: a renewal job obtains a new certificate nightly, but the web server is never reloaded, so the old certificate expires in production even though a valid file sits on disk. Checking the served certificate with `s_client` (not the file) catches this.

Private CA example with constraints: create the issuing CA with `-addext "nameConstraints=critical,permitted;DNS:.corp.example,permitted;DNS:corp.example"`; issue `app.corp.example` and also, as a test, `www.example.com`. A verifier that honours constraints accepts the first and fails the second with a permitted-subtree violation, which `openssl verify` reports. The test demonstrates why an internal root that is added to every laptop does not have to be a master key for the web.

Debugging example: a service works from a workstation and fails from a container with `unable to get local issuer certificate`. `openssl s_client -connect host:443 -servername host -showcerts` shows only the leaf; the server forgot to send the intermediate, and the workstation worked only because its browser cached or fetched it. Fix by serving the full chain file (leaf followed by intermediates, never the root).

## Related problems and ideas

SSH certificates (LNX-204-T03) are a simpler cousin; PAM may use certificate-based smartcard logins (LNX-204-T01); sudo and audit records reference authenticated identities (LNX-204-T02, LNX-204-T07); an SELinux boolean or file context often explains why a service cannot read `/etc/pki` or a key file (LNX-204-T04); firewall rules decide which ports expose TLS (LNX-204-T06); AIDE should cover certificate and key directories (LNX-204-T08); compliance profiles check crypto policy and protocol versions (LNX-204-T09). Alternatives to the public-key infrastructure model: DANE (certificates in DNSSEC), the web of trust (OpenPGP), pre-shared keys, WireGuard's static keys, and SSH-style trust on first use. Not yet mainstream: post-quantum hybrid certificates, Encrypted Client Hello, and certificates with embedded transparency proofs everywhere.

## Active research

Post-quantum migration (hybrid key exchange now deployed widely; signature algorithms and certificate size are open questions), Encrypted Client Hello to hide server names, shorter certificate lifetimes with automation by default, formal verification of TLS libraries, memory-safe implementations (rustls), Merkle tree certificates to shrink handshakes, and better revocation (CRLite-style compressed lists). Open questions include how to rotate trust anchors without breaking old devices and how to keep embedded systems updatable.

## Future direction

Expect public certificate lifetimes to shrink in steps through 2029 (to verify), making automation mandatory rather than optional. Post-quantum hybrids will move from experimental to default, and OpenSSL 3.5 is the first LTS line that carries them. Plan for crypto agility: no hard-coded algorithms, inventory of where keys and certificates live, and renewal tests in CI.
