## The idea

A program that needs to authenticate someone names a **service**. The PAM library reads `/etc/pam.d/<service>` and walks
the lines of four groups: `auth` (who are you), `account` (may you log in now), `password` (change the token) and
`session` (set up and tear down). Each line has a control that decides how its result counts. `required` records a
failure but keeps going, `requisite` stops at once, `sufficient` ends the group on success if nothing earlier failed, and
`optional` rarely matters. Bracketed forms such as `[success=1 default=ignore]` skip lines and are how shipped stacks branch.

On Red Hat family systems the service files include `system-auth` (consoles, sudo) and `password-auth` (SSH and other
remote services), both generated by `authselect` from a profile. Debian family systems use `common-auth` and friends,
generated by `pam-auth-update`. Generated means that your hand edits are refused or overwritten: change policy through
the tool, or through a custom profile.

## Password quality

`pam_pwquality` runs in the `password` group before `pam_unix`. Its policy lives in `/etc/security/pwquality.conf`
(and `pwquality.conf.d/`): `minlen`, `minclass`, `retry`, `enforce_for_root`, `local_users_only`, and the credit options
`dcredit`, `ucredit`, `lcredit`, `ocredit`. A **negative** credit means "at least this many characters of the class";
a positive one is credit toward length. `minclass = 3` is usually simpler. Current NIST guidance favours length and
screening against breached passwords over composition rules, so weigh `minlen` more than `minclass`.

## Lockout with pam_faillock

Three lines are needed in each of the two stack files:

```
auth     required   pam_faillock.so preauth      # before pam_unix
auth     sufficient pam_unix.so ...
auth     required   pam_faillock.so authfail     # after pam_unix
account  required   pam_faillock.so
```

`preauth` refuses a locked account before the password is tested. `authfail` records the failure. The `account` line
resets the counter on success. Policy goes in `/etc/security/faillock.conf`: `deny`, `fail_interval`, `unlock_time`,
`even_deny_root`, `audit`. Records live under `/var/run/faillock` (tmpfs, cleared at boot). `faillock --user alice`
lists them and `faillock --user alice --reset` clears them. On Red Hat family hosts `authselect enable-feature with-faillock`
adds the lines to both files at once.

## A worked example

Goal: four failures in 15 minutes lock a user for 10 minutes; passwords need 14 characters and 3 classes.

1. `authselect enable-feature with-faillock` (or add the lines by hand to both files).
2. `/etc/security/faillock.conf`: `deny = 4`, `fail_interval = 900`, `unlock_time = 600`, `audit`.
3. `/etc/security/pwquality.conf`: `minlen = 14`, `minclass = 3`, `retry = 3`.
4. Keep one root session open. From another terminal fail four logins as a test user; the fifth should be refused even
   with the right password. `faillock --user testuser` shows the records; `--reset` clears them.

What goes wrong: the preauth line placed after `pam_unix` (locked accounts still get their password tested); lockout only
in `system-auth` (SSH guessing continues through `password-auth`); `even_deny_root` turning a guessing attack into a denial of service.

## Connections

- Lockout is per host and per user; SSH `MaxAuthTries` and nftables rate limits ([lesson](lesson:LNX-204-T03), [lesson](lesson:LNX-204-T06)) throttle by source.
- Removing passwords from the remote path with SSH certificates ([lesson](lesson:LNX-204-T03)) is the stronger fix.
- sudo authenticates through its own PAM service ([lesson](lesson:LNX-204-T02)).
- Failures and lockouts become audit records ([lesson](lesson:LNX-204-T07)); a scanner checks the stack ([lesson](lesson:LNX-204-T09)).
