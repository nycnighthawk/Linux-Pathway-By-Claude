# Multi-week fleet rollouts

This lesson follows the topic synthesis `synth/LNX-304/LNX-304-T10.md`, which carries the sources, the history and the failure cases.

## What you will be able to do

- Write a one-page charter and an inventory-based scope for a fleet campaign.
- Design waves, entry gates and stop conditions before starting.
- Prepare a rollback that is valid for the whole soak, and mark the point of no return.
- Run the long tail with an exceptions register, and close with a review.

## Eight questions

What is changing and why now? Which hosts are in scope? In what waves? What gates and stops? Who is on call and who approves? What is the rollback and how long is it possible? How is progress measured? What happens to hosts that cannot move?

## Waves

Wave 0 is the project's own hosts and a lab. Wave 1 holds a few representative production hosts, one of each class. After that, grow geometrically (about 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 percent, then the rest) but stop doubling where the people and systems that recover would be overwhelmed. Respect redundancy, quorum, dependencies and calendars.

## Gates and stops

Entry gate: the previous wave is healthy, the soak has passed, the checklist is done. Stop conditions are written down in advance: for example more than one failed host in a wave, a service-level breach caused by the change, a data-loss event, a failed rollback. Define pause (hold and investigate) and abort (go back), and name who may call each.

## Rollback and no-return

Storage or VM snapshots, restore from backup or reinstall: test on wave 0 and check that the window outlasts the soak. A step that cannot be undone, such as retiring an old key, needs its own approval and a recorded time.

## Communicate and measure

Notice to owners early, reminders, start and end messages, one status page, one incident channel. Report the share at target state, the remaining risk-weighted exposure, the oldest unconverted host, failures and rollbacks per wave, and exceptions with owners and expiry dates.

## The long tail

Run the last 5 to 10 percent through an exceptions register: host, reason, owner, compensating controls, review date, and a decision with a deadline (upgrade, replace, isolate, retire).

## Worked example: the schedule that could not stop

A fixed end date and no stop conditions let a wave continue after a second host failed to boot. Three more failed over the weekend. Afterwards: written stops, a driver check in the entry gate, and a sponsor who accepted a two-week slip.

## Self-check

1. Why must stop conditions be written before the first wave?
2. What limits wave size besides risk?
3. What is a point of no return and who approves crossing it?
4. Why report risk-weighted exposure and not only percent complete?
5. What belongs in an exceptions register?
